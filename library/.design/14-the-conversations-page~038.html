Claude
Etymology first, because it's better than expected.
The suffix is two suffixes: Latin -ivus (adjective: "disposed to, having the nature of") stacked on -itas (noun: "the state of"). Crucially -ivus attaches to the participial stem, so the base is always a completed act. And your three examples share a root: iacere, to throw.
object = thrown against
subject = thrown under
project = thrown forward
So the arc of -ivity is: an act → a standing disposition → a thing you can refer to. Something thrown once becomes a permanent tendency to throw that way, and then becomes an entity in its own right.
Why "productivity" broke the pattern: it isn't from project at all. It's producere/productus — a different verb. The real paradigm is intact and complete:
objection / objective / objectivity
subjection / subjective / subjectivity
projection / projective / projectivity
Projectivity is a live word — projective geometry. The reason it felt wrong is that -ion (the act) has crowded out -ivity (the disposition) in ordinary speech for project. For the other two, English kept both slots.
Now, ivitivity. Parse it: ivity → stem ivit- → ivitive → ivitivity. It's the property of being disposed to do what -ivity does.
And here's the payoff of the recursion: -ivity takes anything and returns an abstract property. Apply it to something that's already an abstract property and the type doesn't change. The operation is idempotent. Ivitivity is where the suffix hits its fixed point — the thingness of the tendency to make things out of tendencies.
Which is also a decent one-word name for the entire nominalizing habit of philosophy.

Claude
You're right that the throwing story can't do the work — the prefixes differ but the suffix doesn't care, so if that were the operative sense all three would take -ivity equally.
The actual source is the scholastic pair, which is grammatical/logical. Subiectum rendered Aristotle's hypokeimenon: what underlies, what a predication is about. Obiectum was what a mental act is directed at. Both name positions in a relation, not kinds of thing. (Medieval usage was close to inverted from ours — "objective being" was the mode of existence something has in the mind, which is why Descartes' "objective reality of an idea" means its representational content. The flip to our sense comes with the subject becoming the knower rather than the thing known about.)
That gives the suffix's job: -ivity abstracts the standing occupancy of a relational role. -ive says "characteristically functions as X"; -ity turns that into a property. So objectivity is the character of being able to stand in the object-position — holding still regardless of who is doing the referring. Subjectivity is the character of occupying the position from which the predicating is done. They pair because they're the two poles of one relation, which is exactly why the debate feels grammatical to you.
The test holds across the class: activity, receptivity, sensitivity, selectivity — all roles. And most tellingly transitivity and reflexivity, which are literally properties of relations. Project fails because it names a substance, not a pole: nothing stands in the project-position relative to anything else. An undertaking is an artifact, and artifacts don't take -ivity.
And the exception confirms it — projectivity is perfectly good in projective geometry, because there a projection is a relation with poles. The moment "project" acquires a role sense, the suffix comes back online.

The research answer changes the picture: objectivity and subjectivity aren't English derivations at all.
They're both calques of German, and they entered English in the same document. The first use of "objectivity" as a noun in English appears in an unsigned review of Charles Villers' Philosophie de Kant in the Edinburgh Review of January 1803, written by Thomas Brown — and the OED dates its earliest evidence for both words to that same 1803 Edinburgh Review. Etymonline gives subjectivity as 1803, "absence of objective reality," popularized in Kantian terminology, comparing French subjectivité and German Subjektivität.
So the suffix isn't doing English work here. The pair arrived whole, as an imported technical correlative. Nobody in English ever took "subject" and productively suffixed it. That's why there's no slot for project — there was no Kantian Projektivität to import.
Your persistence point is exactly right, and Greek confirms it.
The Springer reference work traces the pair to Greek hypokeimenonand antikeimenon, and to the medieval verbs subiicio and obiicio — which mean the opposite of their post-Cartesian usages. Both Greek terms are -keimenonwords: lying. Lying-under, lying-against. Postures of rest. Not throwings at all.
The Greek for "thrown forward" is problēma — that's where problem comes from. It's a separate word precisely because it names something transient: a thing in flight, an obstacle put in your path, something to be dealt with and disposed of. And problem takes no -ivity either. Two words for standing positions form a stable correlative pair; the throwing word doesn't join them, in Greek or in English.
Also: you're right about productivity, and it's not a secret. It's from producere/productus, a different verb entirely. It never had anything to do with project. What it does share is the shape: -ive names a disposition, -ivity names how much of it there is. Look at the class — conductivity, resistivity, permittivity, emissivity, reflectivity, selectivity, sensitivity, connectivity, productivity. Nearly all are magnitudes. Quantities of a capacity.
Which is why projectivity survives only in geometry, where it names the product of any number of perspectivities — a property a mapping definitively has or lacks. The ordinary noun "project" is a count noun for an artifact, and artifacts have no magnitude of disposition to measure.
The medieval inversion is worth having in hand, because it's the reason your grammatical intuition tracks. In Latin usage the two were a correlative pair, not opposites: the obiectum was what stood against the powers of the subiectum, and it was by the subject's faculties that the thing was an object at all — everything existing is a subject, and only subjects with cognitive powers can have objects. Objectivity in the old sense was the mode of being something has in the mind. Aquinas is a documented hinge: he advanced the scholastic account by integrating obiectum and emphasizing the intellect, producing a dual account where both subject and formal object give a science its unity.
Then Kant's translators flipped which pole carried "real," and the two nouns were minted in English together to name the flip.

Claude
You're right and I overreached. Coining a word in English to argue with Kant is English making a word — that's the normal mechanism, not evidence of foreignness. Permittivity was coined too. I let the loan-history distract from the question you actually asked. Here's the definition, plainly.
-ivity = -ive + -ity, and each half does one thing.
-ive takes a verbal base and yields "having the standing tendency to V." Not doing it now — being the kind of thing that does it. Dispositional, not episodic.
-ity takes that adjective and yields the property as a nameable quantity — something a thing can have more or less of.
So: -ivity names the amount of a standing tendency.
That predicts the whole class. Conductivity, resistivity, permittivity, emissivity, sensitivity, selectivity, productivity — every one is a measurable degree of a persisting disposition. Not an act, not an occasion.
Three conditions, and project fails all three.
The base must name (1) a tendency, (2) that persists, (3) that admits of degree.
To be a subject is a condition you remain in. To be an object is a condition you remain in. These hold while you're not doing anything. That's exactly what you said — object and subject persist. Objectivity is then how much a thing holds still under any observer's regard; subjectivity is how much of what appears is owed to the pole doing the regarding. Both are magnitudes of a standing posture.
A project isn't a posture. It's a count noun for an undertaking — a thing you have, with a start and an end. When it's over, nothing about you retains a "projective" tendency. There's no persisting capacity for the suffix to quantify.
And the technical exception fits the rule rather than breaking it: in geometry, projective recovers a genuine standing property of a mapping — a mapping either preserves collinearity or it doesn't, permanently, as a fact about the mapping and not about any one throw. The moment "project" names something that persists, the suffix comes back. That's the confirmation, not a dodge.

You're right, and my version was wrong in a specific way: I flattened one layer. "Disposed to do what -ivity does" describes the suffix from outside instead of applying it. Applying it twice gives exactly your reading —
the property of being disposed to having the property of being disposed to doing something.
Two nested applications, because self-application requires two.
Which also means I have to withdraw the fixed-point claim from my first message. It isn't idempotent. The second layer is real, not a collapse, because second-order dispositions genuinely differ from first-order ones. Trainability is a disposition to acquiredispositions. So is plasticity, malleability, learnability. A thing can be highly ivitive while having almost no particular tendencies yet.
And note what your parse exposes at the bottom: "something." The innermost slot stays open. Conductivity is saturated — you know what's being conducted. Ivitivity can't be, because the suffix supplies structure and never content. So it's not a word with a meaning so much as a word with an arity — one free variable, waiting.
Read as a magnitude, which is what -ivity does: ivitivity is how much capacity a thing has for acquiring capacities, before any of them are specified. Raw disposability. Which is a decent name for whatever it is that makes a young animal different from a rock.
