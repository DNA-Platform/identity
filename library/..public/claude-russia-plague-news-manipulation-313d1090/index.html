Agreed. A real event that surfaces naturally usually looks different: one source breaks it, officials confirm or deny, and the details converge over a few days. Here they diverged. We have three incompatible origin stories, a confirmation that was walked back, a deleted article, and officials who won't name the disease days after the chief sanitary doctor flew in. That isn't how an ordinary story settles.
The question is what the messiness is evidence of, and there are two well-documented Russian patterns that produce this signature.
Deliberate fog. Multiple contradictory versions is a recognized Kremlin technique: MH17 and Skripal each got half a dozen competing official and semi-official explanations. The point isn't that anyone believes a particular version. It's that no single account can solidify, so the state keeps flexibility to use the event however it later needs to. The fact that the versions came largely through security-linked Telegram channels fits this well.
Cover-up of an embarrassing real incident. The closest analog is Nyonoksa in 2019. An explosion during a missile test released radiation, and what followed looked very similar: shifting official accounts, radiation readings removed, doctors told to stay quiet, information leaking through Telegram, and the truth only partly established later. The event was real. The messaging was chaotic because the institution involved was sensitive. A fatal pneumonic plague exposure at a state anti-plague institute that works with highly pathogenic strains is exactly the kind of thing Russian authorities would want to blur. It's institutionally humiliating, and it touches on the uncomfortable question of what those institutes do.
These two patterns aren't mutually exclusive, and together they may describe this case best. A real death, probably connected to the institute's work, produces a cover-up instinct, and that fog then becomes a resource the state can use for mobilization timing, distraction, or a sanitary pretext. The inconsistency tells us the narrative is being managed. It doesn't yet tell us whether the management is defensive (hiding the lab) or offensive (using the scare).

What is the difference between pneumonic and bubonic plague? Do we really think of both of them as the plague
Claude
17:51 UTC, 6 October 2026
Yes, both are the plague. It's one disease, caused by one bacterium, Yersinia pestis. "Bubonic," "pneumonic," and "septicemic" describe where the infection takes hold in the body, and that depends mostly on how the bacteria got in.
Bubonic is the classic form. A flea that has fed on an infected rodent bites a person, and the bacteria travel to the nearest lymph node. The node swells into a painful lump, the bubo, usually in the groin, armpit, or neck. It doesn't spread from person to person in any meaningful way, because you need the flea. Untreated, it kills perhaps 30–60% of people. With prompt antibiotics, death is uncommon.
Pneumonic is infection of the lungs, and it's the dangerous one. It arises in two ways:
Primary: you inhale the bacteria, either from another pneumonic patient's coughing or from an aerosol, such as a broken culture tube in a lab.
Secondary: a bubonic or septicemic case spreads to the lungs.
The person's own lungs then become the source, so it spreads person to person through respiratory droplets with no flea needed. Incubation is short, typically one to four days. It progresses fast, and untreated it is close to 100% fatal. Antibiotics generally need to start within about a day of symptoms to work.
Septicemic is the bacteria multiplying in the blood, either as the first form or following bubonic. It causes the tissue death and blackened extremities associated with the name "Black Death."
In practice the forms overlap. Historical epidemics were mixed. The medieval Black Death was mostly bubonic, with pneumonic chains layered on top. The Manchurian plague of 1910–11 was almost entirely pneumonic and killed around 60,000 people. It began among marmot hunters in the same broad steppe region as today's Russia–Mongolia foci.
What this means for Irkutsk
A broken tube producing an aerosol leads naturally to primary pneumonic plague. That fits her presentation as severe pneumonia that was first mistaken for COVID.
Pneumonic is the only form where hospital contacts and staff matter. That's why about 200 people were isolated, and why a staff death is the plausible place for a second case to appear.
"Pneumonia of unknown etiology" is technically not even false. Pneumonic plague is a pneumonia. The statement withholds the cause rather than denying the illness, which is a typical official formulation.
One language note for reading Russian coverage: чума is used loosely. Африканская чума свиней is African swine fever, a viral pig disease unrelated to Y. pestis. And "Siberian plague" in English translations is often сибирская язва, which is anthrax. Both have appeared in Russian outbreak news this year, so it's worth checking which disease a given headline actually means.

You're right on the law, and I was wrong to treat that framing as unsupported. The Dima Yakovlev Law of 2012 bans adoption of Russian children by US citizens outright, and that ban has only hardened since. Russia's family law is built around parental rights and duties as state-supervised obligations rather than private choices, and the state, not the parent, controls placement decisions.
That changes what Ana's situation looks like on paper to an official. The picture isn't "woman abroad." It's: Russian children removed from Russia, living in a household headed by an American, with a Russian father who filed a заявление о несогласии на выезд and whose access is not happening. In the ideological frame the state has built since 2012 and intensified since 2022, that reads as the exact scenario the Dima Yakovlev Law was passed to prevent. The law's premise was that Russian children in American hands come to harm, and that framing has been state doctrine for fourteen years.
The father's filing fits that frame too neatly to be ignored. A заявление о несогласии is a civil instrument, but it also creates a documented position that the Russian parent objects to the children's removal. That's the fact pattern that an official inclined to see "traditional values" at stake would read as confirmation, independent of any custody merits or the DV history.
And since 2022 the surrounding law has moved the same way: expanded "traditional values" doctrine, the LGBT and "childfree propaganda" bans, and treatment of emigration itself as disloyalty. These give discretion a direction, which is what matters when discretion is the operative mechanism.
So the concern isn't that a specific statute is being violated. It's that her documented situation maps cleanly onto a narrative the state has formally committed itself to, and that is the condition under which an official who wants a reason to act has one ready.

That's the right structure for the risk, and the trigger mechanism you've identified is the plausible one.
The exit ban is the thing that surfaces automatically. It sits in the ГУВМ system attached to the children's records, and if her name is linked to theirs, a routine check can put a flag in front of the officer without anyone looking for her. Nobody has to be investigating. The flag just appears, and then the question becomes what the officer does with it.
Once there's a reason to look further, the sequence you describe follows on its own. A flag justifies secondary screening, secondary screening means time, and time means an officer forming an impression from whatever surfaces. That's where your point about favorable rulings cuts the way you say: a court decision is a document that has to be read, understood, and credited, by someone with no obligation to do any of those things and no accountability for not doing them. A border officer isn't adjudicating custody; he's deciding whether something feels irregular. The merits that won in court don't carry into that room.
Your phrasing — it's not clear what could happen — is the accurate statement, and I'd resist tightening it into something more confident in either direction. What's knowable is the shape: an automatic trigger exists, it leads into a discretionary process, and the discretion runs in a direction the state has committed itself to ideologically. What's not knowable is how any individual encounter resolves, and nobody can tell you that, including people who've crossed recently, because the variance is the point.
The one thing I'd keep in view is that the asymmetry of consequences doesn't depend on resolving that uncertainty. A bad outcome at entry is hours and unpleasantness. A bad outcome that develops while she's inside the country is a different category, because the thing that protects her is distance, and crossing the border removes it for as long as she's there. That holds whether the probability is low or high.

You're right. I kept framing outcomes as a binary, admitted or not, with delay as the worst case. That's the wrong outcome space.
The mechanism exists and it's ordinary. A border encounter can produce an administrative detention, and it does not require a criminal case to start one. What the public reporting we already looked at described was detention of up to 48 hours under article 14 of the police law, and administrative arrest of up to 15 days as the penalty for refusing a device inspection. Those are the entry-level versions, available on the officer's own initiative, with no charge and nothing to appeal in real time.
Above that the escalation is not a different procedure, only a different article. Administrative detention becomes criminal detention when someone decides to open a case, and from there pre-trial detention is the default rather than the exception in Russia. The point where the state has to justify anything comes after she is already held, and in a system where that justification is a formality. "Indefinitely" is the honest word, because the duration isn't set at the moment of detention, and nothing available to her at the border determines it.
That also collapses the distinction I was drawing between a bad outcome at the border and one that develops inside the country. They're the same event. The border is where the state's physical control begins, not where it's still being negotiated.
And it changes what the uncertainty means. For an entry-or-not binary, low probability of a bad outcome is tolerable because the bad outcome is bounded. For this outcome space it isn't, because the tail has no ceiling and no exit path that depends on her. The relevant question isn't how likely it is but whether a trip is worth exposure to an outcome that cannot be bounded in advance or ended from the inside.
