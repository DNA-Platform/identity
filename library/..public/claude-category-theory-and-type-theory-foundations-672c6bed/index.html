What type theory would a category theorist know?
The short answer is Martin-Löf Type Theory (MLTT), particularly its dependent type variant, and increasingly Homotopy Type Theory (HoTT). But the relationship is more nuanced than "category theorists use type theory as their foundation."
The key connection isn't just Curry-Howard — it's the extended Curry-Howard-Lambek correspondence, which is a three-way equivalence:
Logic: Propositions, proofs, implication
Type Theory: Types, terms/programs, function types
Category Theory: Objects, morphisms, exponential objects
So a category theorist doesn't encounter type theory as a foundation beneath their work. They encounter it as a parallel language — another way of saying the same things. Specifically, the internal language of a cartesian closed category is the simply typed lambda calculus. The internal language of a topos is a form of higher-order intuitionistic type theory. The internal language of an (∞,1)-topos is something like HoTT.
Would they find it foundational?
Here's where it gets interesting: mostly no, and for a reason that I think resonates with your instincts about SRT.
Category theorists tend to be structuralists — they care about patterns of relationship, not what the objects "are" underneath. The standard foundational options look like this to them:
ZFC set theory: Works but feels like it adds irrelevant specificity. (Is the number 2 really {{∅}}? Who cares?)
Type theory (MLTT/HoTT): Closer to how they think, but they view it as one particular categorical structure (a locally cartesian closed category, roughly), not as bedrock.
Category theory itself: Some (Lawvere, notably) tried to make this foundational via ETCS (Elementary Theory of the Category of Sets), but this is a minority position.
The pragmatic reality is that most category theorists work informally within whatever foundation is convenient, treating the foundational question as largely irrelevant to what they're doing. The joke is that category theory is "foundation-independent" — it describes structural patterns that hold regardless of what's underneath.
Can you express category theory within type theory?
This is where genuine technical difficulties arise. The answer is "sort of, but with friction":
You can define individual categories in MLTT, but the category of all sets (or the category of all categories) runs into universe-level issues, analogous to size problems in set theory.
HoTT handles this better for ∞-groupoids (which are types), but ordinary 1-categories with non-invertible morphisms require extra machinery (directed type theory, which is still being developed).

Good — let's build this up properly.
What is a category?
A category is almost disappointingly simple. It's three things:
A collection of objects (call them A, B, C...), a collection of morphisms (arrows) where each morphism f has a source object and a target object (written f: A → B), and acomposition operation that takes any two morphisms where the target of the first matches the source of the second and produces a third (if f: A → B and g: B → C, then g∘f: A → C).
Two rules must hold. Composition is associative: h∘(g∘f) = (h∘g)∘f. And every object has an identity morphism id_A: A → A that does nothing — f∘id_A = f and id_A∘g = g.
That's it. That's the whole definition.
Now here's why this is deceptively powerful. Notice what's not specified: what the objects are, what the morphisms are, what composition means concretely. A category is a pattern that shows up everywhere:
Set — objects are sets, morphisms are functions, composition is function composition. This is the one mathematicians treat as "default."
Grp — objects are groups, morphisms are group homomorphisms (functions that preserve group structure).
Top — objects are topological spaces, morphisms are continuous maps.
Poset — any partially ordered set is a category where there's at most one morphism from A to B (meaning A ≤ B). Composition is transitivity.
And here's one that should interest you: any formal theory can be seen as a category. Objects are formulas, morphisms are proofs (a proof is a morphism from its premises to its conclusion), and composition is chaining proofs together.
Why "category" deserves the title of the theory
The reason categories took over isn't the definition above — it's what happens when you start asking aboutrelationships between categories. This is where the power lives.
A functor is a map between categories. It sends objects to objects and morphisms to morphisms, preserving composition and identities. So a functor F: C → D is a "structure-preserving translation" from one entire mathematical world to another.
A natural transformation is a map between functors. If F and G are both functors from C to D, a natural transformation α: F ⇒ G is a systematic way of transforming F's outputs into G's outputs, one component for each object, such that everything commutes properly.
So you have three levels: objects and morphisms inside a category, functors between categories, and natural transformations between functors. Eilenberg and Mac Lane invented category theory in 1945 specifically to make natural transformations precise — they needed the entire apparatus just to say what "natural" meant in "natural isomorphism," which was a term algebraic topologists kept using informally.
The revelation was that this three-level structure — things, maps between things, maps between maps — is the skeleton of almost all mathematics. Once you see it, you can't unsee it. Every mathematical discipline turns out to be the study of some category, and the deep theorems are often really theorems about functors. The reason homology works in topology, for example, is that it's a functor from Top to Grp — it systematically translates topological problems into algebraic ones while preserving the relevant structure.
Category theory earned its title because it turned out to be the minimal vocabulary needed to describe what mathematicians are actually doing when they do mathematics across any field.
What is a topos?
A topos is where things get foundationally interesting. It's a specific kind of category that has enough internal structure to support logic.
A topos is a category with three properties. It has all finite limits (you can take products, equalizers, and pullbacks — essentially you can do all the basic constructions you'd want). It has exponential objects (for any two objects A and B, there's an object B^A that behaves like "the collection of all morphisms from A to B" — this is the categorical version of function spaces). And it has a subobject classifier — this is the crucial one.
The subobject classifier is an object Ω with a special morphism true: 1 → Ω such that every "subobject" (categorical version of a subset) corresponds uniquely to a morphism into Ω. In the category Set, Ω is just {true, false}, and a map X → {true, false} is a characteristic function picking out a subset. So the subobject classifier is the categorical abstraction of truth values.
Here's why this matters: once you have a subobject classifier, logic happens inside your category automatically. You get conjunction (by taking products of subobjects), disjunction, implication, and quantifiers, all defined purely in terms of categorical structure. The internal logic of a topos is always at least intuitionistic — you get everything except the law of excluded middle, which holds only in special toposes (like Set).
So a topos is a category that's rich enough to be a universe of discourse — a world in which you can do mathematics, with its own native logic. Different toposes have different logics. Sheaf toposes over topological spaces have logics that reflect the topology. The effective topos has a logic where "existence" means "computably constructible."
Why this should matter to you
The topos concept is essentially the category theorist's answer to the question "what is a foundation for mathematics?" — except they give infinitely many answers, one per topos, and say the interesting thing is the relationships between them.
Compare this to SRT: you have a single foundational system (first-order, single-sorted) that can express different semantic domains by specifying different catalogues and reference types. A topos gives you a different logic per context. SRT gives you a different semantics per context. The topos approach pluralizes the logic. SRT keeps the logic fixed and pluralizes the meaning.

First-order axiomatization of a single category
This is straightforward. You have two sorts (or two predicates if you want single-sorted): objects and morphisms. You have functions dom(f) and cod(f) giving you the source and target of each morphism. You have a partial composition operation comp(f,g) defined when cod(f) = dom(g). You have an identity function id(A) for each object. And then your axioms are:
dom(id(A)) = A and cod(id(A)) = A. The domain and codomain of a composition behave correctly: dom(comp(f,g)) = dom(f) and cod(comp(f,g)) = cod(g). Composition is associative: comp(f, comp(g,h)) = comp(comp(f,g), h). And identity is the unit: comp(id(A), f) = f and comp(f, id(B)) = f when appropriate.
That's clean and perfectly first-order. No problems. But it only describes one category. It's like axiomatizing one particular group. The interesting part of category theory isn't any single category — it's the relationships between them.
Lawvere's attempts to axiomatize the whole thing
William Lawvere, who took foundations more seriously than almost any other category theorist, made two attempts.
ETCS (Elementary Theory of the Category of Sets, 1964) axiomatizes the category Set directly, without reference to ZFC. The axioms say things like: there exists a terminal object (a set with one element), there exist products and equalizers, there's a natural number object, there's a subobject classifier, and a choice axiom. It's a first-order theory. It works. It's equivalent in strength to bounded Zermelo set theory with choice. You can do most of ordinary mathematics inside it.
But it doesn't capture category theory itself — it captures sets from a categorical perspective. You've replaced ZFC with a different axiomatization of roughly the same mathematical universe.
ETCC (Elementary Theory of the Category of Categories, 1966) was the more ambitious attempt — axiomatize the category of all categories as the foundational object. This would actually make category theory its own foundation. Lawvere proposed axioms for what a "category of categories" should satisfy.
This largely failed. The axioms turned out to be either too weak (they had unintended models that didn't behave like the category of categories should) or required supplementation that essentially smuggled set theory back in. Why the axiomatization problem is hard
The difficulty is structural and it connects directly to your observation. A group can be axiomatized because group elements are inert — they just sit there and get operated on. A category has objects that are themselves structured things (other categories, or sets, or spaces), and morphisms that are structure-preserving maps between structured things. When you try to axiomatize "the category of all categories," you're asking for a first-order theory whose domain contains entities that are themselves interpretations of that very theory.
This is a self-reference problem. And category theory has no native mechanism for handling it, because — as you noticed — it has no recursive generative principle. It can describe self-referential structures from outside but it can't be one.
Compare: SRT has R(x,y,t) where t is itself a referent in the domain, so the theory's own relationship types are objects in the theory. That's the self-containing move that category theory can't make.
So what axioms would a complete category theory need?
Honestly, nobody knows. The closest thing to a consensus is that you'd need something like:
The ETCS axioms for a base topos (giving you a universe to work in), plus axioms asserting the existence of enough "internal categories" within that topos, plus some axiom asserting that the whole structure is "complete" in some sense — that every categorical construction you'd want to perform is available. But specifying that last condition precisely, without either being too weak or collapsing into set theory, remains an open problem.
This is the state of play. Category theory is a mature, powerful mathematical discipline that still cannot fully axiomatize itself after 80 years. Not because nobody's tried, but because the structural features that make it powerful — the ability to move freely between levels of abstraction — are precisely what resist being captured in a fixed axiom system.

The Yoneda Lemma
I mentioned this before but let me say what it actually does. It says: if you know all the morphisms from every object X into an object A, you know A completely (up to isomorphism). More precisely, the functor Hom(-, A) determines A.
Why this matters concretely: in algebraic geometry, the objects you care about (schemes, varieties) are often horrendously complicated to describe internally. But their "functor of points" — what maps into them look like from every possible source — is often much more tractable. Grothendieck's entire revolution in algebraic geometry rested on this insight. Instead of asking "what is this geometric object?" you ask "what does this geometric object look like from every possible vantage point?" and Yoneda guarantees you lose nothing. He literally rebuilt the foundations of algebraic geometry around this idea, and it led to the proof of the Weil conjectures, which were among the deepest open problems in number theory at the time.
Adjoint Functors
An adjunction is a pair of functors going in opposite directions, F: C → D and G: D → C, that are "approximately inverse" in a precise sense. The slogan is that adjunctions capture the notion of "best approximation" — F(X) is the best approximation in D to the object X in C, and vice versa.
Why this matters: an enormous number of fundamental mathematical constructions turn out to be adjunctions in disguise. Free groups, tensor products, compactification of topological spaces, the relationship between forgetting structure and freely generating structure — these are all adjoint pairs. Before category theory, these were unrelated constructions in unrelated fields. Recognizing them as instances of the same pattern meant that theorems proved about one could be transferred to others. Daniel Kan's discovery of adjoint functors in the 1950s is probably the single result that elevated category theory from bookkeeping to genuine mathematics.
And there's a deep theorem here: a functor has a left adjoint if and only if it preserves all limits. This is the Adjoint Functor Theorem, and it gives you existence results for free. You want to know if a certain construction exists? Check that a certain functor preserves limits. That's often much easier than building the construction by hand.
Grothendieck's Tohoku Paper and Abelian Categories
In 1957 Grothendieck wrote a paper that essentially said: homological algebra (the study of chain complexes, exact sequences, derived functors — machinery that was developed for topology) works in any abelian category, not just categories of modules. He axiomatized what properties a category needs for homological algebra to function, and then proved the theorems once at that level.
The payoff was immediate and enormous. Sheaf cohomology, which was a mess of ad hoc constructions in algebraic geometry, suddenly became a clean, general theory. You could compute cohomology of anything that lived in an abelian category, and the proofs carried over automatically. This is what enabled the development of étale cohomology, which was the key technical tool in proving the Weil conjectures.
The Seifert–van Kampen Theorem via Pushouts
This is a more elementary example but it shows the pattern clearly. The classical theorem says: if you decompose a topological space into overlapping pieces, you can compute its fundamental group from the fundamental groups of the pieces and their overlap. The classical proof is fiddly and specific to fundamental groups.
The categorical perspective says: the fundamental group functor preserves pushouts (a categorical construction for gluing things together). Once you know that, the theorem is almost immediate, and it generalizes instantly to higher homotopy groups and other algebraic invariants. One insight replaces dozens of specific arguments.
Kan Extensions
Mac Lane said "all concepts are Kan extensions." That's an exaggeration but not by much. A Kan extension is a way of extending a functor along another functor — you have F: C → E and K: C → D, and you want to find the "best" functor D → E that's compatible with both. Limits, colimits, adjunctions, and many other constructions are all special cases of Kan extensions.
The practical significance is that Kan extensions give you a universal recipe for extending partial information to complete information. If you know how something behaves on a subcategory, Kan extensions tell you the canonical way to extend that behavior to the whole category. This shows up in homotopy theory, representation theory, and increasingly in applied settings like database theory and data migration.
The pattern across all of these
Notice what's happening in every case. Category theory isn't discovering new objects or proving things about specific structures. It's revealing that constructions mathematicians already had in different fields arethe same construction, and then proving theorems at the general level that apply everywhere simultaneously. The value is in the compression and transfer — you prove it once and deploy it everywhere.
It's a theory of analogy made precise. Which is genuinely powerful but, as you've noted, fundamentally different from what a foundation does. A foundation generates. Category theory translates.

Per Martin-Löf
Martin-Löf was trained in mathematical logic and statistics in Stockholm. His intellectual lineage runs through Brouwer's intuitionism — the early 20th century program that said mathematics is a mental construction and a proof of existence must exhibit the thing claimed to exist. Brouwer rejected the law of excluded middle (you can't prove something exists by showing its nonexistence leads to contradiction), rejected the axiom of choice, and insisted that mathematical objects are things you build, not things you discover in a Platonic heaven.
The problem with Brouwer's program was that it was philosophically compelling but formally underdeveloped. Brouwer was famously hostile to formalization — he thought formalism was the enemy of mathematical intuition. So intuitionism remained a philosophical stance rather than a working foundation. Heyting developed intuitionistic logic, which gave it formal bones, but there was no real system you could work in the way you could work in ZFC.
Martin-Löf set out to fix this. Starting in the early 1970s, he developed Intuitionistic Type Theory (also called Martin-Löf Type Theory, MLTT), which was intended to be a complete foundation for constructive mathematics. The key ideas:
Propositions are types. A proposition P is identified with the type of its proofs. "There exists an X such that P(X)" is the type of pairs (x, p) where x is a thing and p is evidence that P holds of x. If the type is inhabited (something lives in it), the proposition is true. If it's empty, the proposition is false. There is no truth without a witness.
Proofs are programs. Under the Curry-Howard correspondence, a proof of an implication P → Q is a function that takes a proof of P and returns a proof of Q. A proof of "for all x, P(x)" is a function that takes any x and returns a proof of P(x). Proofs aren't static logical objects — they're computational procedures. And because they're programs, they're finite, they're writable, they terminate.
Dependent types. This is Martin-Löf's big technical innovation. A type can depend on a value. So you don't just have "list" — you have "list of length n," where the type itself encodes the length. You don't just have "number" — you have "number greater than 5." The proposition "for all n, there exists a prime greater than n" becomes a function type: a function that takes any n and returns a pair (p, evidence) where p is a prime greater than n. The type is the theorem statement, and an inhabitant of the type is the proof, and that inhabitant is a computable function.
No axiom of choice needed. In type theory, the axiom of choice is actually provable — not because it's snuck in, but because "there exists" already means "I can produce one." If for every x you have a proof that some y exists, you already have a function mapping each x to its y. The constructive interpretation makes choice trivial rather than controversial.
What this means for your concern
Every proof in Martin-Löf type theory is a term — a finite, writable expression in a formal language. If you prove something exists, you've built it. There are no ghosts. The adjoint functor theorem, as stated in classical category theory, is not provable in MLTT because it asserts existence without construction. The judgment forms
Martin-Löf's system doesn't start with propositions or axioms in the way first-order logic does. It starts with four fundamental judgments — assertions you can make:
A type — "A is a well-formed type" a : A — "a is a term of type A" (equivalently: a is a proof of A, a inhabits A) A = B type — "A and B are the same type" a = b : A — "a and b are the same term of type A"
Everything in the system is a rule for deriving these judgments from other judgments. There are no free-floating propositions. Every statement has a typing context — a list of assumptions of the form x₁ : A₁, x₂ : A₂, ... that tells you what variables are in scope and what types they have.
The type formers and their rules
Each type former has four rules: formation (when can you form this type?), introduction (how do you build an inhabitant?), elimination (what can you do with an inhabitant?), and computation (what happens when introduction meets elimination?). This four-rule pattern is the backbone of the entire system.
Π-types (dependent function types)
Formation: If A is a type, and B(x) is a type for every x : A, then Π(x:A).B(x) is a type.
Introduction: If b(x) : B(x) for every x : A, then λ(x).b(x) : Π(x:A).B(x). You build a function by giving a recipe that works for every input.
Elimination: If f : Π(x:A).B(x) and a : A, then f(a) : B(a). You use a function by applying it.
Computation: (λ(x).b(x))(a) = b(a) : B(a). Applying a lambda to an argument just substitutes.
When B doesn't depend on x, this reduces to the ordinary function type A → B. The "for all" quantifier in logic is a Π-type — a proof of "for all x:A, P(x)" is a function that takes any x and returns a proof of P(x).
Σ-types (dependent pair types)
Formation: If A is a type, and B(x) is a type for every x : A, then Σ(x:A).B(x) is a type.
Introduction: If a : A and b : B(a), then (a, b) : Σ(x:A).B(x). You build a pair by providing a witness and evidence.
Elimination: If p : Σ(x:A).B(x), then π₁(p) : A and π₂(p) : B(π₁(p)). You can extract both components.
Computation: π₁(a,b) = a and π₂(a,b) = b.
This is the existential quantifier. A proof of "there exists x:A such that P(x)" is a pair: the actual x, together with the proof of P(x). No ghosts — existence is exhibition.
Identity types (Id-types)
Formation: If A is a type and a, b : A, then Id_A(a,b) is a type.
Introduction: For any a : A, refl(a) : Id_A(a,a). The only way to construct a proof of identity is reflexivity — a thing equals itself.
Elimination: This is the J rule, which is the most subtle part of the whole system. It says: if you have a proof p : Id_A(a,b), and you have a property C(x,y,q) defined for any x, y : A and q : Id_A(x,y), and you can prove C(x,x,refl(x)) for all x, then you can conclude C(a,b,p). In other words: to prove something about an arbitrary identity proof, it suffices to prove it for reflexivity.
Computation: When p is refl(a), the J rule reduces to the reflexivity case.
Identity types are where things get philosophically deep. The question "what inhabitants does Id_A(a,b) have beyond refl?" is the question that leads to HoTT. In ordinary MLTT, you typically assume there's at most one — this is called uniqueness of identity proofs (UIP). HoTT rejects this and says identity types can have rich structure.
Universe types
Formation: U₀ is a type. If A : U₀, then A is a type. U₁ is a type, and U₀ : U₁.
This is the hierarchy I mentioned. Types live in universes, and universes are themselves types in larger universes. U₀ contains "small" types. U₁ contains U₀ and all "large" types built from small ones. And so on.
This is where Martin-Löf was burned by Girard's paradox. The rule "Type : Type" (one universe containing itself) is inconsistent. So you need the hierarchy. Every time you want to quantify over types, you go up a level.
Natural number type (ℕ)
Formation: ℕ is a type.
Introduction: 0 : ℕ, and if n : ℕ then succ(n) : ℕ.
Elimination: This is the recursion/induction principle. If you have a base case c : C(0) and a step case f : Π(n:ℕ).C(n) → C(succ(n)), then you get rec(c,f) : Π(n:ℕ).C(n).
Computation: rec(c,f)(0) = c and rec(c,f)(succ(n)) = f(n, rec(c,f)(n)).
This is how you build things inductively. Note the parallel to your transduction — you start with 0, and succ is an operation that adds one more. Each natural number is finitely constructed. There's no completed infinity, just the rule for always being able to construct the next one.
Empty type (0) and Unit type (1)
0 has no introduction rule — nothing inhabits it. Its elimination rule says: from a proof of 0, derive anything. This is ex falso quodlibet. The empty type is falsehood.
1 has exactly one introduction: * : 1. Its elimination is trivial. The unit type is trivial truth.
Coproduct types (A + B)
Formation: If A and B are types, A + B is a type.
Introduction: inl(a) : A + B if a : A. inr(b) : A + B if b : B.
Elimination: case analysis — provide handlers for both cases.
This is disjunction. A proof of "P or Q" is either a proof of P (tagged left) or a proof of Q (tagged right). Constructive disjunction — you always know which side.
Now — polymorphism
In this system, polymorphism shows up through Π-types over universes. The polymorphic identity function is:
id : Π(A : U₀). A → A id = λ(A). λ(x). x
This is a function that takes a type A as its first argument, then a value of that type, and returns it. The polymorphism is Π over the universe — "for all types A."
The key theorems about polymorphism in this setting:
Parametricity (Reynolds' theorem, adapted to type theory): A polymorphic function must behave uniformly across all types. If f : Π(A:U).A → A, then f must be the identity function. There's no way to inspect what A is and do something different. This is because types in MLTT carry no runtime information — the polymorphism is truly uniform.
Naturality from parametricity: Any polymorphic function between type constructors is automatically a natural transformation. If you have f : Π(A:U). F(A) → G(A) where F and G are type constructors, parametricity forces f to commute with all maps. This is where category theory and type theory converge — the categorical concept of naturality falls out of parametric polymorphism for free.
Preservation under substitution: If a : A and A = B type, then a : B. Types that are equal are interchangeable everywhere. This seems obvious but it's doing important work — it means type equality is a congruence.
Now — connecting to SRT's seven roles
Your three-bit classification from R(x,y,t):
S only (100): pure subject — relates to distinct others but is never an object or a type
O only (010): pure object — is related to, but never relates outward or serves as a type
R only (001): pure relational — serves as a relationship type but never appears as subject or object
S+O (110): subject-object — both relates and is related to
S+R (101): subject-relational — relates to others and also serves as a type
O+R (011): object-relational — is related to and also serves as a type
S+O+R (111): fully polymorphic — can fill any role
In MLTT, these roles are rigidly separated by the judgment structure. A term a lives at one level. A type A lives at a higher level. A universe U lives higher still. The only way something can be both a term and a type is through the universe hierarchy — A : U₀ makes A simultaneously a term (of U₀) and a type (that can have its own terms). But this is always mediated by the universe.
In SRT, the roles are properties that a referent earns through its relationships:
t -> R makes t a relationship type (gains R role). x.S means x has subjecthood (gains S role). y.O means y has objecthood (gains O role). And crucially, these aren't mutually exclusive — the same referent can have all three properties simultaneously.

For the combined roles: This is where SRT genuinely extends beyond MLTT. The first argument is a type. The second argument is a value of that type. In TypeScript, the generic parameter <T> is the first Π. The compiler infers T at each call site. In MLTT, you pass it explicitly (though inference is often added as sugar).

Step 2: Intuitionistic Logic
Intuitionistic logic has the same connectives as classical logic: ∧ (and), ∨ (or), ⇒ (implies), ¬ (not), ∀ (for all), ∃ (there exists). Same syntax. Same formation rules. Quantifiers are in.
What changes is the meaning of each connective. In classical logic, a proposition is true or false — that's its meaning. In intuitionistic logic, a proposition's meaning is its proof conditions. Specifically:
A ∧ B means: you have a proof of A and a proof of B.
A ∨ B means: you have a proof of A, or you have a proof of B, and you know which one.
A ⇒ B means: you have a method that converts any proof of A into a proof of B.
¬A means: A ⇒ ⊥. A method that converts any proof of A into a proof of falsehood. (i.e., having a proof of A is impossible.)
∃x.P(x) means: you have a specific witness w and a proof of P(w).
∀x.P(x) means: you have a method that, given any x, produces a proof of P(x).
In classical logic, ∃x.P(x) can be true without anyone knowing which x works. In intuitionistic logic, that's meaningless — existence requires a witness.
