The Parable of the Talents is from the Gospel of Matthew (chapter 25, verses 14–30). In it, a master going on a journey entrusts his property to three servants. He gives one servant five talents, another two, and a third one talent — each according to their ability. A "talent" was actually a unit of weight/currency in the ancient world, worth a very large sum (roughly 20 years of a laborer's wages), so yes, it essentially involves coins/money.
The first two servants invest and double their money. When the master returns, he praises them: "Well done, good and faithful servant." The third servant, afraid of the master's harshness, buries his single talent in the ground and returns only what he was given. The master rebukes him, calling him wicked and lazy, takes away his talent, and gives it to the servant who had ten.
The parable is generally interpreted as a lesson about using the gifts and opportunities God gives you rather than squandering them out of fear or complacency. It's also where the English word "talent" (meaning skill or ability) actually originates — the meaning shifted over centuries from the monetary unit to the metaphorical idea of God-given gifts that should be cultivated.

Yes, it's well-documented. The trail goes:
Greek τάλαντον (talanton) → a unit of weight (originally meaning "balance" or "scales") → then a unit of currency equal to that weight in silver or gold
Latin talentum → borrowed from Greek, same monetary meaning
Old English talente → attested only in the monetary/weight sense
Then the key shift: the meaning "gift committed to one for use and improvement" developed by mid-15th century, probably mostly from the parable of the talents in Matthew 25:14-30. The OED is pretty direct about it — its etymology for the "mental endowment; natural ability" definition simply reads "From the parable of the talents, Matt. xxv. 14-30."
And then the general sense of "special natural ability or aptitude" emerged by around 1600.
There's also an interesting wrinkle — the earliest uses of the word in the "ability" sense cited in the OED all have explicit reference to a talent being something entrusted by God for which one must give account. So the theological framing was baked right into the early usage. It only later secularized into just meaning "you're good at something."
Calvin apparently played a role too — he helped shape the modern meaning by defining talents as gifts from God in the form of a person's calling or natural ability, extending their use beyond church duties to encompass all of life.
So yes — your intuition was exactly right. It's not a coincidence, it's a direct line from the parable to the word.

OK, now I have enough to give you a considered answer. Let me be precise about what makes the "talent" case special and then assess which other words truly parallel it.
The specific pattern with "talent" is: a word existed with meaning A (unit of currency), the Bible used it in a metaphorical/parabolic context, and that metaphorical usage became the word's primary meaning in English (meaning B: innate ability). The original meaning is now basically dead. That's a very specific and unusual phenomenon.
Here are the candidates I can identify, roughly ranked by how closely they match:
Scapegoat is probably the closest parallel, though it works slightly differently. The word was coined by William Tyndale in 1530 as a translation of Hebrew azazel in Leviticus, combining "scape" (escape) + "goat." It originally meant the literal ritual goat sent into the wilderness on Yom Kippur. The transferred meaning — "one who is blamed or punished for the mistakes or sins of others" — is recorded by 1824. So the word was invented for the Bible and then its meaning shifted metaphorically. That's slightly different from "talent" where a pre-existing word got a new meaning from biblical usage, but it's close — the literal goat meaning is now dead, and the metaphorical meaning is all that survives.
Shibboleth is another interesting case. In the biblical story, the nation of Gilead used the word — which meant "stalk of grain" — as a test to identify members of the Tribe of Ephraim, who couldn't pronounce it correctly. Today "shibboleth" means a password, test phrase, or distinguishing marker of a group. Nobody uses it to mean grain anymore. The Bible used it in a narrative context where its function (as a linguistic test) overshadowed its meaning (grain), and that function became the English word's definition.
Nimrod is a fascinating case, though it goes through an extra twist. When "nimrod" first entered English, it referred to a tyrant or mighty hunter, based on the biblical figure. Then — and this is debated but widely believed — Bugs Bunny sarcastically called Elmer Fudd a "nimrod," and the "fool/idiot" meaning took over. So the Bible gave it one metaphorical meaning, and then American pop culture gave it another. That's a two-stage drift rather than the clean single-step you see with "talent."
Behemoth has a simpler path: this English word meaning a huge creature comes from the Book of Job, where it describes a large, grass-eating animal. The Hebrew word may have just meant "beast" or "cattle," but through Job's dramatic description, English adopted it to mean something of monstrous size. Not quite as clean as "talent" — the meaning didn't really reverse or fundamentally shift so much as amplify.
Babel is worth mentioning too — the Tower of Babel story gave English "babble," meaning confused or nonsensical sounds. Though there's etymological debate about whether "babble" truly derives from Babel or is independently onomatopoetic.
So to directly answer your question: I think "talent" is genuinely the cleanest, most dramatic example of this pattern. "Scapegoat" and "shibboleth" come closest, but they work through slightly different mechanisms — one was coined for the translation, the other shifted from a noun's meaning to its narrative function. "Talent" is uniquely elegant because a common, mundane word (a unit of weight) got completely redefined by a single parable's metaphorical power, and the new meaning fully displaced the old one. It's arguably the most profound single-word impact the Bible has had on the English lexicon.

That's a really elegant abstraction. Currency is already a symbol — it has no intrinsic value, it's pure potential convertible into something real. So a unit of currency is inherently unstable as a word, because it's always pointing away from itself toward the thing it buys. If a talent mostly buys "one career's worth of labor," then the word is already almost synonymous with a life's productive capacity. The symbol just... collapses into what it symbolizes.
And that's a general principle beyond this one case. Money words are especially vulnerable to this kind of semantic absorption precisely because they're already abstract placeholders. The word has no concrete referent to anchor it — unlike, say, "stone" or "river" — so it drifts naturally toward whatever it most commonly represents in practice.
Which also explains why this transformation is rare. Most currency units aren't tightly associated with one specific thing they purchase. A dollar buys everything and nothing in particular, so "dollar" stays meaning "dollar." But a talent was such a large sum — roughly a career — that it had a natural semantic attractor pulling it toward "what a person is worth." The parable just gave that drift a story to crystallize around.
