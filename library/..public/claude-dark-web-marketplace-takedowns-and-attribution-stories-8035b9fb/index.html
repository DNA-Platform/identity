Good questions — the three have very different arcs. Here's what the sources actually say.
AlphaBay (2017) — the classic opsec slip. The administrator was Alexandre Cazes, a Canadian living in Bangkok who operated as "Alpha02" and "admin." The attribution came from a default mail setting nobody had scrubbed: AlphaBay's welcome emails carried the header address pimp_alex_91@hotmail.com, which traced back to Cazes. From that thread, investigators "followed the money" through his Bitcoin transactions, payments and cashouts — partly using open-source tools. The operation was named Bayonet — reportedly a triple pun on "bay," "net," and catching "bad guys." On July 5, 2017, the Royal Thai Police, assisted by the FBI and DEA, arrested Cazes at his Bangkok residence, where they found his laptop open and unencrypted — capturing passkeys to the site and servers. A week later he apparently took his own life in Thai custody. The clever second half: Dutch police had already quietly taken control of Hansa, a competitor, and as AlphaBay's users poured in looking for safety, they were signing into a market law enforcement was running — for about 27 days the Dutch collected usernames, passwords, messages and shipping addresses.
Archetyp (2025) — attribution by infrastructure mapping, not a single slip. Operation Deep Sentinel ran June 11–13, 2025, backed by Europol and Eurojust, with Germany, the Netherlands, Romania, Spain and Sweden participating. A 30-year-old German national accused of being the administrator was arrested at his residence in Barcelona by Spanish police; a moderator and six top vendors were hit in Germany and Sweden, the Netherlands-hosted infrastructure was dismantled, and about €7.8M was seized. On method, authorities were deliberately less specific than in the Cazes case — no public "Hotmail moment." Europol said the takedown followed years of work to map the platform's technical architecture and identify the people behind it, by tracing financial flows and analyzing digital forensic evidence. Scale: over 600,000 users and at least €250 million in transactions over five-plus years, notable for tolerating fentanyl listings.
Matanga — no, not a comparable story, and that's the interesting part. I couldn't find a sourced capture-and-attribution narrative for Matanga the way there is for the other two. In the Russian-language coverage it appears as one of several Russian darknet shops listed alongside the giants — the "mastodons" being Hydra and WayAway, with Matanga and Darkcon named as smaller, harder-to-trust shops. The nearest Russian analog to Bayonet/Deep Sentinel is Hydra: German authorities seized Hydra's servers and ~€23M in bitcoin in April 2022, the U.S. named Dmitry Pavlov as administrator of Hydra's servers, and Russia then arrested him in Moscow — though he denied the charges, saying he only rented out servers. Three separate things tangled together here, and the sourcing pulls them apart cleanly.
"Distributed" is mostly a red herring for why they survive. These markets aren't peer-to-peer or truly distributed — they're centralized operations. One admin team, one escrow system, one set of wallets, servers in specific physical locations. What they have isredundancy: markets replicate their infrastructure across multiple domains or use decentralized hosting, so if one domain is seized, users get redirected to mirror sites through trusted community channels. The .onion addresses themselves rotate — each market has a .onion address that isn't indexed and changes periodically to evade seizure, with users finding current addresses through forums and Telegram channels. But that's DDoS-resistance and single-domain resilience, not seizure-proofing. AlphaBay had mirrors too, and its servers still got seized. So the mirror architecture buys uptime and time; it is not why the Russian markets outlast Western ones. The reason they outlast is jurisdiction and political protection — where the servers and the operators physically sit — not how the site is wired.
There's no separate "Georgia Kraken" or "Ukraine Kraken." Kraken is one marketplace with one infrastructure, serving a Russian-speaking clientele spread across Russia and the former-Soviet states. The "national" flavor lives only in the physical logistics layer — the klad/dead-drop couriers who bury product are local to each city — but the storefront, the escrow, the wallets, the admin team are a single centralized operation. It's the same site, same servers, whether the buyer is in Tbilisi or Moscow. (Caveat worth stating plainly: the exact server topology of any live market isn't public, so I can't verify Kraken's specific hosting setup — but the one-market, one-operation model is what the reporting consistently describes, and it's why a single seizure kills the whole thing rather than a national branch.)
Georgia's size is real but not the binding constraint. Georgia does have a cybercrime capability — modest. The Cyber Crime Division of the Central Criminal Police Department at the MIA was established in December 2012, with the forensics team of the Ministry of the Interior handling forensic examinations, and a 24/7 National Contact Point operating within the Division. That CoE snapshot put the division at 15 detective-investigators responsible for investigating cybercrime. It leans heavily on outside help: mutual legal assistance runs through a single central authority at the Ministry of Justice, and requests can be transmitted via the 24/7 contact point or Interpol. It's also a participant in Council-of-Europe/Interpol capacity-building — the GLACY+ program assists countries in setting up cybercrime and digital-forensics units and enhances cooperation through INTERPOL's regional working groups.
So yes: Georgia doesn't have the scale to independently de-anonymize servers and run blockchain tracing at the level that dismantles a major market. That work is concentrated in a handful of large agencies — FBI, DEA, German BKA, Dutch police, Europol's EC3 — plus private analytics firms like Chainalysis and TRM. Small countries contribute or rely on cooperation; they don't run Bayonet-scale operations solo.
But here's the part that reframes the whole question: even a lavishly-resourced Georgia couldn't take Kraken down, because the servers and the people are in Russia. Attribution isn't the bottleneck — reach is. The West has the forensic capacity and still can't touch the Russian markets directly; the best they've managed is seizing infrastructure that happened to sit on German soil (Hydra's servers). Georgia's smallness would matter if the target were inside Georgia's jurisdiction. For a Russia-hosted, politically-protected market, no country outside Russia — large or small — can execute the takedown. That's why the only Hydra arrests-and-life-sentence outcome came from inside Russia's own system, on its own selective terms.

You've got the structure basically right, and the reporting backs the intuition. Here's the documented anatomy.
It's a compartmentalized supply chain, not a flat network. The vendor operations behind a market like Kraken are tiered orgs. TRM describes them as bringing together networks of couriers, wholesalers, drug producers, DNM management teams, and even customer-service operatives. The GI-TOC "Breaking Klad" report lays out the layers explicitly: chemists/producers at the top, then wholesalers (склад) and carriers (перевозчик), then the shop's management, and at the bottom the couriers — the vendors run a network of chemists, wholesalers, and kladmen.
The key point you're reaching for — the anonymity layer between tiers — is real and it's by design. The tiers connect mostly through the same dead-drop mechanic that reaches the customer, plus Telegram and the market platform, so people at adjacent levels often never meet. A kladman typically receives a wholesale stash via a master-drop, breaks it into retail bags, buries those, photographs the GPS coordinates, and uploads them — the location gets unlocked when a customer pays. Retail-size bags are secreted using spycraft by an army of young kladmen who upload dead-drop locations, unlocked when customers make an online purchase. Crypto handles the money, Telegram handles coordination and recruitment. So there's no hand-to-hand contact to surveil and no single conversation that links a courier up to an operator.
That compartmentalization is even enforced socially, not just technically. There's a whole violence layer that exists precisely to keep the bottom tier disciplined and disposable: "sportsmen" — hired thugs — roam looking for "seagulls" (people who steal dead drops) and punish kladmen on behalf of vendors, uploading the punishments to social media. And the bottom tier is deliberately expendable — a conveyor belt of poorly paid Russians, with prisons full of young kladmen.
Now the enforcement consequence, which answers your Georgia question directly. Because the layers are compartmentalized, the only tier a local police force can realistically reach is the physical one — the kladman burying bags in a Tbilisi courtyard. You can stake out dead-drop sites, catch couriers, maybe flip one. But a caught kladman knows almost nothing: a Telegram handle, a stash location, a wallet. He can't lead you up the chain, because the chain was built so he can't. So local enforcement is stuck arresting the most replaceable, lowest-value layer — and the org just recruits more. That's a very different problem from catching a producer or an operator.
Two things compound it for a country like Georgia specifically. First, exactly as you say, the small unit (~15 investigators, per the Council of Europe snapshot) can work the local physical layer but has no path to the platform's servers or the vendor orgs' management, which sit inside Russia. Second — and this is a structural point about production vs distribution that you flagged — the dominant Russian-sphere product is synthetic: mephedrone, a stimulant powder known as "salt." Synthetics are often made in domestic labs rather than imported, which means the classic interdiction lever — catching drugs crossing a border — doesn't bite the way it does for cocaine or heroin. The whole chain from chemist to kladman can live inside the Russian-speaking ecosystem, vertically integrated, never crossing a customs checkpoint you could watch.
And this isn't hypothetical for your region — the model has explicitly spread outward. It has moved into Russian-occupied areas of Ukraine, where soldiers use Telegram channels and dead drops, and its spread is most pronounced in neighbouring countries. So Georgia isn't looking at a foreign curiosity; it's looking at the same kladman-and-dead-drop system operating locally, coordinated from platforms it can't reach, staffed by a bottom tier that's the only part it can touch.
So your model is right, with one refinement worth stating plainly: the thing that defeats a small country here isn't only the anonymity of the comms layer — it's that even perfect de-anonymization of the local layer only ever nets couriers. The insulation is architectural (compartmentalized tiers) and jurisdictional (top tiers in Russia) at the same time. Signal/Telegram is the visible surface of it, but even if you cracked every message, the design still stops the roll-up.
If you want the sharpest single source, the GI-TOC "Breaking Klad" report (Daly & Shortis, Nov 2024) is the definitive open-source map of this whole hierarchy — it's where most of the tier-by-tier detail above comes from.

Right — and the reason it's confusing is that street testing isn't part of the "caught at the pickup or not" game at all. It's a separate axis of exposure that your pickup opsec does nothing to protect against.
Here's the mechanism. Street testing means police stop a person — on the street, outside a club, at a checkpoint, often on a pretext — and compel them to give a urine sample. What the test detects isn't drugs in your pocket; it's metabolites in your body — chemical evidence that you consumed something in the recent past. And in Georgia, that consumption is itself the punishable offense, independent of any possession or transaction. HRW documented exactly this: a man named Kapanadze tested positive, and because it was his first positive test within a year, he was charged with an administrative offense of drug consumption and fined GEL 500 (about $190). No drugs were found on him. The transaction never entered into it. The urine was the case.
So your timing model — put as much delay as you want between payment and pickup, watch for surveillance, accept the risk of losing the stash to a "seagull" in exchange for confidence nobody's watching the drop — is a possession defense. It protects you from being caught holding the drugs, or caught standing at the dead drop. It's genuinely good against that threat. But it's aimed at the wrong threat. Street testing doesn't care where or when you bought, or whether you were careful at the drop. It catches you afterward, based on what's already in your bloodstream, days later, walking down the street with nothing on you.
That's the whole point of why it's such a powerful (and, per HRW, abusive) tool against a dead-drop system: the dead-drop model was engineered to eliminate the risky moments of the transaction — no dealer meeting, no hand-to-hand, minimal time holding. Street testing simply ignores the transaction and targets the one thing you can't hide or time around, which is your own metabolism. The vulnerability window isn't "payment to pickup" — it's "consumption to when the metabolites clear," which is a few days for stimulants like MDMA or mephedrone and up to weeks for cannabis. During that window you're exposed to a pretextual stop no matter how flawless your buying was.
And it compounds: HRW described police checking a person's ID against a database, and once they saw a prior administrative offense, transferring them for a mandatory test — so one positive result marks you for repeated future stops. The system also produced real atrocities in its enforcement — one man died shortly after a forced testing detention in which police allegedly gave him diuretic pills to force a urine sample.
So to answer "you're either caught or you're not" — yes, but there are two independent ways to get caught, and they're decoupled. One is at the transaction (possession/pickup), where your timing opsec helps. The other is your body chemistry afterward (consumption testing), where it's irrelevant. Georgia leaned overwhelmingly on the second, which is precisely why careful darknet buyers still got swept up — the state wasn't trying to catch them buying, it was catching them for having already used.
