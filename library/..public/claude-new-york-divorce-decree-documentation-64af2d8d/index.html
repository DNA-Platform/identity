Yes. In New York, the court-issued document ending a marriage is formally titled "Judgment of Divorce" (form UD-11), signed by a Supreme Court justice, but it's commonly referred to as a divorce decree — both terms are used interchangeably by NY courts, county clerks, and the Department of Health.
NY actually has two related documents:
Divorce decree / Judgment of Divorce — the full court order with all terms (custody, support, property, etc.), filed with the County Clerk in the county where the case was heard.
Divorce certificate — a short summary record (parties, date, place) filed with the NYS Department of Health, only for divorces granted on or after January 1, 1963.
For divorces granted before January 1, 1963, the divorce decree is the only document available — no certificate exists. To get a certified copy of the decree itself, you contact the County Clerk where the divorce was granted, not the state.

What Georgia is looking for is the Judgment of Divorce itself (the actual decree from the NY Supreme Court) — apostilled and translated into Georgian. That single document establishes both: (a) the marriage existed, and (b) it was legally terminated, with terms.
The full package Georgian authorities typically require for foreign divorce recognition:
Certified copy of the Judgment of Divorce from the County Clerk in the NY county where the divorce was granted. Not a certificate, not a summary — the full signed judgment.
Apostille from the NY Department of State (Albany) — Georgia joined the Hague Apostille Convention in 2007, so an apostille is sufficient; no further legalization needed.
Certified Georgian translation by a notarized translator in Tbilisi (House of Justice or a licensed translation bureau can do both translation + notarization in one stop).
Optionally: your marriage certificate, also apostilled and translated, if they're questioning whether the marriage existed at all.
The certificate of no appeals you already have is useful as a supplement attached to the decree, but on its own it answers the wrong question.

The apostille can only be issued by the NY Department of State — not by a US embassy, not by DC, and not from Georgia. So the document has to physically travel back to New York. There's no remote workaround for that.
The process for a NY court-issued divorce decree:
Verify your decree is properly certified by the County Clerk. Look at it — does it have the County Clerk's signature and a raised or colored seal (red, blue, purple) saying "true copy" or "certified"? A judge's signature alone is not enough. NY DOS requires the County Clerk to first authenticate the judge's/clerk's signature before they'll apostille.
If yes: skip to step 2.
If no: you need a fresh certified copy from the County Clerk in the county where the divorce was granted. They mail it for a small fee.
Submit to NY Department of State for apostille. $10/document. Mail-in works; you don't need to appear. Address: NYS Department of State, Division of Licensing Services, Apostille Unit (Albany).
Return shipping. Include a prepaid international courier envelope (DHL/FedEx) or use your Traveling Mailbox in Sanford as the return address, then forward to Tbilisi.
Translate in Tbilisi. Once apostilled, take it to any licensed translation bureau or the House of Justice — they'll do certified Georgian translation of both the decree and the apostille. The apostille itself must be translated, not just the decree.
Practical options from where you are:
DIY by mail: You ship the decree to a NY contact (or directly to the County Clerk if it needs re-certification), then they forward to NY DOS, then back to your Traveling Mailbox, then to you. Cheapest. 4–8 weeks realistic, plus shipping.
Apostille service: Companies like Apoling Solutions, Global Apostille, or American Notary Service Center handle the whole chain (County Clerk → DOS → international shipping). Roughly $75–150 in service fees plus apostille and shipping. They can also retrieve a fresh certified copy from the court if yours isn't clerk-certified. 2–3 weeks typical.
Two things I'd want to know to give you the tightest next step:
Which NY county was the divorce granted in?
Is the decree physically in Tbilisi with you, or somewhere in the US?

No. The apostille authenticates a specific document instance, not a signature in the abstract. Even though it's the same clerk's signature, each document is a separate physical instance requiring its own apostille — that's why NY DOS charges per document ($10 each), not per signature or per signer.
The only exception is when documents are physically bound together by the clerk into a single attested packet (ribbon-bound with a notation declaring them one instrument). Decree + certificate of no appeals are almost always issued as separate documents, so they'd each need their own apostille.
Practically: just send both. $20 total instead of $10, same envelope, same trip — the marginal cost is trivial compared to the risk of Georgia rejecting one of them.

Your coordinators are right on the practical reality. Here's what the law actually says vs. what happens in practice:
Formal rule: Georgia's Law on Civil Acts (Dec 2011) does list "documents confirming the termination of previous marriages" as a required submission for previously-married foreigners, and the strictest reading says these may need apostille or consular legalization. That's the textbook answer.
Practical reality: Georgia is famous for being one of the easiest countries in the world to marry in — within 24 hours of arriving, in some cases. Implementation at individual House of Justice branches is highly flexible, and experienced coordinators have working relationships with specific officers who accept affidavit-level evidence routinely. There's no certificate-of-no-impediment requirement (unlike most EU countries), no waiting period, no medical exam, no residency requirement.
So if the people walking you through this say your affidavit is enough for the specific branch they're submitting to, that's based on knowing what that branch actually accepts. The apostilled decree would be the bulletproof version, but it's not the only version that works.
The real exposure isn't Georgia — it's whether the US (or any future jurisdiction) will recognize the marriage if your prior dissolution isn't on file with apostilled evidence. For a US-citizen-to-Russian-citizen marriage registered in Georgia that you might later need recognized in the US for immigration, tax, or estate purposes, that's where the apostilled decree quietly matters more than for the Georgian ceremony itself.
Worth doing the apostille in the next month or two regardless — just for the file, not for Wednesday.
