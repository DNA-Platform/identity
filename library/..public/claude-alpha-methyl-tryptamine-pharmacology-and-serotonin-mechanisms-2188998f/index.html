AMT (Indopan in the old Soviet clinical literature) is unusual because it hits three mechanisms at overlapping potencies rather than being dominated by one. It acts as a releasing agent of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, as a serotonin receptor agonist, and as a weak monoamine oxidase inhibitor. The IC50 for MAO-A inhibition is about 380 nM, similar to para-methoxyamphetamine (PMA) and 4-MTA — worth flagging because chronic dosing with MAOI-plus-releaser drugs is the mechanism behind PMA's serotonergic toxicity, and AMT is pharmacologically in that same family.
On your specific SERT vs 5-HT2A question, the two activities are roughly comparable in potency, which is the interesting part. For monoamine release in rat synaptosomes, αMT's EC50 values are 22–68 nM for serotonin, 79–112 nM for norepinephrine, and 79–180 nM for dopamine — so it's a fairly balanced SNDRA with a modest serotonin preference (the range reflects different labs: Nagai 2007, Blough 2014). At 5-HT2A, αMT is a potent agonist; the 5-fluoro analog (7c) was 3-fold more potent than the unsubstituted α-methyltryptamine (7a) as a 5-HT2A agonist, and since 5-fluoro-AMT's EC50 at 5-HT2A is 8.47 nM with Emax 107%, that puts AMT itself at roughly ~25 nM EC50 as a near-full 5-HT2A agonist. So SERT-substrate EC50 (~22–68 nM) and 5-HT2A EC50 (~25 nM) are within the same order of magnitude. You don't get clean dissociation between "entactogen dose" and "psychedelic dose" the way you sometimes can with MDMA — the 5-HT2A agonism is riding along at every effective dose, which matches the subjective reports of AMT being MDMA-like and frankly psychedelic simultaneously.
On your structural aside (which I read as "it is notable" — I think that's a typo) — yes, it's genuinely the key point. αMT is to tryptamine what amphetamine is to phenethylamine: the α-methyl group blocks MAO metabolism of the primary amine and confers releaser activity at monoamine transporters. But because the scaffold is an indolealkylamine rather than a phenethylamine, it retains high affinity for 5-HT2A (which prefers tryptamine-like ligands). MDMA, being a phenethylamine, is a much weaker direct 5-HT2A agonist — its subjective profile comes almost entirely from SERT reversal plus some 5-HT1A/1B and TAAR1 activity. So AMT isn't really a "cleaner MDMA" — it's closer to a hybrid of MDMA + a tryptamine psychedelic, which is why historical clinical interest (as an antidepressant) was abandoned and why it's probably a worse therapeutic candidate than MDMA rather than better. The therapeutic advantage of MDMA in PTSD trials depends on the absence of strong 5-HT2A agonism.

For the SERT-mediated component, yes — the prediction is very strong and well-supported. MDMA's effect on 5-HT release can be blocked by 5-HT uptake inhibitors such as citalopram, suggesting that MDMA interacts with the 5-HT uptake site, and in healthy volunteers citalopram (40 mg IV) markedly reduced most of the subjective effects of MDMA, including positive mood, increased extraversion and self-confidence, and also attenuated cardiovascular and adverse effects. Escitalopram is the S-enantiomer of citalopram and the pharmacologically active half, so the mechanism transfers directly. Since AMT is a SERT substrate (it has to be carried into the neuron via SERT to reverse the transporter and cause efflux), and escitalopram occupies the same substrate-binding site, chronic escitalopram should meaningfully blunt AMT's serotonin-release component — the empathogenic part.
But the interesting bit is what you'd expect to be preserved. The direct 5-HT2A agonism doesn't require SERT at all; AMT just diffuses across the membrane (it's small and lipophilic) and hits 5-HT2A from either side. The relevant comparator is the psilocybin+escitalopram trial: escitalopram pretreatment did not significantly attenuate ratings of altered states of consciousness from psilocybin, though it reduced bad drug effects, fear, anxiety, and cardiovascular effects. So you'd predict an SSRI + AMT combination to selectively strip away the MDMA-like empathogenic/prosocial layer while leaving the tryptamine-psychedelic perceptual layer largely intact — essentially converting AMT from a hybrid empathogen-psychedelic into something closer to a pure (and probably less pleasant) classical psychedelic.
One important caveat that makes this more of a pharmacological thought experiment than a practical regimen: AMT is also a MAO-A inhibitor with IC50 ~380 nM. An SSRI plus an MAOI is one of the classic serotonin-syndrome combinations, and even though the SSRI would blunt the release component, the combination of SERT blockade (raising baseline 5-HT), MAO-A inhibition (blocking degradation), and residual SERT-reversal activity is exactly the convergence that produced the fatalities with PMA and 4-MTA. So the mechanistic prediction is real but doesn't translate into "it'd just be a weaker trip" — it translates into substantially elevated toxicity risk.

Yes, the recreational history is well-documented. αMT started being used as a recreational drug in the 1960s and use as a designer drug increased in the 1990s; it became a controlled substance in the United States in 2003. As a recreational drug its use remained marginal from the 1960s–90s, then saw an upsurge linked to the clubbing scene, and remained uncontrolled in the UK until 2015. So there's a fairly clean historical arc: Soviet clinical use as Indopan in the 60s (5–10 mg tablets for depression), dormancy, then a revival as a gray-market "research chemical" bought online in the late 90s and early 2000s, then successive scheduling waves (US 2003, Canada, China, UK 2015). Erowid, Bluelight, and PsychonautWiki have extensive trip-report archives going back 20+ years, and there's even a WHO Expert Committee on Drug Dependence critical review from 2014.
Routes: oral is the most common, and users report that αMT may be smoked, with doses between 2 and 20 mg; a dose of 20 mg of αMT is said to be equivalent to 50 μg of LSD. Insufflation and rectal use are also reported but less common. On the freebase question — yes, for smoking you want the freebase rather than the HCl salt. Research-chemical vendors historically sold it in both forms, with "AMT fumarate" and "AMT HCl" being common salts; the freebase has a lower melting point and vaporizes without decomposing, whereas the salts don't vaporize cleanly. So there's no chemistry conversion required in the sense of e.g. freebasing cocaine from its salt with ammonia — you just buy the form you want. The freebase is a solid at room temperature (mp ~98–100 °C).
Duration: yes, famously long and with a slow onset, which is part of what makes it recreationally awkward. An oral dose of AMT takes between 30 and 120 minutes to come up, although some users report onset as long as 180 minutes; due to the long time taken for the effects to develop, users have been known to redose thinking that nothing is happening. Total duration runs 12–16 hours typically, with rare cases or extreme doses exceeding 24 hours. This is almost the opposite profile from MDMA (fast onset, 3–5 hour duration), and closer to mescaline in length.
As for whether it still exists — yes, it's still synthesized and sold on gray/darknet markets, though it's a Schedule I equivalent in most major jurisdictions. The Soviet Indopan tablets are long gone (they were discontinued decades ago, and the pharmaceutical registry in the USSR's successor states doesn't include it). What occasionally still appears is in jurisdictions with slower analogue laws, or as novel homologues (5-MeO-AMT, 5-F-AMT, 4-Me-αMT, isoAMT) that exploit gaps in specific national scheduling language. In terms of clinical existence, it's essentially zero — there's no current or near-term therapeutic program, because its MAO-A inhibition, long duration, and mixed psychedelic-releaser profile are all exactly the features that modern drug developers are trying to remove from analogues. The more interesting descendants from a pharmaceutical standpoint are the non-hallucinogenic derivatives like zalsupindole (DLX-001; AAZ-A-154; (R)-5-MeO-N,N-dimethyl-isoAMT), a non-hallucinogenic serotonin [receptor modulator] being developed by Delix Therapeutics for depression — which keeps the neuroplasticity-promoting scaffold while engineering out the 5-HT2A-mediated perception changes.

Yes, Erowid specifically breaks out the routes: AMT's effects usually last 8-14 hours when swallowed and 3-6 hours when insufflated. So the route does substantially change duration here — roughly a 3x compression. That's meaningful because, as you noted, this isn't universal. For plenty of drugs (e.g., caffeine, amphetamine), the terminal half-life dominates and route just shifts Tmax while total duration stays similar. For AMT the slow gut absorption itself seems to be doing much of the work of "long duration" — once you bypass the gut with insufflation, the pharmacokinetics reveal a shorter underlying elimination curve.
On gut breakdown: AMT isn't really degraded heavily in the gut in the DMT sense. DMT is orally inactive because gut and hepatic MAO-A rips apart unsubstituted tryptamines before they reach systemic circulation — that's why ayahuasca needs a β-carboline MAOI. The whole point of the α-methyl group on AMT (and on amphetamines, for that matter) is that it sterically blocks MAO from oxidatively deaminating the primary amine, making the molecule orally bioavailable. The smoked-vs-oral dose ratio tells the story: common oral dose is 20-40 mg; common smoked dose is 6-10 mg, so maybe 3-4x potency shift across routes. If gut MAO were shredding it, you'd expect something more like the 20-30x shift you see going from oral DMT (inactive) to smoked DMT (active at 20-40 mg). So what's happening with AMT orally is more "slow absorption from a lipophilic base with a basic amine" than "massive first-pass destruction." The slow onset (oral doses have an onset of 30-120 minutes and peak at 3-5 hours) is absorption kinetics.
On the "smoother onset curve when smoked, MDMA alternative" framing — I'd push back a bit. A few threads tangled together in the folk claim:
Smoked AMT is sharper, not smoother, than oral MDMA. Any vaporized freebase amine crosses the BBB in seconds to a couple minutes. What smoked AMT is smoother than is smoked DMT, because AMT's lower 5-HT2A potency and longer half-life give a less "rocketship" ascent. That comparison makes sense. Comparison to MDMA doesn't really — oral MDMA comes on over 30-60 minutes, which is gentler than any smoked route.
The "not so thirsty/toasty" claim (I'm reading that as dehydration/hyperthermia) isn't well-supported. At doses over 30 mg, αMT may cause several side effects, including anxiety, muscle tightness, vomiting, and hyperthermia, and the fatalities associated with AMT — particularly in the 2012-2015 UK "legal high" period where there were 22 deaths linked to αMT in England and Wales — often involved hyperthermia and serotonin-syndrome features, which is exactly what you'd predict from a SNDRA-plus-MAOI pharmacology. MDMA has a worse thermal reputation mostly because it's used at higher doses in dancing-in-crowded-clubs contexts, but AMT isn't intrinsically cooler.
Why AMT isn't being developed as an MDMA-alternative therapeutic: the 8-14 hour oral duration is a serious clinical liability (MAPS-style MDMA sessions are 6-8 hours and that's already at the edge of what's tolerable for therapist-dyad protocols), the mandatory 5-HT2A agonism means you can't separate "emotional processing" from "perceptual alterations" in the way MDMA therapy explicitly tries to, and the MAOI component creates drug-drug interaction risk with the SSRIs many PTSD patients are on. The therapeutic question isn't "can we find something MDMA-like?" but "can we find something with MDMA's prosocial-emotional profile and fewer complications" — and AMT adds complications rather than removing them. The actively-developed compounds in this space are things like the Emerald Bioscience/MAPS programs and Delix's non-hallucinogenic plasticity-promoters, which are going the opposite direction from AMT.

Honest answer: the published and community literature on AMT intranasal is thinner than for 2C-B or MDMA — Erowid's dose page lists oral and smoked explicitly but leaves intranasal more vague, with the FAQ author noting "AMT can be snorted, but I don't know too much about this." Most trip reports land at a similar dose to oral or slightly less, not the dramatic reduction you see with 2C-B. So there isn't a clean authoritative number, but the best inference is that AMT sits closer to the MDMA end of the spectrum than the 2C-B end, with the duration compression being the dominant effect (8-14 hours oral vs 3-6 hours insufflated) rather than a big potency shift.
The pharmacokinetic reason this should be true is worth unpacking, because it's exactly the question you're asking. The size of the oral-vs-intranasal potency gap is mostly a function of first-pass metabolism. 2C-B hits hepatic MAO-A and CYP2D6 hard on first pass; oral bioavailability estimates are in the 30-60% range, so bypassing the liver with nasal uptake gives you roughly the 2-3x potency boost people describe (10 mg insufflated ≈ 20-30 mg oral). MDMA has high oral bioavailability (~70-75%) and the major metabolic losses happen later in the pharmacokinetic curve, so bypassing first-pass gives only modest dose compression — the ~25% reduction you mentioned. AMT has the α-methyl group blocking MAO deamination (the whole reason it's orally active at all, unlike DMT), so it behaves more like MDMA on this axis: decent oral bioavailability, modest intranasal dose reduction, duration compression that's really about removing the slow gut-absorption phase rather than about bypassing massive first-pass loss.
On nasal tolerability specifically — AMT isn't the 2C-B-style excruciating chemical burn, but it's widely reported as unpleasant for a different reason: it has a famously foul smell and taste that lingers in the sinus drip for hours. Erowid's FAQ page has the line "AMT doesn't taste all that bad. (It's the smell that'll get to you)," which captures the user consensus. The 2C-x compounds are known for the burn because the freebase nitrogen is on a short ethylamine tether from a relatively electron-rich aromatic ring and the molecules are small and highly water-soluble after protonation, giving fast mucosal irritation. AMT's irritation profile is more "sinuses feel awful for 10+ hours" than "acid glass for 20 minutes."
So to directly answer your comparative framing: literature and user-report convergence is that AMT intranasal is MDMA-like in dose ratio (small reduction, maybe 25-50% at most), not 2C-B-like, and the unpleasantness is chronic-smell rather than acute-burn. The salient clinical point is that going intranasal doesn't really "fix" any of the pharmacology problems that make AMT a bad MDMA-alternative — it just trades a 12-hour session for a 4-hour one while keeping the hyperthermia risk, the mandatory 5-HT2A trip, and the MAOI interaction profile.

Yes, exactly right, and this is basically correct first-principles pharmacokinetics. For a given dose with comparable bioavailability across routes, AUC is conserved (that's definitional — AUC = F·Dose/CL, and CL is a property of the body, not the route). So if you squeeze the dwell time by 3x, the curve has to get taller by roughly 3x to preserve the integral. Empirically this is exactly what the AMT dose data shows: smoked common is 6-10 mg vs oral common 20-40 mg, which is a ~3-4x reduction in dose for roughly equivalent (or more intense) subjective peak experiences in trip reports. Same story for 2C-B where 10 mg insufflated ≈ 25 mg oral in subjective intensity. You're getting more peak-per-milligram when the delivery is compressed, which means you can use less drug to reach the same Cmax while delivering it over a shorter window.
The caveat you already flagged (sigmoidal receptor dose-response) is the main thing that bends the relationship away from strictly linear. Once you saturate a receptor population, additional Cmax doesn't buy you additional subjective effect at that target. But — and this is where your pharmacology background pays off — different targets have different EC50s, so higher peaks can recruit secondary pharmacology that was subthreshold at the oral plateau. For AMT specifically: SERT substrate activity EC50 ~22-68 nM, 5-HT2A EC50 ~25 nM, but MAO-A IC50 is ~380 nM. An oral dose might give plasma levels that sit comfortably above SERT/5-HT2A thresholds but barely tickle MAO-A. A smoked or snorted dose that achieves a much higher transient Cmax can briefly push MAO-A inhibition into a meaningfully relevant regime — which changes the qualitative profile, not just the intensity. So the compressed route isn't just "same trip, shorter" — the shape of the receptor-occupancy polypharmacy shifts.
The other pharmacokinetic subtlety is that oral AMT's long duration isn't really about the elimination half-life — it's about absorption-limited kinetics. Slow gut absorption keeps plasma levels flat-topped (steady-state-like) for hours because the drug is being fed in at roughly the rate it's being cleared. The intranasal/smoked curve reveals the underlying elimination half-life, which is probably something like 3-4 hours. So your mental model should be: oral gives you a rectangular plasma profile with modest height and long width; fast routes give you a spiked profile with the same area but triangular shape. The "duration" change is really the oral rectangle collapsing toward the intrinsic decay curve.
The clinical translation of this: from a therapeutic standpoint, the AUC conservation is actually a problem rather than an advantage. The total integrated serotonergic exposure — and therefore the potential for downstream serotonergic neurotoxicity, which is well-documented for αET and suspected for αMT at high doses — scales with AUC, not with subjective duration. So intranasal AMT doesn't reduce the total neurotoxic load per dose; it just concentrates it into a shorter window with a higher Cmax, which if anything is worse from the "peak metabolic stress on serotonergic terminals" standpoint. This is one of the reasons no legitimate pharma program has tried to develop AMT via alternative routes — rearranging the time-intensity trade-off doesn't address the underlying pharmacology liabilities.

Yes, the onset compression is actually more dramatic than the duration compression, which is striking. Oral AMT takes 30-180 minutes to come on; insufflated is reported around 5-30 minutes (one of the Erowid-archived Usenet reports notes "I started out with 4 mg intranasally, which burns and smells bad. The effect took quite a while to come on, even by this route of ingestion" — "quite a while" in context meaning tens of minutes, not two hours); smoked is seconds to a couple of minutes. So the onset ratio is something like 30-60x compression (oral 60-120 min → smoked ~1 min), whereas duration compression is only ~3x (14 hr → 4 hr). This asymmetry is a dead giveaway that the oral curve is absorption-rate-limited on the front end and clearance-rate-limited on the back end. The fast routes reveal the "true" pharmacokinetics: a sharpish peak with a modest half-life, probably 3-4 hours based on the insufflated duration.
Your hypothesis on the clinical rationale is actually quite sound, and I think it's the correct read of why Indopan existed at all. Indopan was dosed at 5-10 mg — well below the 15-20 mg recreational threshold — and at that sub-psychedelic range you'd get meaningful monoamine modulation (SERT substrate activity, mild MAO-A inhibition, some 5-HT2A tone) without the perceptual effects. The long duration in that regime is precisely the clinically desirable feature. Modern pharma consensus is explicit about this: a half-life of 12-48 hours is generally ideal for once-daily dosing of oral drugs, and compliance with antidepressants (which are notoriously skipped) benefits enormously from forgiving kinetics. Fluoxetine's half-life is 1-3 days, and that of its active metabolite norfluoxetine is 7-15 days, which is a big reason Prozac became dominant — missed doses don't crash plasma levels and discontinuation syndromes are mild. So Indopan at 5-10 mg with an 8-14 hour effect would have looked, to a 1960s Soviet psychiatrist, like a perfectly reasonable once-daily antidepressant with built-in forgiveness for noncompliance. The long duration was the pharmaceutical asset.
What the Soviet program presumably underestimated was the ceiling problem. AMT has a narrow therapeutic index if you treat it as an antidepressant: doubling or tripling the dose doesn't just give you "more antidepressant effect," it recruits the 5-HT2A agonism and higher SERT occupancy and starts producing perception changes, hyperthermia risk, and MAOI-like food/drug interaction vulnerabilities. Modern SSRIs have basically flat dose-response curves above threshold for precisely this reason — you can't really "overdose into a trip" on escitalopram, which makes them clinically manageable in a way AMT never was. The discontinuation of Indopan reportedly happened because of serious side effects, which in context probably means patient reports of psychedelic-range effects at inadvertently high doses, plus the MAOI-related tyramine/food concerns that started being recognized around that period (the "cheese effect" was characterized in 1963 with tranylcypromine).
The modern equivalent of your intuition — "can we keep the long-duration dosing advantage without the ceiling problem?" — is part of why compounds like vortioxetine (57-66 hour half-life, multimodal serotonergic action, but no releaser activity and no MAOI) were developed. They kept the "long duration = good compliance" feature and engineered out exactly the pharmacology that made AMT unusable. So your read is historically correct: the long duration was considered a feature, not an incidental property. The problem was that it was attached to a pharmacology that couldn't be scaled safely.

You're right, and I was parsing your earlier claim too literally. The distinction you're drawing — betweenabsolute speed of arrival (onset latency) and shape of the subjective rise curve once effects have begun (onset profile) — is the right one, and it does actually show up in the literature. You can have a route that reaches effect quickly but then ramps subjective intensity gradually, versus oral MDMA which reaches effect slowly but then rushes sharply through the come-up phase once the threshold is crossed.
The AMT subjective-profile reports do support what you're describing. PsychonautWiki's description of AMT's body high specifically emphasizes its non-wave-like quality: this sensation does not manifest itself in the form of a continuously shifting tingling sensation that travels up and down the body spontaneously; it is instead felt like an extended, unchanging activation of every nerve ending on the body that lasts throughout the entire duration of the experience. That's the opposite of the MDMA pattern, where the rushing/waving quality during come-up is characteristic enough to be a diagnostic subjective marker. PsychonautWiki also notes that AMT's empathogenic effects are "a little weaker and less sharp than with MDMA." Pharmacologically you can argue this makes sense: MDMA has unusually efficient SERT reversal kinetics coupled with VMAT2 disruption, which gives a fast-dumping release curve. AMT's multi-modal action (slower release through SERT, direct 5-HT2A agonism, partial MAO-A inhibition) layers three different time-courses on each other, which tends to smooth rather than peak.
And your therapeutic framing — that a fast-but-smooth onset would be clinically valuable — is actually where the field is actively going. This is exactly the rationale for the MDMA prodrug programs. Lysine-MDMA and lysine-MDA are derivatives of MDMA and MDA, respectively, currently undergoing their first human clinical trial in healthy subjects. Similar to the FDA-approved medication lisdexamfetamine—which attaches the amino acid lysine to dextroamphetamine to create a prodrug with a more gradual onset of action—lysine is being similarly attached to MDMA and MDA to potentially achieve a similar effect. So the therapeutic-development community is explicitly treating MDMA's sharp come-up as a liability worth engineering out — your intuition that a smoother curve would be clinically preferable is literally what Phase 1 programs are being built around. The Vyvanse analogy is the right one.
Where I'd still hold some caution isn't about the smoothness claim but about the other things smoked AMT drags along with that profile. The smooth, gradual empathogenic rise is real, but it's bundled with 5-HT2A agonism that's going to be intensity-scaled with the Cmax of a smoked route, the MAOI interaction surface doesn't go away, and the AUC-driven serotonergic neurotoxicity concern from αET-class compounds is arguablyworse at higher Cmax peaks. So "smoother experiential profile" is a legitimate point in AMT's favor that I shouldn't have brushed aside, but it's not sufficient to close the therapeutic case — it's one axis of several, and the field's actual answer has been to keep MDMA's clean pharmacology and engineer the onset curve via prodrug chemistry rather than adopt a messier pharmacology that happens to have a nicer curve.
