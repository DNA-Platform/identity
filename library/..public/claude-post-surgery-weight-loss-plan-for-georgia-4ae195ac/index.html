Here's the plan. One number I'm missing is your current weight and height — an aggressive 1 kg/week means a ~1,000 kcal/day deficit, which is only safe and sustainable if your maintenance is high enough. For a 43-year-old man around 90 kg, maintenance is roughly 2,400–2,600 kcal, putting your target at ~1,500–1,600 kcal/day. Tell me your stats and I'll pin it exactly.
The regimented structure (Tbilisi-friendly)
Same meals daily, weighed on a kitchen scale — repetition is what makes it regimented and trackable:
Breakfast (~350 kcal): 3 eggs + cucumber/tomato, or matsoni (2%) with a small handful of walnuts. Eggs and matsoni are cheap and everywhere in Georgia.
Lunch (~500 kcal): 150–200 g chicken breast or pork tenderloin, 150 g cooked buckwheat or rice, big salad with 1 tsp oil. Carrefour, Agrohub, and Goodwill all carry everything here; buckwheat (гречка) is abundant.
Dinner (~450 kcal): Fish (trout is good and local), or lobio without the bread, plus vegetables. Skip khachapuri, shoti, and khinkali during the cut — they're the calorie bombs in the Georgian diet.
Snack (~200 kcal): Fruit, or tvorog/khacho (curd cheese) — high protein, cheap, ideal for a cut.
Protein target: ~1.6 g/kg bodyweight to preserve muscle in a deficit. Weigh yourself daily, same time, track the 7-day average — that's your real signal.
Gym, foot-protective (weight-bearing OK, no impact)
Cardio: Stationary bike and rowing machine are your two best tools — real calorie burn, zero impact. Swimming if your surgeon clears the incision for water.
Strength: Seated machines (chest press, rows, lat pulldown, leg extension, seated leg curl), plus dumbbell work from a bench. Skip squats, lunges, treadmill, and jumping until cleared.
Three strength sessions + 3–4 bike/row sessions of 30–45 min per week pairs well with the deficit.
A trainer is worth it once your surgeon signs off — bring the surgical notes so they program around the foot rather than guessing.

Yes — what you're describing is essentially the Warrior Diet / OMAD family of intermittent fasting: fast through the day, eat everything in a late window. For you it's actually a smart match, because quetiapine hunger hits hardest in the evening and night — so putting all 1,500 kcal where the hunger lives, instead of spreading thin portions across a day of constant hunger, tends to work much better in practice.
Your numbers at 5'7", 180 lbs: maintenance roughly 2,300–2,500 with light activity, so 1,500 gives you a solid deficit — closer to 0.8–0.9 kg/week than a full 1.0, which is honestly the better version of "aggressive." Protein target ~130 g/day.
Structure: 18:6-ish, evening-loaded
Daytime: black coffee, tea, water, sparkling water. Bouillon/broth if you need something. Zero-calorie only.
~6 PM — Main meal (~1,000 kcal): This is where the real food goes. 250 g chicken, pork, or trout; buckwheat or rice; vegetables with oil; matsoni. Big, satisfying, high protein.
~9:30–10 PM — Pre-bed meal (~500 kcal): Built for the quetiapine hunger. Best option: tvorog/khacho (200–250 g) with walnuts and a little honey or fruit — casein-heavy dairy digests slowly, so it keeps blunting hunger into the night. Eggs also work here.
The pre-bed slot being planned is the key move — it stops medication hunger from turning into an unplanned raid, because the answer to "I'm starving" is "yes, and your 500-calorie meal is scheduled in 40 minutes."
Two practical notes: the first week of fasting until dinner is the hard one, then ghrelin adapts to the schedule and daytime hunger genuinely quiets down. And fasted low-intensity cardio (the bike/row sessions) slots nicely into late afternoon, right before your window opens — you finish the workout hungry and walk straight into your biggest meal.

Interview questions that will separate a real trainer from a rep-counter:
"I'm recovering from foot surgery — how would you modify a program around a no-impact restriction?" A good answer names specific substitutions (seated/machine work, bike, rowing, pool) without hesitation. A bad answer is "we'll go easy."
"Have you trained clients through injury recovery before? What did that look like?"
"I eat one main meal at 6 PM plus a small meal before bed, at 1,500 kcal — can you work with that structure?" You want someone who adapts to your protocol, not someone who insists on 6 meals a day.
"How do you track progress?" Look for: logged lifts, weekly weight averages, measurements — not vibes.
Ask about English (or Russian) fluency up front — in Saburtalo most trainers speak Russian, many speak English, but confirm before paying for a package.
