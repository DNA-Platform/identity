The Russia block: what it actually was, and where it stands
You're right that it's been lifted, but it helps to know there were three separate things stacked on top of each other, because only one of them applied to Russians.
1. The 75-country immigrant visa pause — this was the one that hit you, and it's dead. On January 14, 2026 the State Department issued a cable (26 STATE 3740) directing consulates to refuse immigrant visas under INA §221(g) for nationals of 75 countries, including Russia, on "public charge" grounds, effective January 21. Interviews could still happen; visas just couldn't be issued. On August 21, 2026, Judge Jeannette A. Vargas (S.D.N.Y.) vacated it in Catholic Legal Immigration Network, Inc. v. Rubio, No. 26-CV-00858, holding the Secretary exceeded his statutory authority — the INA bars nationality-based discrimination in immigrant visa issuance and requires individualized adjudication. State has since updated its own public guidance confirming the January pause is no longer in effect. The government filed a notice of appeal to the Second Circuit on September 10, so this isn't permanently settled, but the vacatur stands unless stayed.
2. The 39-country travel ban (Proclamations 10949 and 10998). Russia is not on either the full or partial suspension list. This one is still in force and is legally separate, but it doesn't touch you.
3. A worldwide interview-scheduling freeze. On August 25, 2026 State paused immigrant visa interview scheduling at every post worldwide for consular officer training on new public-charge standards. There's still no formal government-wide restart announcement, only post-by-post resumption. As of mid-September, embassies have been instructed to resume processing for 75-country nationals and a federal judge in N.D. Cal. ordered State to file thrice-weekly status reports on resumption through September 30. This is now your main practical obstacle, not a Russia-specific one.
What Ana and the children are entitled to
Ana is an immediate relative as the spouse of a US citizen. No annual cap, no visa bulletin, no queue for a number. IR-1 if you've been married 2+ years when she's admitted; CR-1 (2-year conditional card, then Form I-751) if less.
The children qualify as your stepchildren under INA §101(b)(1)(B) — and this is the one hard gate — only if you married their mother before each child's 18th birthday. The rule is strict, there's no waiver, and a marriage one day after the 18th birthday fails. If they were under 18 when you married in Georgia, each is an immediate relative (IR-2) so long as they're unmarried and under 21. You do not need to adopt them.
One thing worth knowing now: a stepchild is a "child" for green card purposes but not for citizenship purposes — a stepchild doesn't acquire citizenship through the US citizen stepparent unless adopted. So the kids get green cards through you, but their route to citizenship runs through Ana naturalizing (3 years as an LPR married to you), after which they'd acquire automatically under INA §320 if still under 18 and living with her.
The mechanics
There's no separate "notifying the US" of your marriage. No registration requirement exists. The I-130is the notification — you submit the Georgian marriage certificate with it.
File three separate Form I-130s — one for Ana, one for each child. Stepchildren don't ride along as derivatives on a US citizen's spousal petition. $625 online, $675 paper.
NVC stage — DS-261, fees ($120 affidavit of support review, $325 per immigrant visa applicant), DS-260 for each applicant, civil documents, I-864.
Interview at Embassy Tbilisi. Tbilisi processes immigrant visas for residents of Georgia, and post assignment follows residence, not nationality — so you're not sent to Warsaw or Astana the way Russians resident in Russia are for nonimmigrant visas. This is a meaningful advantage.
Medical exam with a Tbilisi panel physician, then visa issuance, then admission as LPRs.
Two things likely to bite you
Domicile. This is the one I'd flag hardest. An I-864 sponsor must have a domicile in the United States. Unlike an income shortfall, a joint sponsor cannot cure it. If you're living abroad you must show either that the absence is temporary and you've maintained US domicile, or that you intend in good faith to establish domicile no later than the date they're admitted, with evidence supplied to NVC or brought to the interview. A US employer relationship, US tax filings, property, bank accounts, a lease, and a written declaration of intent all go to this. Worth assembling before you file, not after.
The children's other parent. US law doesn't require his consent for the I-130 or the visa. But consular officers do look at custody documentation for a minor immigrating with one parent, and the Russian-side exit question is entirely separate from the American one. That's a known constraint in your situation rather than something new here.
Timeline
Honest answer: wide and currently unpredictable.
I-130 adjudication: published estimates diverge badly right now. Around 10–15 months at most service centers per one reading of the USCIS tool; trackers using consular-processing IR data put it at 17–18 months and rising, citing a USCIS processing stall since mid-March 2026; the tool's outer range for spouses of US citizens runs to 80 months. Check the USCIS Processing Times tool yourself against the specific service center once you have a receipt number.
NVC: typically 2–6 months if documents are clean.
Interview scheduling at Tbilisi: currently the wild card, because of the worldwide freeze.
Planning range: roughly 18 months to 3 years from filing to admission, with the front half being the most predictable part. Filing sooner is strictly better — the I-130 clock runs regardless of what the consular backlog does.
I'm not a lawyer and this is a fast-moving area where three separate legal tracks are in active litigation. Given the domicile question and the custody documentation, this is worth an hour with an immigration attorney who does consular processing before you file.

Domicile — the timing problem is already solved
You don't need to know the date. The Foreign Affairs Manual anticipates exactly your situation. From 9 FAM 601.14-7(a)(3), a petitioner living abroad qualifies as a sponsor if he satisfies the officer that he has taken steps to establish a domicile, and that he has either already taken up physical residence in the USor will do so concurrently with the applicant. The FAM says it explicitly: the sponsor does not have to precede the applicant, but if he doesn't, he must at least arrive concurrently. And: there is no time frame for establishing residence — the officer just has to be satisfied it will happen no later than the applicant's immigration.
So the arrangement you're making isn't "move by date X." It's "accumulate a continuous, dated evidentiary record of intent, and be on the plane with them." That's a much easier thing to do over an 18-month-to-3-year horizon.
The FAM lists the officer's own evidence menu, which is effectively your checklist:
Opening a US bank account
Transferring funds to the US
Making investments in the US
Seeking employment in the US
Registering children in US schools
Applying for a Social Security number
Voting in local, state, or federal elections
The FAM also requires you to establish an address — a house, an apartment, or documented arrangements to stay with family or friends. That last one matters: a written arrangement with a relative is sufficient. You don't need a lease running for three years.
Two things worth knowing:
The shortcut probably doesn't apply to you. Under 9 FAM 601.14-7(b), a US citizen abroad is deemed domiciled if his employment meets INA §319(b)(1) — US government, a US institution of research recognized by DHS, a US firm engaged in foreign trade and commerce, a public international organization, or a religious denomination. The research-institution list is at 8 CFR 316.20, and it's a fossil: last substantively set at 32 FR 9634 (1967), listing things like the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission and Radio Liberty. Baylor isn't on it, and neither is any modern academic medical center. USCIS has acknowledged the list is outdated. Don't spend time on this route; it's faster to build the intent record.
Two other numbers. Your household size for the I-864 is four (you, Ana, both children), so the 125% poverty-guideline threshold is set against that. And the FAM is explicit at 601.14-3(b)(1)(d)(i) that an I-864 is required for an IR-2 based on a stepparent relationship — the children don't get the Child Citizenship Act exemption that biological children of a USC do. That's the same stepchild/citizenship gap I flagged before, showing up again.
Nothing about domicile goes stale. What goes stale is the medical exam (must be under six months old at entry) and police certificates. Those you time to the interview; domicile you build starting now.
The father's consent — there is no slot for it
This is the part where your model needs adjusting, and I think the adjustment helps you.
On the US side, it never gets injected. No US form asks for it. The I-130 doesn't require it, the DS-260 has no field for it, and no statute or regulation conditions an IR-2 on the non-petitioning biological parent's consent. It goes into the CEAC document upload and the interview packet as supporting custody documentation, where it carries discretionary weight with a consular officer looking at a minor emigrating with one parent. Useful, not required. So there's no deadline you're racing and no moment where its absence stops the case.
Where it actually has legal force is elsewhere, and this is where it's worth real money to you:
Russian exit control. Under Art. 20 of 114-FZ, a minor exits Russia with one legal representative unless the other has filed a заявление о несогласии. Under MVD Order No. 651 (31 Aug 2021, as amended 2 Apr 2024), the parent who filed it can withdraw it out of court — and by the same mechanism, nothing stops him filing a fresh one afterward. That's precisely the reneging risk you named, and it's structural, not a matter of his character.
The Hague channel — and here the geography matters. Per travel.state.gov, Russia acceded to the 1980 Abduction Convention in 2011 but the US never accepted the accession, so the Convention is not in force between the US and Russia. Georgia, however,is a US treaty partner. Since the children's habitual residence is Georgia, the live channel if he later objected would be a Hague return petition filed in US federal court on the Georgia–US relationship. A clear, documented written consent to relocation is the Article 13(a) consent-and-acquiescence defense. That is the single place where a signed piece of paper does the most work for you.
What the instrument should be
Weakest to strongest:
Нотариальное согласие на выезд. Weakest. It's built for trips — it has country fields and date fields, and it says nothing about relocation. Better than nothing, but don't mistake it for the thing.
Notarial agreement on the children's place of residence (соглашение об определении места жительства детей), with an express, open-ended consent to permanent relocation to the United States for residence and education. Both children named in full, no end date, apostilled — Russia and the US are both Apostille Convention parties — with a certified English translation.
A court act. A мировое соглашение approved by the court, or a determination of место жительства carrying relocation language, with the withdrawal of the exit objection recorded in it.
Your instinct is right but points the opposite way from what you said. You're worried a court agreement is weaker because he might agree in court and then not perform. That's only true if the order requires a future act by him — signing something later, appearing somewhere later. The fix isn't to bypass the court for a private notarial document he could equally walk back. It's to make the court order self-executing: the order itself is the permission, nothing further required from him. A judicial act isn't a unilateral declaration he can revoke; a notarial consent much more nearly is.
So yes — get it as early as you can. Nothing in the US process consumes it, nothing expires it, and it only gets harder to obtain as positions harden. But structure it so the paper you walk away with needs no further cooperation from him to function.
What he'll want in exchange is access terms. Whatever you agree there travels with you, since a US court will generally look at the foreign order if this is ever relitigated. Worth pricing that in before you open the negotiation rather than after.
The exact wording is what does all the work here, and it has to satisfy three different legal systems at once. This is the document to have both the Russian lawyers and a US immigration attorney read before anyone signs.

Independent contractor status doesn't get you the shortcut, but you don't need it. You're on the intent-to-establish route, which you'll clear comfortably.
Add the address. The FAM is specific that the sponsor "must establish an address (a house, an apartment, or arrangements for accommodations with family or friend)." Investments and income aren't a substitute for this one item. Documented arrangements with a relative satisfy it. Pair it with a signed declaration of intent to return, and you've covered the only part of the FAM checklist your list doesn't already hit.

Money — income first, assets only if income falls short
Your instinct is right, but the order matters and it's better than you're assuming.
Investment returns are income, not just assets. Per 9 FAM 601.14-6(a)(2), income means total unadjusted income as shown on the tax return before deductions, and explicitly includes rent, interest, and dividends — not just salary. The current I-864 instructions (08/24/26 edition) say the same. So dividends and interest go on the income line directly. You may never reach the assets question.
SSDI counts; SSI doesn't. SSDI is not a means-tested benefit and may be included as income, documented with the award letter. SSI is means-tested — USCIS's own I-864P page lists it alongside SNAP, Medicaid, and TANF — and cannot be counted toward the income requirement. Separately, and in your favor: 9 FAM 601.14-13(e)(3)(f) says a sponsor is not required to disclose past receipt of means-tested benefits and officers may not hold a sponsor's past receipt against the affidavit. The restriction is only on counting it as income.
Your threshold is a household of four. Reported 2026 figures for the 48 contiguous states at 125% cluster at $41,250 for four, though I saw sources disagreeing — confirm on uscis.gov/i-864p before you calculate, since some pages are still showing 2025 numbers.
If income does fall short, assets substitute at 3x, not 5x. This is a real advantage for you. The normal rule is five times the shortfall. But per 9 FAM 601.14-6(d)(3), sponsors of an immediate-relative spouse, and of a child of a USC who will not acquire citizenship under §320, only need three times. Your stepchildren fall in that second category precisely because the stepchild/citizenship gap excludes them. The same rule that costs you an I-864 for the kids also cuts your asset multiplier. Assets must be in the US, readily convertible to cash within one year, and documented as to location, ownership, value, and any liens.
When do they look? The I-864 goes in at the NVC stage, after I-130 approval — not at filing. The poverty guideline that governs is the one in effect when you submit it (601.14-15(a)(6)), so your threshold locks at that date. The sufficiency call is the consular officer's. And here's the useful part: 601.14-6(b)(3)(a) says if the tax return shows income at or above the threshold, the officer must find the I-864 sufficient without requesting further evidence, and may request more "only if there is a specific reason (other than the passage of time)" to doubt it. Clear the income line and the assets never get examined.
Two cautions: verification can run to employers, financial institutions, the IRS, and SSA (601.14-13(j)), and USCIS now treats signing the I-864 as authorization to pull consumer reports. And contractor income isnet after Schedule C deductions — if you optimize deductions aggressively, the number that reaches the income line may be well below what you actually earn. Worth looking at your last return with that specifically in mind.
The travel ban — it doesn't surface, but it does something worse
Does it show up in the US process? No. There's no mechanism. The DS-260 doesn't ask, the I-130 doesn't ask, and the security checks don't query Russian border-control records. A заявление о несогласии under 114-FZ is an administrative entry in a Russian system with no international propagation — no Interpol notice, nothing in Georgian or US databases.
And as a physical obstacle it is currently inert. The Art. 21 objection operates at the Russian border. It stops exit from Russia. It does not stop a child sitting in Tbilisi from boarding a flight. The children are already out. The one dependency worth tracking: if a child's загранпаспорт expires during an 18-to-36-month process, it has to be renewed — at the Russian embassy in Tbilisi, on one legal representative's application, not by returning to Russia. Check both expiry dates now, because the passport must be valid for at least six months past the interview.
Where it actually bites is evidentiary, and this is the part I'd think hardest about. The ban is the cleanest possible documentary proof that the father objects to the children leaving. It is the exact opposite of the Article 13(a) consent-and-acquiescence defense in a Hague proceeding — and the live Hague channel here is Georgia→US, since Georgia is a US treaty partner and the children's habitual residence is Georgia. Russia's non-partner status doesn't help you on that route.
There's also Abbott v. Abbott, 560 U.S. 1 (2010), where the Supreme Court held that a ne exeat right — a right to consent before a child is taken abroad — is a "right of custody" conferring a return remedy. A filed exit objection looks a lot like that. The counter-argument is Convention Art. 3: rights of custody are determined under the law of the child's habitual residence, which is Georgia, not Russia. Whether a Russian exit objection translates into a Georgian-law custody right is a genuinely open question and exactly the thing to put to the Georgian lawyers and a US Hague practitioner.
Which reframes the negotiation. Withdrawing the ban isn't primarily about unblocking the move — that's already unblocked. It's about converting his recorded position from "objects" to "consents," and removing the single best exhibit he'd have. That may make it cheaper to buy than you'd expect, because what you're asking him to give up doesn't currently stop anything physical. But it also means the lever he still holds is the Hague route, not the ban — so the consent document has to be drafted to close that door, which is the Georgian-law and relocation-consent language, not just a withdrawal filed with the MVD.
Your custody documentation — unqualified right to determine the children's residence — is strong for the consular officer, who will likely ask for custody papers for a minor emigrating with one parent. It's helpful but not dispositive on the Hague question, which turns on the father's residual rights rather than the mother's.

Money — you're done, and don't overpay for it

Which means you should not convert to fuller employment to strengthen the domicile case — SSDI has substantial-gainful-activity limits, and you'd be trading a benefit you're already using to win an argument you've already won. Domicile is satisfied by steps and intent, not by employment volume. You have enough steps.
On Ana's income: it's treated differently from ordinary household income. Under 9 FAM 601.14-8(c)(1), a sponsored immigrant's income counts toward the sponsor's household only if she signs Form I-864A — which she can, because she has accompanying family members (the children) and is your spouse. But the officer may require evidence the income continues after immigration. Income that travels — remote, freelance, contract — documents easily. Income tied to a Georgian payer does not, because it stops at the airport. Worth sorting which category hers falls in before you count on it as gap-fill.
Housing: no relative needed. The FAM says "a house, an apartment, or arrangements for accommodations with family or friend" — a signed lease timed to the move satisfies it cleanly. Before that, documented search plus earmarked funds.
The consent you described is the wrong shape
This is the important one. You said: leave the ban off until the children are in the US, and provide consent.
A consent that expires on arrival is not consent to relocation. It's consent to a trip. For Article 13(a) purposes it's arguably worse than nothing, because it documents on the record that his consent had a terminus and then ended — which is precisely the "wrongful retention" theory rather than a defense against it.
What you need is unconditional consent to permanent relocation and residence with the mother, with no end date and no trip framing. If he'll agree to the temporary version he can usually be moved to the permanent version, because from his side the practical difference feels small. It isn't.
The enforcement structure is also the wrong shape
"He behaves, we don't sue; he stops, we sue" is a standing hostage arrangement. It never terminates, it requires his continued non-action forever, and it leaves him a permanent live reason to reopen the question — including after you're all in Texas, when reopening costs him nothing and costs you everything.
The alternative is one court-approved мировое соглашение that does all of it at once: resolves the property claim on stated terms, withdraws the exit objection, records unconditional consent to relocation, and sets access. Judicially approved, self-executing, nothing further required from him. That's the difference between a promise you have to keep enforcing and a fact.
Two cautions on drafting it. First, courts in most systems — Russian ones included — will not approve an agreement that on its face trades child-related rights for money, because the child's interests aren't the parents' to barter. Structure it as two settlements concluded in parallel, not as consideration flowing one for the other. Second, for the same reason, consent that looks bought or coerced is a weaker Article 13(a) defense in a US court later. Keep the framing at "we're resolving everything," not "consent or we sue," and keep it in the lawyers' hands.
Price the leverage before you spend it
Art. 38(7) of the Family Code applies a three-year limitation to division of former spouses' common property. But per Plenum of the Supreme Court No. 15 of 5 Nov 1998, п.19, read with GK Art. 200(1), the three years runs not from dissolution of the marriage but from the day the person knew or should have known their right was violated. So the claim may be very much alive or may be gone, depending entirely on when Ana learned he wasn't splitting.
Your negotiating position is worth exactly what that claim is worth. Have the Russian lawyers assess limitation before you build the trade on it — because if he takes advice and learns the claim is time-barred, your leverage evaporates mid-negotiation.
"He can't afford lawyers" is the plank I'd remove
This is the weakest assumption in the set, and it's specific to the Hague channel.
Under ICARA, 22 U.S.C. §9003, a left-behind parent files a return petition directly in state or federal court where the child is located. He does not need a Russian lawyer, a Georgian lawyer, or money. The State Department's Office of Children's Issues runs an attorney referral program that places left-behind parents with pro bono counsel, and US courts appoint counsel for respondents. In one reported case a father who couldn't even obtain a US visa litigated a return petition through pro bono counsel and testified by telephone from abroad.
Cost is close to the worst available predictor of whether a Hague petition gets filed. Motivation is the predictor.
And two smaller corrections in the same vein. Russia having no Hague relationship with the US is true and doesn't help — the removal would be from Georgia, which is a US treaty partner, and that's the channel. Russian courts having favored Ana entirely is genuinely good evidence, but it's the wrong kind: a Hague court doesn't decide who should have custody, it decides whether the removal breached rights of custody under the law of the habitual residence and whether he was exercising them. A Russian court's view of the merits doesn't bind a US federal court and doesn't answer that question.
None of which means the negotiation is a bad idea. It means the thing you're buying should be unconditional and judicially recorded, and you shouldn't discount the downside because you think he can't afford to act on it.

Better: one court-approved мировое соглашение doing all of it at once — resolves the property claim, withdraws the exit objection, records unconditional relocation consent, sets access terms. Art. 38(7) of the Family Code applies a three-year limitation to division of former spouses' common property. "He can't afford lawyers" is the plank I'd remove
Specific to the Hague channel, this is the weakest assumption in the set.
Under ICARA, 22 U.S.C. §9003, a left-behind parent files a return petition directly in state or federal court where the child is located. No Russian lawyer, no Georgian lawyer, no money required. Cost is close to the worst available predictor of whether a Hague petition gets filed. Motivation is the predictor.

What survives his change of mind is narrower than I've been implying, and it's this: Article 13(a) asks about his state of mind at the time of removal, not now. If he consented in writing on Monday and the children fly Tuesday, his reversal in December doesn't retroactively unconsent the departure. He can change his mind about the future. So the document isn't a leash. It's a dated fact that a later court has to reckon with. That's a much smaller claim than I was making, and it's the only one that holds.
Two clocks then do most of the remaining work:
Article 12. If a return petition is filed within a year of removal, return is ordered. After a year, return is ordered unless the child is shown to be settled in the new environment. School, housing, community, language — the settled defense gets stronger every month.
Habitual residence migrates. Once the children have genuinely made a life in the US, their habitual residence becomes the US, and the Convention stops supplying a return remedy at all, because there's no wrongful removal from somewhere they no longer habitually reside. The 1980 Hague Child Abduction Convention — but via Georgia, not Russia, and you've caught a real error in how I've been describing it.
A Hague return order returns the child to the State of habitual residence. Not to the petitioner, and not to the petitioner's country. If the children's habitual residence is Georgia, the only return order available is a return to Tbilisi. Article 19 is explicit that a return decision is not a determination on the merits of custody — it decides forum, not who gets the children. So the successful-petition scenario ends with the family back in Georgia, where Ana holds unqualified residence rights, and with custody then litigated in Georgian courts. It does not end with children in Russia.
For a US court to order return to Russia, it would have to find the children habitually resident in Russia immediately before the removal, and there'd have to be a US–Russia treaty relationship. Neither holds. There is no mechanism here by which he traps them in Russia from the United States. I let that fear stand by talking about "Hague exposure" without saying where the exposure actually points. That was sloppy of me.
And your Georgian point is stronger than I treated it. Article 3 defines wrongful removal by reference to rights of custody under the law of the State of habitual residence — Georgia. So a petition doesn't turn on what Russian law gives him or on what he filed with the MVD. It turns on whether Georgian law, and the operative custody instrument, gave him a right to veto the children's relocation.
