Short version: if this was Tritrichomonas foetus, the odds that your course was pharmacologically incomplete are high — high enough that "the drug didn't work" and "the drug was never given at a working dose" can't yet be told apart. That distinction is the single most useful thing to walk into the vet with.
Here's the reasoning, and the gaps to close.
The dosing math is the crux. The established protocol is 30 mg/kg once daily for 14 days — that's the effective dose and also the upper limit cats safely tolerate; some strains need 50 mg/kg. Ronidazole at 30 mg/kg SID for 14 days is the treatment of choice, and this is the upper limit safely tolerated by cats. Underdosing or an inappropriate duration is explicitly listed as a cause of apparent treatment failure, alongside reinfection and resistance.
For a typical Bengal (4–5 kg), 30 mg/kg means roughly 120–150 mg/day. Your first six days at 66 mg were about 13–16 mg/kg. The later eight days at 100 mg were about 20–25 mg/kg — better, still under target. Only if she weighs ~3.3 kg or less did the 100 mg portion actually hit 30 mg/kg.
Why that first stretch matters: in experimental infection, 10 mg/kg produced initial improvement and then relapse in every cat, 2–20 weeks out, while 30–50 mg/kg cleared all of them for months. At 10 mg/kg, ronidazole caused initial improvement but infection relapsed in all 5 cats 2 to 20 weeks after treatment; at 30 or 50 mg/kg, 10/10 cats were negative for follow-up durations of 21 to 30 weeks. Your first six days sat squarely in the "improves but relapses" zone. So "ronidazole helped but didn't clear it" is exactly the expected outcome of the dose she got, not a puzzle.
Even at full dose it fails a meaningful fraction of the time. In a retrospective study of 104 cats, 64% had a good response while about 36% had an inadequate response or relapse shortly after treatment. A large study put the all-cause failure rate around 21%. So the baseline relapse rate is already high; underdosing stacks on top of it.
The negative PCR is weaker evidence than it looks — but not for the reason you might assume. Sensitivity is imperfect: false negatives come from formed, dried, or old stool, litter contamination, low organism load, PCR inhibitors, and intermittent shedding. Guidelines recommend pooling samples across several days, and colonic-flush samples are more sensitive than voided ones. Notably, one large study found no association between ronidazole administration and PCR results — the lack of association of ronidazole with PCR test results was an important finding. So don't lean on "the drug was masking it." A negative here is better explained by shedding/sample quality/timing, or by genuine reduction, or by it never having been T. foetus.
On your two hypotheses — they don't discriminate the way you'd hope. Ronidazole has activity against Giardia in cats too, not just T. foetus. Ronidazole has been shown efficient against giardiasis in dogs and in cats. So "ronidazole helped" is consistent with T. foetus, giardia, or both. The response doesn't tell you which.
So the realistic decision tree for the vet:
T. foetus, underdosed → improved not cleared. Best fit for the whole picture. Next step: a properly dosed 30 mg/kg × 14-day course, ideally after confirming.
Giardia (alone or co-infection), partially hit by ronidazole. Plausible, and it needs different follow-through — fenbendazole ± metronidazole, plus a Giardia-specific antigen test and environmental decontamination.
Resistant strain. Possible but premature. Resistance is a diagnosis after an adequate course fails — and hers wasn't adequate. Don't let anyone jump here yet.
What to bring / pin down:
Her exact current weight. Tell me and I'll give you the precise mg/kg she actually got versus the 30 mg/kg target — that turns this from a range into a number.
Confirm before concluding. Ask for a pooled multi-day fecal qPCR, ideally a colonic-flush sample, and specifically a qPCR with an internal amplification control (it flags inhibitors that cause false negatives). Add a separate Giardia antigen test, since ronidazole muddies that read.
When was the PCR taken, on what stool? A negative on a formed stool during/just after treatment is weak. Standard is retesting ~2 weeks after the last dose.
Full-dose plan and the "sensitive" question. Find out what her sensitivity was actually based on — did she show neuro signs (lethargy, ataxia, tremors, inappetence), or was the reduced dose precautionary? Those signs are reversible on stopping. If it was just caution, under-dosing again would repeat the trap.
Reinfection. Other cats in the house? Environment, litter, quarantine — reinfection is a named failure cause.
Give me her weight and I'll tighten point 1.
This touches on medical decision-making for a living animal under real stakes — everything above is to prepare you to reason with the vet, not to replace the exam and their diagnostics.

Understood — dropping the framing. Peer to peer, here are the numbers and where your observation sits in them.
First-course failure statistics, at the correct dose (30 mg/kg SID × 14d):
Even properly dosed, the first course does not fully work in a substantial minority. The retrospective data most people cite: of 45 cats completing ronidazole, 29 (64%) showed a good clinical response and 16 (36%) had partial or no improvement, or relapsed shortly after stopping. The largest cohort (1717 cats) reported a cleaner all-cause figure: a 21% all-potential-causes failure rate in cats with preconfirmed infection. So your baseline "first course doesn't fully clear it" rate, at target dose, is roughly 20–36%.
But hers wasn't at target dose, and that changes the prior sharply. The controlled dose-response is the datum that matters for your case: at 10 mg/kg, ronidazole caused initial improvement but infection relapsed in all 5 cats 2 to 20 weeks after treatment; at 30 or 50 mg/kg, 10/10 cats stayed negative for 21 to 30 weeks. Her first six days (~13–16 mg/kg if she's 4–5 kg) sit in the low arm of that curve — the arm where clinical improvement followed by incomplete clearance was the rule, 5/5, not the exception. So the probability her first course was parasitologically incomplete isn't in the 20–36% band; it's high, because she got a dose the literature associates with exactly the partial-then-persistent pattern you're describing.
Two things the negative PCR does not do here: it doesn't lower that probability (individual-animal sensitivity is imperfect — the bovine regulatory single-PCR analog runs ~70–80%, and feline false negatives come from formed/dried/litter-contaminated stool, low load, inhibitors, intermittent shedding), and it isn't explained by "ronidazole suppressed the test" — the large cohort found no association between ronidazole administration and PCR results. Your original call to treat through a negative PCR on false-negative grounds was sound, and the partial response is corroborating evidence, not something the negative test overrides.
Your thesis — does "loose but continent" fit a partial response? Yes, and here's the mechanism so you can defend it precisely. T. foetus produces neutrophilic/lymphoplasmacytic colitis of the distal ileum and colon; the clinical signature is large-bowel — frequent small-volume stools, urgency, incontinence, mucus. Reducing organism burden reduces the colitis, and urgency/continence recover before stool consistency does, because mucosal healing lags the drop in inflammatory drive. So "was uncontrollable, now loose but she's not soiling, holding since mid-treatment" is a coherent partial-response curve: burden down, continence restored, consistency still catching up.
The honest caveat, which you'll want anyway because the vet may raise it: "still loose" alone can't distinguish (a) residual T. foetus, from (b) post-colitis mucosal recovery lag after clearance, from (c) a second process — dysbiosis, diet, or giardia (ronidazole hits giardia too, so the response isn't pathogen-specific). The continence gain is a real graded outcome and it's positive. Consistency is the ambiguous variable. The only thing that resolves the ambiguity is a correctly timed qPCR — pooled multi-day or colonic-flush, ~2 weeks after a full-dose course. Positive → residual infection; negative with persistent loose stool → look at colitis/other causes.
The second cat. This is the strongest structural argument for retreating both, and it's independent of her PCR. Transmission is fecal-oral via shared litter, bowls, and grooming, and asymptomatic carriers can shed the organism and contribute to reinfection; cats sharing an environment with an infected cat should also be treated to ensure full eradication. The general rule is not to treat an isolated asymptomatic cat (narrow margin, resistance selection) — but that rule inverts when there's a symptomatic in-contact cat, because an untreated carrier re-seeds the treated one. That's your exact situation. An untreated partner is a plausible route to "relapse" that's actually reinfection, and reinfection is a named cause of apparent failure. So the reinfection-control logic is: treat both concurrently in the same window, full dose, plus environmental decontamination (daily litter-tray bleaching/drying; the organism survives in moist stool for days). Ideally PCR the partner too, but by your own false-negative reasoning, a negative on the asymptomatic cat doesn't rule him out, and concurrent empiric treatment is defensible.
If the vet pivots away from T. foetus on the strength of the negative PCR, the clean scientific rebuttal is a stack, not one point: (1) the PCR's individual-animal false-negative rate is non-trivial and isn't lowered by the drug; (2) a documented clinical response to a pathogen-directed nitroimidazole is positive evidence that shouldn't be discarded on one insensitive negative; (3) the dose delivered was subtherapeutic, so incomplete resolution is the predicted outcome and argues for completing an adequate course, not against the diagnosis; and critically (4) "resistant strain" is not yet on the table — resistance is diagnosed after anadequate protocol fails, and hers wasn't adequate. The defensible next step is a full 30 mg/kg × 14d course in both cats + decontamination + a correctly timed confirmatory qPCR, rather than reopening the differential.
