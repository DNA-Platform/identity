Pharmacological advantage: Secnidazole has a much longer half-life (~17-29 hours) compared to metronidazole (~6-8 hours). Interesting hypothesis, but the selection pressure doesn't actually work that way—and the reason why is instructive.
Why the "evolving to evade tests" theory doesn't hold:
For selection to favor test-evading Giardia, being detected would need to correlate with death. But the causal chain is: detection → treatment → death. The selection pressure is on surviving treatment, not surviving detection. A Giardia strain that evades the antigen test but gets killed by metronidazole has no advantage. And as your case demonstrates, empirical treatment happens anyway when clinical suspicion is high—so evading the test doesn't even reliably prevent treatment.
The actual reasons for false negatives are more mundane:
Intermittent shedding—Giardia cysts aren't released continuously. There are days when an infected animal sheds few or none, purely based on the lifecycle timing and where trophozoites are in the gut. Bad luck on sample day = negative test.
Cyst concentration thresholds—antigen tests need a minimum cyst density to trigger positive. Early infection, late infection, or mid-treatment can all produce "true infection, below detection threshold."
Sample handling—cysts degrade, antigen denatures. Time and temperature between collection and testing matter.
Where resistance IS a real concern:
You're right to worry about resistance, but it operates on the drug axis, not the detection axis. Metronidazole resistance in Giardia is genuinely increasing—some studies show 20%+ treatment failure rates in certain populations. The mechanism is usually reduced activation of the prodrug inside the parasite (nitroimidazoles require anaerobic reduction to become toxic).
That's actually another reason secnidazole can succeed where metronidazole failed—slightly different pharmacokinetics and tissue penetration can overcome marginal resistance.
