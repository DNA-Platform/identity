It's a theorem, not folklore: Liouville (1835), made rigorous by Ritt and Rosenlicht, algorithmic in Risch. And your instinct is exactly the shape of the proof: you first fix what "closed form" means as a specific algebraic closure operation, and then show the antiderivative is not in that closure. It's a relative statement, the same way "√2 is irrational" is relative to ℚ.
The setup. Work in a differential field: a field with a derivation D. Start with ℂ(x), D = d/dx. An elementary extension is a finite tower F₀ ⊂ F₁ ⊂ ... ⊂ Fₙ where each step adjoins one of three things: an algebraic element, an exponential (t with Dt = t·Du for some u already present), or a logarithm (t with Dt = Du/u). "Elementary function" means: lives in some such tower. That is the definition. Nothing else counts, by fiat.
Liouville's theorem (the part you need): if f and g are in a differential field K and f·e^g has an antiderivative in some elementary extension of K, then the antiderivative is already of the form R·e^g with R ∈ K, meaning R satisfies
R' + g'R = f.
So the whole tower collapses: adjoining exps and logs and roots can't help, the answer had to be there at the ground floor times e^g.
Applying it to e^{-x²}. K = ℂ(x), f = 1, g = -x². You need a rational R with R' − 2xR = 1. Write R = P/Q in lowest terms. If Q is nonconstant, R' has a pole of one order higher than R at any root of Q, so R' − 2xR has a pole there and can't equal 1. So Q is constant and R is a polynomial. Then −2xR has degree deg R + 1 while R' has degree deg R − 1, so the left side has degree ≥ 1 unless R = 0, which gives 0 ≠ 1. No such R. Done.
Rosenlicht's 1972 Monthly paper "Integration in finite terms" is about eight pages and contains the whole thing.
On "introducing something else." You can always do it. Define erf as the antiderivative and adjoin it; the differential field ℂ(x, e^{-x²}, erf) is perfectly good, and Risch-style algorithms work in it. The theorem says only that erf is not in the elementary tower over ℂ(x). It's the same move as π being transcendental over ℚ: you don't say π doesn't exist, you say the field operations from the starting point don't reach it, and reaching it requires a genuinely new generator.
On "prime function." Your framing is right and it has a name: Picard-Vessiot theory, or differential Galois theory. Adjoining erf to ℂ(x, e^{-x²}) is a Picard-Vessiot extension for the equation y'' + 2xy' = 0, its differential Galois group is the additive group 𝔾ₐ (one new transcendental, no algebraic relations), and elementary integrability corresponds to a restricted class of groups. So erf is "irreducible" in exactly the sense that √2 is irreducible over ℚ: it generates a nontrivial extension, and the extension has no proper subextension where the integral could hide. The analogy with primes is a little weaker, since there's no unique factorization of functions, but the analogy with field-theoretic transcendence and Galois groups is tight.

You're right, and my hedge was wrong. Uniqueness of factorization is uniqueness of the multiset of prime ideals, not of any expression. 7·7·7 and 49·7 and 343 and 300+43 are all expressions of one object; the factorization is what's left after you quotient out ordering, grouping, and units. The theorem is about what survives that quotient, not about a privileged way of writing.
Seen that way the differential algebra side is the same shape, not a weaker version of it. What's invariant about erf is not any expression for it but the extension it generates: transcendence degree one over ℂ(x, e^{-x²}), differential Galois group 𝔾ₐ. You can write the Gaussian CDF as ½(1 + erf(x/√2)), or as a Kummer confluent hypergeometric ₁F₁, or as the incomplete gamma γ(½, x²), or as a Faddeeva function. Those are 49·7 versus 7·7·7. Every one of them generates the same extension, and the Galois group doesn't see which name you used. The "prime" is the extension modulo its automorphisms, exactly as 7 is a prime modulo units.
And the analogue of "no unique way to construct the number" is present too, and it's a theorem: Liouville's result says the order in which you adjoin exponentials, logarithms, and algebraics to build a tower is irrelevant to whether the integral is reachable. Any tower that reaches it collapses to R·e^g at the ground floor. That is the differential-algebra version of your observation that grouping 7³ as 49·7 changes nothing.
So the correct statement of the intuition: an object is prime relative to a set of construction operations when the extension it generates has no proper subextension reachable by those operations. Integers under multiplication, erf under exp/log/algebraic adjunction. Same definition, same indifference to how it's written.
