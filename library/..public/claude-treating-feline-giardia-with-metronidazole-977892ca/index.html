The antibiotic is Metronidazole (Flagyl). It's a nitroimidazole that works by disrupting DNA synthesis in anaerobic organisms and protozoa like Giardia.
Standard vs. Extended Treatment Duration
For uncomplicated giardiasis, 5-7 days is typical. However, for refractory or relapsing cases like yours, 10-14 days is often necessary. The 3-day course was likely insufficient—not because it didn't work pharmacodynamically, but because it didn't achieve complete eradication. The symptomatic improvement you saw confirms the drug was effective against the pathogen; the relapse suggests residual organisms repopulated once drug concentrations dropped.
Your Clinical Reasoning is Sound
You're essentially asking: if empiric therapy produced complete resolution, what additional actionable information will the fecal test provide? This is a legitimate question. The test (likely fecal flotation, ELISA antigen test, or PCR) can:
Confirm giardia vs. other protozoa (Tritrichomonas, coccidia) or bacterial pathogens that might require different treatment
Identify co-infections requiring combination therapy
Rule out other causes if metronidazole fails again
However, if the test simply confirms giardia, the treatment remains metronidazole (or fenbendazole)—just longer duration. So your instinct to extend treatment empirically while awaiting results is reasonable, especially given the clear initial response.
Re-infection vs. Relapse
True re-infection requires environmental decontamination—litter boxes, bedding, perianal fur (giardia cysts are sticky). Without this, you're fighting a losing battle regardless of treatment duration.

The Other Cat
Yes, he needs the extended course too. If both cats share a litter box and he only got 3 days, he's likely still shedding cysts even if asymptomatic. Cats can be subclinical carriers. You'll create a ping-pong reinfection cycle between them—one clears, gets reinfected by the other, and vice versa. Treat both simultaneously for the full duration.
Environmental Decontamination Protocol
This is actually the critical piece most people underdo:
Litter box: Don't just scoop—completely dump, wash with boiling water or steam, and disinfect. Quaternary ammonium disinfectants work well. Dilute bleach (1:32) requires 10+ minutes contact time to kill cysts. Do this daily during treatment if possible, or at minimum every 2-3 days.
End-of-treatment bath: Bathe both cats on the final day of antibiotics. Giardia cysts stick to perianal fur. The cat grooms, reingests cysts, cycle continues. This step is frequently skipped and is a common cause of "treatment failure."
Bedding and surfaces: Hot water wash for fabric. Giardia cysts are susceptible to desiccation and heat, so dry environments and steam cleaning are effective.
Zoonotic Risk
Low but not zero. Cats typically carry Giardia assemblage F, which is relatively host-specific. Human giardiasis is usually assemblages A and B. Cross-species transmission is documented but uncommon. Standard hygiene—handwashing after litter box contact—is sufficient for immunocompetent adults. Ana or anyone immunocompromised should take extra precautions.

Replacing Litter Boxes
Yes, absolutely—this is the simplest solution. Plastic litter boxes develop micro-scratches that can harbor cysts even after disinfection. They're inexpensive. Throw them out at the end of treatment (not the beginning, or you'll just contaminate the new ones), buy fresh, and eliminate that variable entirely.
Floor Cleaning
Professional cleaning is unnecessary. Giardia cysts are hardy in moist environments but actually quite vulnerable to:
Desiccation: Dry floors are hostile to cysts. Mop with hot water, then let dry completely.
Heat: Steam mop is ideal if you have one
Quaternary ammonium cleaners: Most household floor cleaners work fine with adequate contact time
Focus on the area around the litter box and anywhere the cats frequent. You don't need to decontaminate your entire home—just the high-traffic feline zones.
Quarantine Question
If both cats are being treated simultaneously with the full course, strict quarantine isn't necessary—they're both shedding and both being treated. Quarantine matters more if only one is being treated or if one finishes treatment before the other.
What IS helpful: separate litter boxes during treatment so you can monitor stool quality individually and ensure the symptomatic cat is actually improving.
Timeline: Start fresh (new boxes, bathed cats, clean floors) on the final day of treatment for both cats. That's your reset point.

Osteotomy at Week 5—Flight Advice
The good news: two short flights with a layover is much better than one long flight. The layover gives you forced movement time.
During Flights
Ankle pumps: Flex and point your foot (within pain limits) every 15-20 minutes. This activates the calf muscle pump that pushes venous blood upward.
Aisle seat: Easier to extend the leg, get up if needed
Hydrate: Cabin air is dehydrating, dehydration increases clot risk
Avoid alcohol: Same reason
Loosen the boot slightly if swelling increases: Altitude causes expansion. If it feels tight mid-flight, adjust. Don't cut off circulation.
During Layover
Walk around as much as your weight-bearing status allows. This is your protection.
DVT Warning Signs to Watch For
In the days after flying:
Calf pain or tenderness (not ankle—calf)
Asymmetric swelling (one leg significantly more than the other)
Warmth or redness in the calf

"Still Hard" at Week 5 is Normal
Since no one apparently told you what to expect—here's a rough osteotomy recovery timeline:
Weeks 1-2: Worst swelling and pain, minimal weight bearing
Weeks 3-6: Where you are now. Bone is still healing. Stiffness, soreness, limited mobility. Boot-dependent.
Weeks 6-8: Often when transition out of boot begins (varies by procedure and surgeon)
Months 3-6: Progressive strengthening, physical therapy, gradual return to normal activity
Full recovery: 6-12 months for complete bone remodeling and return to baseline function
