Short version: the scholarly debate splits along exactly the lines you and Ana are drawing, and both of you are pointing at something real. Here's the map.
What "jihad" actually means
The word means "struggle" or "striving." In the Quran and hadith it covers everything from inner moral effort to armed combat. Classical jurists (9th–14th c.) developed a detailed law of war under the heading of jihad: who may declare it, rules of engagement, treatment of non-combatants, treaties. Most of that tradition treats armed jihad as a collective duty of the state, not something an individual can launch on his own. The modern usage — private groups declaring war on civilians — is a 20th-century innovation (Qutb, Azzam, bin Laden) that most traditional scholars reject as illegitimate. The "greater jihad = inner struggle" framing is a Sufi-era reading that is popular in the West but historically secondary to the legal one.
Ana's point: the texts
If you count violent verses, the Bible has more — Joshua, Judges, Samuel, the Psalms, Revelation. But the comparison isn't symmetric. The Old Testament violence is narrative: God commanded Israel to do X at a specific time. Most Christian readings treat it as history, not standing instruction. The Quran's combat verses (2:190–193, 9:5, 9:29, 8:39, etc.) are phrased as directives, and a legal tradition built rules on top of them. So the honest textual claim isn't "more violence" but "more of the violence is legislative." Muslim scholars answer that those verses are tied to specific 7th-century campaigns and are constrained by other verses ("no compulsion in religion," 2:256; "if they incline to peace, incline to it," 8:61) — the same move Christians make with Joshua. The disagreement is over which verses govern which, and Muslims have argued about that among themselves for 1,400 years.
Your point: religion in power
This is the strongest empirical argument. Christian polities with fused religious-political authority produced the Crusades, the Inquisition, the Albigensian massacre, the Thirty Years' War (roughly 8 million dead), Calvin's Geneva, the witch trials, and the conquest of the Americas with theological justification. When Christianity ran states, it looked a lot like what Ana associates with Islam. Recent examples with explicit Christian religious framing: the Lord's Resistance Army, Rwandan churches' complicity in 1994, anti-balaka militias in CAR.
On the Nazis specifically: the picture is mixed. There was a pro-Nazi "German Christian" movement and the churches largely failed to resist, but the leadership was ambivalent-to-hostile toward Christianity and the ideology was racial, not scriptural. Better like-for-like cases are the Spanish Inquisition or Calvinist/Puritan theocracies.
The historical divergence
Three structural differences that explain why the two religions look different now:
Founding conditions. Muhammad was a head of state and military commander; Islamic law and governance grew together from year one. Jesus was executed by the state, and Christianity spent 300 years as a persecuted minority before Constantine. Christianity had to build its politics later, on top of a text that assumed powerlessness; Islam's text assumes the community is governing.
The European split. After the wars of religion, Europe secularized government — a bloody, contingent process, not a feature of Christianity. The Muslim world never had an equivalent break, partly because it was being colonized during the period when it might have. So today's comparison is secular-heritage states vs. states where religious law still has standing.
The last 50 years. Saudi oil money exported Wahhabism globally from the 1970s; the US and Saudis funded jihadist networks in Afghanistan against the Soviets; the Iranian revolution made political Islam look viable; the Iraq war created the conditions for ISIS. Much of the violence a New Yorker associates with Islam is downstream of these specific events, not of the 7th century.
Where the real disagreement sits
Ana's side, at its strongest (Bernard Lewis, Ayaan Hirsi Ali, some Muslim reformers like Abdullahi an-Na'im): Islam's legal tradition makes the political fusion harder to undo than Christianity's, so "fundamentalists in power" is more available as an outcome.
Your side, at its strongest (Karen Armstrong, William Cavanaugh, most religious-studies scholars): religious violence tracks political conditions, not scripture; every tradition has the texts for it, and which texts get activated depends on who holds power and what they're fighting about.
Both positions are held by serious people. The empirical data most cited: Muslim-majority countries vary enormously (Indonesia, Senegal, Bosnia vs. Saudi Arabia, Afghanistan), which favors the political-conditions view; but polls on support for sharia as state law are high across much of the Muslim world, which favors the tradition-matters view.

1. Does the Quran command violence against any non-Muslim you encounter?
No. Nothing in the text says "when you see a non-Muslim, attack him." The combat verses are framed around war: fighting "those who fight you" (2:190), the Meccan polytheists who had broken a treaty (9:1–5), and "People of the Book" until they pay jizya (9:29 — a tax marking subordinate status, not death or conversion). Christians and Jews are explicitly permitted to keep their religion; their food and (for Muslim men) their women are lawful (5:5). Classical law codified this: non-combatants, women, children, monks, and the elderly were not to be killed; treaties were binding; and only the ruler could declare war. The verse Ana may have in mind, 9:5 ("kill the polytheists wherever you find them"), is the one modern militants lean on — but it's addressed to a specific treaty-breaking group, and the same passage says to let them go if they repent and to grant protection to any who ask for it (9:6).
2. Old Testament: comparable legislative violence?
Yes, and arguably more of it. Death by stoning is prescribed for adultery (Deut 22:22–24), blasphemy (Lev 24:16), Sabbath violation (Num 15:32–36), idolatry (Deut 17:2–5), a rebellious son (Deut 21:18–21), and a non-virgin bride (Deut 22:20–21). Leviticus 20:13 prescribes death for male same-sex intercourse. Deut 20:16–17 orders total extermination of the Canaanite peoples. These are legal directives, not narrative — structurally the same category as the Quranic combat verses, and harsher in scope.
3. New Testament on homosexuality
No death penalty, but condemnation persists. Romans 1:26–27 describes same-sex acts as "against nature" and a consequence of idolatry; 1 Corinthians 6:9–10 and 1 Timothy 1:10 list them among things that exclude from the kingdom of God. Jesus himself says nothing on the subject. So the New Testament shifts from execution to moral condemnation and exclusion — which is roughly what medieval Christian states converted back into secular capital punishment for sodomy.
4. Is there a parallel "this is outdated" mechanism?
Both traditions have one, but they run in opposite directions.
Christianity: the standard reading is that the Mosaic law was fulfilled or superseded by Christ. Acts 15 released Gentile converts from most of it; Paul argues believers are "not under the law" (Rom 6:14; Gal 3). This lets Christians drop the stoning laws wholesale while keeping the moral condemnations of the New Testament — which is why Leviticus 20:13 is dead letter but Romans 1 still gets cited.
Islam: the doctrine is naskh (abrogation) — a later verse can cancel an earlier one. The problem for Ana's side of the argument: the militant reading uses naskh to claim the late Medinan "sword verse" abrogates the earlier tolerant verses like 2:256 and 109:6. The mainstream reading limits naskh narrowly and treats the combat verses as situationally bound. So Islam does have an "outdated" mechanism, but the text-internal ordering makes it usable by hardliners in a way Christian supersessionism isn't. Modern reformers (Fazlur Rahman, an-Na'im) propose the reverse — that the universal Meccan verses should govern and the Medinan ones are historical — which is essentially the Christian move applied to the Quran, but it remains a minority position.
One factual footnote for the stoning comparison: the Quran does not prescribe stoning for adultery (it prescribes 100 lashes, 24:2). Stoning entered Islamic law through hadith, not the Quran — which is why some reformers reject it.

Is the New Testament less violent than the Quran?
Yes, by any straightforward reading. Jesus's teaching is close to pacifist: turn the other cheek, love your enemies, put away the sword, "my kingdom is not of this world." He never leads a fight, never legislates punishment, and dies without resisting. The exceptions Ana's critics would cite are thin: "I came not to bring peace but a sword" (Matt 10:34) is about family division over faith; "buy a sword" (Luke 22:36) is followed by "two is enough," which most read as symbolic; and Revelation is saturated with apocalyptic violence, but it's God doing it at the end of time, not instruction to believers. Paul tells Christians to submit to rulers who "bear the sword" (Rom 13) — he endorses state violence but doesn't tell believers to wield it.
So on a founder-to-founder or text-to-text comparison restricted to the New Testament, Ana's position holds. The Quran contains rules of war; the New Testament contains none, because its community had no state and no army.
What Muhammad said and did about violence
The honest answer is that Muhammad was a statesman and military commander in a way Jesus never was, and the record reflects that.
What he did: Led or authorized roughly 60–80 military expeditions from Medina. Fought at Badr, Uhud, and the Trench. Expelled two Jewish tribes and, after the Trench, had the men of the Banu Qurayza executed (traditional sources say 600–900) for treaty violation during the siege — the most cited episode by critics. Ordered the killing of several individuals who had mocked or incited against him (Ka'b ibn al-Ashraf; some accounts, contested by Muslim scholars, add Asma bint Marwan). Conquered Mecca in 630 with almost no bloodshed and declared a general amnesty for the people who had tried to kill him for 20 years.
What he said: Both sides can quote him. The hadith hardliners rely on: "I have been commanded to fight people until they testify there is no god but God" (Bukhari) — mainstream scholars restrict it to the Arabian polytheists, but it's there. On the other side: the rules of engagement he set — don't kill women, children, the old, monks in their cells, or those who surrender; don't mutilate; don't burn crops or cut fruit trees. "The best jihad is a word of truth before a tyrant." "God is merciful to those who are merciful." The treaty of Hudaybiyya, where he accepted humiliating terms to avoid a battle. The Constitution of Medina, giving Jews equal standing in the community.
The fair summary
Jesus taught nonviolence and was killed; Muhammad taught regulated violence and won. That asymmetry is real, and it's the strongest version of Ana's argument. Your counter is also real: Christian history didn't follow Jesus. Within three centuries of Constantine, Christians were doing everything Muhammad did and more, without any textual license for it — which suggests the founder's teaching constrains a religion's politics less than one might hope. The Islamic tradition, meanwhile, spent most of its history with jihad as a rarely-invoked state prerogative under elaborate legal limits, while Christian Europe fought wars of religion that dwarfed anything in the early caliphates.
So the two of you are answering different questions. Ana: which scripture and founder are more violent? — the Quran and Muhammad, clearly. You: which religion produces more violence when it governs? — history says the difference is small, and depends on the century.

Yes, that's a fair framing, and it's one serious scholars use. The like-for-like comparison to the Quran isn't the Gospels; it's the Torah, because both are founding law codes for a people that governs itself. Compared to Deuteronomy, the Quran is milder — no stoning, no commanded exterminations, treaty obligations and protected status for other monotheists. Compared to the Sermon on the Mount, it's obviously harsher, but that's comparing a constitution to an ethical philosophy, as you say.
Same logic applies to founders. Jesus never had to decide what to do with a tribe that broke a treaty during a siege. Muhammad did. The closer analogue to Muhammad is Moses, or Joshua, or Constantine — or, in Christian terms, the tradition's later statesmen: Charlemagne, who gave the Saxons the choice of baptism or death; Innocent III, who preached the Albigensian crusade; Calvin's Geneva, which burned Servetus. Once Christianity had an army to command, its leaders produced a body of just-war theory (Augustine, Aquinas) that reads much like classical jihad law: legitimate authority, just cause, proportionality, protection of non-combatants. The two traditions converged once they had the same job.
So the fair comparison is: Islamic law vs. canon law and Christian just-war doctrine; the early caliphate vs. Byzantium or Carolingian Francia; a Muslim state vs. a Christian state. On that comparison the differences are real but small, and mostly track local politics.
The one place Ana's side would push back: it matters where the violence sits. In Christianity, the state's army has to be justified in spite of the founding text; in Islam, it's justified by it. That means a Christian reformer can always appeal to the founder against the state, and a Muslim reformer has a harder time — the founder was the state. Whether that difference is decisive or marginal is exactly what the two of you are disagreeing about, and it's a live question in the scholarship, not a settled one.

Here's the record. Every time Christianity got an army, the doctrine to use it followed within a generation, and it converged on something structurally like classical jihad.
Rome, 4th–5th c. Constantine legalized Christianity in 313; by 380 it was the state religion; by 392 paganism was banned. Eusebius wrote the theology of the Christian emperor as God's earthly counterpart. Augustine, facing the Donatist schism, invented the just-war framework and the doctrine of religious coercion: reading "compel them to come in" (Luke 14:23) as license for the state to force heretics back into the church. That argument justified persecution for the next 1,200 years.
Byzantium — the counterexample. Basil of Caesarea ruled that soldiers who killed in war should abstain from communion for three years. In the 960s, Emperor Nikephoros Phokas asked the Church to declare soldiers who died fighting Muslims martyrs — explicitly a jihad-equivalent — and the patriarch refused. Eastern Christianity in power never produced a holy-war doctrine. This is the strongest case on Ana's side: a Christian state that declined the option.
Frankish empire, 8th c. Charlemagne's Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae (c. 785): death penalty for refusing baptism, eating meat in Lent, cremating the dead, or hiding from the priest. 4,500 Saxons beheaded at Verden in one day. Baptism or death, by law.
Crusades, 11th–13th c. Urban II at Clermont (1095) offered remission of sins for fighting — the Latin church's first formal holy-war theology, and it's the closest structural match to jihad: religious authority declares it, spiritual reward for participants, martyrdom for the fallen. Bernard of Clairvaux wrote that a knight killing an infidel was committing "malicide, not homicide." Innocent III then turned the machinery inward on the Cathars (1209): "kill them all, God will know his own."
Scholastic law, 13th c. Aquinas held that heretics deserved death — worse than counterfeiters — and that apostates could be compelled by force. Innocent IV authorized torture in inquisition (1252). This is the direct analogue to the classical fiqh on apostasy that Ana would point to in Islam.
Spain, 1513. The Requerimiento: a document read aloud (in Spanish) to indigenous peoples demanding submission to the Pope and king; refusal legally justified war and enslavement. Functionally identical to the classical Islamic invitation-then-jizya-then-war sequence.
Reformation states. Luther, 1525: "smite, slay, and stab" the rebelling peasants; a prince who does so "may merit heaven." Calvin approved burning Servetus (1553); Beza wrote the treatise defending execution of heretics. Puritan Massachusetts hanged Quakers.
Modern. Rwanda 1994 — Catholic and Anglican clergy participated in genocide; the Lord's Resistance Army; Christian nationalist militias in CAR.
What this shows
Once in power, Latin Christianity produced: coerced conversion by law, heresy and apostasy as capital crimes, holy war with spiritual reward, and religiously-mandated conquest. That's the full package Ana attributes to Islam's texts, generated from texts that contain none of it. The reformers had to work around the Gospels to get there — which supports your point that the founder's teaching isn't what determines the outcome.
Ana's best rejoinder from this same record is Byzantium: an entire Christian civilization in power for a millennium that never developed holy-war doctrine, and the Western church took 700 years after Constantine to do so. Islamic law had it within a century. So the difference isn't whether the doctrine appears, but how easily and how fast — and that's a real difference, even if it's smaller than the popular view suggests.

Who the Arabs were before Islam
Muhammad was born around 570 in Mecca. Arabia at that time was religiously mixed:
Polytheists — the majority, including Muhammad's own tribe, Quraysh. Mecca's Kaaba housed hundreds of idols; the chief ones were Hubal and the three goddesses al-Lat, al-Uzza, and Manat. "Allah" already existed as the name of a high creator god above them (Muhammad's father was named Abdullah, "servant of Allah"). Pilgrimage to Mecca predates Islam; Muhammad kept it and stripped out the idols.
Jews — large tribes in Yathrib (later Medina), Khaybar, and Yemen, settled since at least the Roman period.
Christians — mostly on the edges: the Ghassanids (Byzantine clients, Monophysite), the Lakhmids (Persian clients, Nestorian), and Najran in the south. Mostly forms the Byzantine church considered heretical, which shaped what Muhammad heard about Christianity.
Hanifs — individual Arab monotheists who rejected idols and claimed to follow Abraham's religion without being Jewish or Christian. Muhammad's early biographers name several in Mecca. Islam's self-description is essentially this: hanif is a Quranic term of praise.
When did they become "sons of Ishmael"?
The label came from outside first. Genesis 25 names Ishmael's twelve sons, and several match known North Arabian tribes (Nebaioth, Kedar, Tema, Dumah). Josephus (1st c. AD) and Christian writers like Jerome and Sozomen (5th c.) routinely called Arabs "Ishmaelites," "Hagarenes," or "Saracens." Sozomen reports that some Arabs, on learning of their supposed descent from Ishmael, adopted Jewish-style customs — so the idea was circulating among Arabs at least a century before Muhammad.
Arab genealogists organized tribes into two branches: the southern Qahtan and the northern Adnan, with Adnan traced to Ishmael. Quraysh were Adnani. Whether that lineage was genuinely pre-Islamic or back-filled after Islam is debated; the Quran itself makes it foundational, saying Abraham and Ishmael together built the Kaaba (2:127) and that Abraham was neither Jew nor Christian but a hanif (3:67).
When Islam branched
Muhammad received his first revelation around 610 and preached in Mecca for 12 years. He didn't see himself as founding a religion; he saw himself as restoring Abraham's original one, which Jews and Christians had corrupted. The decisive break came in Medina around 624, when the direction of prayer switched from Jerusalem to Mecca and the Jewish tribes, who had refused to recognize him, became adversaries. From that point Islam is a distinct community with its own law, calendar, and pilgrimage — Abrahamic in genealogy and theology, Arab in scripture, ritual center, and language.
So the sequence is: Arab polytheists with a vague Abrahamic pedigree assigned by their Jewish and Christian neighbors → a monotheist minority who took that pedigree seriously → Muhammad, who made it the foundation of a new law and state.

Two different answers depending on whether you mean the historical origin or the concept.
The concept of "Abraham's religion"
As a named idea — a pure faith older than and separable from Jewish law — it's surprisingly late. Paul invented it. In Galatians 3 and Romans 4 he argues that Abraham was justified by faith 430 years before the law was given to Moses, so faith, not law, is the original covenant, and Gentiles can join it without becoming Jews. That's the rhetorical move that lets Christianity exist as a non-Jewish religion. Muhammad's version in the Quran (Abraham was "neither Jew nor Christian but a hanif," 3:67) is the same argument aimed at both predecessors. So "Abraham's religion" as a loose concept appears around 50 AD and is reused around 620.
Before Paul, Jews didn't think of themselves as following "Abraham's religion." They followed the Torah of Moses; Abraham was the ancestor who received the promise, not a religious founder.
The historical origin of the thing itself
Abraham is traditionally placed around 2000–1800 BC, but there's no archaeological or textual evidence for him, and the Genesis stories contain anachronisms (camels, Philistines, Chaldean Ur) that point to composition in the first millennium BC. Most scholars now think the patriarchal narratives took their present shape in the 7th–5th centuries BC, partly to give a set of disparate tribes a common ancestor.
What can be traced historically:
~1200 BC — "Israel" first appears in the Merneptah Stele as a people in Canaan. Archaeologically they look like Canaanites: same pottery, same language, same god-names (El, whom the Bible fuses with Yahweh).
~1200–1000 BC — Yahweh emerges as Israel's national god, possibly imported from the south (Midian/Edom — Egyptian texts mention "Shasu of Yhw"). He is worshiped alongside other gods; there are Israelite inscriptions to "Yahweh and his Asherah."
622 BC — Josiah's reform: centralized worship in Jerusalem, suppressed other cults, and "found" the book of Deuteronomy. This is where the Bible starts being written as a law code.
586–538 BC — Babylonian exile. Second Isaiah declares Yahweh the only god, not just Israel's god. This is where monotheism proper begins.
~450 BC — Ezra promulgates the Torah as binding law. This is Judaism as a text-based religion.
The comparison you're after
If you want the moment comparable to Jesus's lifetime — a founder with a message and no state — the best candidates are Moses (traditional) or the prophets of the 8th–6th centuries (historical). But the closer structural parallel to Muhammad is Josiah and Ezra: religious leaders who had state power and turned the tradition into law. Which lines up with the earlier point — the Quran's real peer is the Torah, and the Torah was written by people who governed.

21:08 UTC, 30 August 2026
Muhammad lived from about 570 to 632 AD. Born in Mecca, first revelation around 610 at age 40, migrated to Medina in 622 (year 1 of the Islamic calendar), conquered Mecca in 630, died in Medina in 632. By his death most of the Arabian peninsula was under his authority; within a century his successors held territory from Spain to the Indus.
For scale: he was born about 40 years after the death of Justinian and 40 years before the Byzantine–Persian war that exhausted both empires — which is a large part of why the Arab conquests moved so fast.
Your distinction is right. Arabs existed as a people for well over a millennium before him (first attested in Assyrian records, 853 BC), with their own language, poetry, and tribal structure. Muhammad gave them a religion, a scripture in their language, a state, and a shared identity that outlasted the tribes. But the religion quickly outgrew the ethnicity: within a few centuries most Muslims were Persians, Turks, Berbers, Indians, and later Southeast Asians. Today Arabs are roughly 20% of Muslims, and Indonesia is the largest Muslim country. The mismatch cuts the other way too — there are millions of Arab Christians whose churches predate Islam by centuries.
So Muhammad is the founder in a stronger sense than Jesus is for Christianity: Jesus left a movement that Paul and others turned into a religion; Muhammad delivered the scripture, set the law, built the state, and ran it. Nothing comparable exists for Judaism, whose founding is spread across Moses, the prophets, and Ezra over many centuries.
