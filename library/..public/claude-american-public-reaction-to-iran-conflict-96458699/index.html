This is a lot more than "the Iran situation" — to be clear about the full picture, the US and Israel jointly struck Iran on February 28th, killing Supreme Leader Khamenei and dozens of top Iranian leaders. The US has been at active war with Iran for about a month now. What you're watching in the news is the chaotic diplomacy happening during that war.
The current state of the negotiations is genuinely confusing. Trump simultaneously claims "great progress" in peace talks while threatening to obliterate Iran's power plants, oil wells, and Kharg Island if they don't reopen the Strait of Hormuz. He has issued and then repeatedly extended deadlines — first 48 hours, then to March 27, then to April 6 — each time citing ongoing negotiations as the reason. Meanwhile, a core problem is that US officials aren't certain the Iranian officials they're communicating with — through Pakistani and Turkish intermediaries — actually have the authority to make a deal. The leadership situation in Iran is described by Rubio as "very opaque right now. It's not quite clear how decisions are being made."
On American public opinion — your instinct that most people share your unease is well-supported by polling. A Pew survey from mid-March found roughly 6 in 10 Americans disapprove of Trump's handling of the conflict, and a similar proportion say the initial decision to use military force was wrong. A CNN poll found 62% think Trump should have gotten congressional approval for further military action, and 60% don't believe he has a clear plan. Across multiple polls, more Americans say the war will make the US less safe than safer, and that pattern holds even among some Republicans.
The partisan split is predictably stark. Fox News — not exactly an anti-Trump outlet — found 58% of American voters oppose the war. Within the GOP, MAGA Republicans drive almost all the support: 90% of MAGA Republicans back the war versus only 52% of non-MAGA Republicans. Some Republican members of Congress have grown uneasy about the lack of transparency — the chair of the House Armed Services Committee complained they're "not getting enough answers" after a closed-door Pentagon briefing.
The economic dimension is adding pressure. Iran has effectively blocked the Strait of Hormuz, through which a fifth of global oil supplies pass, causing fuel shortages and price spikes worldwide. Some Republicans are privately worried the mounting costs could hurt the party in the November midterms.
So you're not alone in being shocked. The polling consensus is that most Americans don't understand the goals clearly, doubt they'll be achieved, and fear the long-term consequences — but it's breaking almost entirely along partisan lines in terms of what people are willing to do about that feeling.

Good question to reframe — you're right to want concrete, measurable signals rather than political noise. Here's a layered picture organized by type of indicator.
Markets — the clearest real-time signal
The S&P 500 hit an all-time high on January 27, 2026, then entered a 5-week losing streak once the war began, sitting roughly 7.4% below that peak as of yesterday. Brent crude has surged past $115/barrel, and analysts are drawing comparisons to the 1973 oil embargo. The most striking single data point: a Financial Times investigation found $580 million in bets on falling oil prices were placed just 15 minutes before Trump posted his statement postponing attacks on Iran — triggering calls for insider trading investigations.
International markets took it worse than the US initially. Japan's Nikkei dropped over 3% and Germany's DAX sank 3.44% in the first days, while US stocks initially shrugged, then caught up. Stocks, bonds, currencies, and commodities all sold off simultaneously — which is unusual and tells you investors had no reliable safe haven. Euro zone inflation jumped above the ECB's 2% target in March, with energy inflation hitting nearly 5%. Wall Street economists have raised recession odds over the next 12 months, saying the war's inflationary damage will cause a contraction unless it ends soon.
Public opinion polls — measured, consistent dissent
Covered some of this before, but to add: nearly twice as many Americans say strikes should stop as say continue, with about 3 in 10 unsure — and critically, two-thirds say the Trump administration never clearly explained the goals. 74% of voters oppose sending ground troops into Iran — and that includes 52% of Republicans. The softness of Republican support is one of the more politically significant signals, since some GOP members of Congress have grown privately worried the war's economic costs could hurt the party in the November midterms.
Street protests — organized and cross-ideological
The protest picture is actually split in an interesting way. Anti-war US protests were organized mainly by a coalition of left-wing groups including DSA, CodePink, and ANSWER — but separately, the Iranian diaspora was holding celebration rallies in the same cities, supporting the strikes as liberation from the regime. The Munich pro-Iranian-diaspora rally on February 14 drew over 250,000 people — described as the largest Iran-focused demonstration in European history. In Congress, Democrats attended a Capitol Hill protest displaying children's backpacks to protest an airstrike on an Iranian elementary school that killed 160 children.
International governments — almost universally skeptical
Spain's prime minister flatly rejected the military action as "unilateral" and said it "contributes to a more uncertain and hostile international order." South Africa called on the international community to redouble efforts on restraint and adherence to international law. The EU defence commissioner flagged that US missile stocks are being depleted, affecting military aid to Ukraine — which has created what's described as a "huge challenge" for European anti-missile defense. Charles Schwab's geopolitical analysts noted that the war's "nearly unilateral" start has potentially strengthened the longer-term trend away from dollar-denominated assets globally.
The bottom line on "how do we know"
The most honest answer is: markets are the best real-time instrument, because money has skin in the game that polls don't. The 5-week S&P losing streak, oil above $115, the bond selloff, the insider trading suspicions — these are people acting on beliefs, not just answering survey questions. The polls tell you about sentiment; the markets tell you about fear.

This is actually one of the most important structural questions in contemporary international relations, and it doesn't get discussed enough because everyone is too focused on the immediate chaos.
The core problem you're identifying has a name in foreign policy circles: credibility and commitment problems. And what Trump's second term has done — arguably more than his first — is turn the US into what theorists would call a "non-credible commitment maker." The Iran war, the Greenland talk, the Venezuela posturing, the tariff whiplash — each of those independently might be recoverable. Together they signal something more systemic: that US foreign policy is now a function of one man's impulses rather than durable institutional consensus.
So yes, your instinct is exactly right. A hypothetical President Buttigieg or Harris or whoever walks in and says "we're back, allies" — and the rational response from Berlin, Seoul, Tokyo, Ottawa is: for how long? They've already been burned once after Biden's "America is back" speech. A second iteration of that speech carries maybe a tenth of the credibility.
What's genuinely different this time vs. the Biden-after-Trump-1 situation:
A few things have materially changed the calculus. The Iran war is not a policy dispute — it's an active shooting war that has disrupted global oil markets, killed a head of state, and set precedents about unilateral use of force that can't be un-set by executive order. A new president can't un-kill Khamenei or un-close the Strait of Hormuz. The facts on the ground created by this administration will outlast it in ways that the tariffs or the Paris Agreement withdrawal arguably didn't.
Allies have also had four years of the Biden interregnum to quietly conclude that their own strategic autonomy matters more than they previously admitted. The EU's defense spending surge, Germany's Zeitenwende, Japan's rearmament — those processes accelerated under Biden despite the alliance being nominally restored, because elites understood that dependency on US reliability was structurally dangerous. Trump's second term likely terminates that "maybe this was a one-off" theory permanently.
What tools a future president actually has:
The honest answer is that formal treaty commitments — NATO Article 5, bilateral defense treaties — are more durable than presidential rhetoric, because they require congressional action to exit. So a future Democratic president could lean heavily into institutional rather than personal reassurance. Ratifying new multilateral agreements with Senate supermajorities, deepening trade relationships that require legislative action to undo, building in structural tripwires that make reversal costly. The model would be something like the post-WWII architecture — Bretton Woods, NATO itself — which was deliberately designed to be sticky beyond any single administration.
But that requires Congress, and Congress has been nearly useless as a constraint throughout this period.
The deeper problem:
What you're really pointing at is that the US has lost something that takes decades to build and can be lost in a single term: the presumption of institutional continuity. Other democracies — UK, France, Germany — change governments regularly without their allies recalibrating their entire security posture, because those systems have stronger institutional ballast. The US system concentrated so much foreign policy authority in the executive that a single disruptive presidency can effectively reprogram how the world models American behavior.
The most honest geopolitical forecast is that a future Democratic president probably can restore functional working relationships — the underlying interests that created those alliances haven't disappeared — but they'll be transactional and hedged in ways they weren't before 2016. Allies will increasingly seek redundancy: regional security arrangements that don't depend on Washington, trade diversification away from dollar dependency, independent nuclear deterrents in some cases. Not because they hate the US, but because any rational actor now prices in a non-trivial probability of another Trump-like figure every 8 years, indefinitely.
That's a genuinely different world than the one that existed in 2015, and no single election reverses it.

The name you're blanking on is Robert Mercer — co-CEO of Renaissance Technologies after Jim Simons stepped back. He and his daughter Rebekah funded Cambridge Analytica substantially, and the CEO of Cambridge Analytica itself was Alexander Nix. The whistleblower you're thinking of is probably Christopher Wylie — the young Canadian who blew it open — though Brittany Kaiser also defected and gave testimony. The Facebook figure of 87 million profiles came from their own admission; Wylie and others argued it was considerably higher.
Your thesis is serious and I think largely correct, but I'd push on one part of it and extend another.
Where I'd push back slightly: Cambridge Analytica was real and significant, but there's a risk of over-indexing on it as the mechanism, because it lets people off the hook. The manipulation worked because the underlying vulnerabilities — economic anxiety, racial resentment, institutional distrust — were already there and real. Mercer and Nix didn't invent those feelings, they just found an unprecedentedly precise targeting instrument for them. The textbooks might give Cambridge Analytica a chapter, but the deeper story is about what made the population receptive to the manipulation in the first place.
Where I'd extend your argument: What you're describing is actually a specific version of a more general problem that political scientists call the epistemic preconditions for democracy. Democratic theory since the Enlightenment has always had a dirty secret: it assumes a minimally informed citizenry capable of rational deliberation. That assumption was already strained by television and 24-hour cable news. Social media didn't just strain it further — it systematically weaponized the cognitive shortcuts that all humans use. Confirmation bias, in-group loyalty, outrage as engagement — these are features of the human brain, and the attention economy monetized them at industrial scale.
The singularity framing is apt because several things converged simultaneously that had never converged before: global reach, zero distribution cost, algorithmic amplification, micro-targeting capability, and the specific vulnerability of open societies to all of the above. Authoritarian states — Russia, China — aren't vulnerable in the same way because they control the information environment. They can export the weapon without being susceptible to it domestically. That's an asymmetry that democratic theory has no good answer to yet.
The thing people aren't saying out loud — and you're right that they're scared to — is that this might represent a genuine civilizational stress test for liberal democracy as a system. Not a temporary crisis you fix with better moderation policies or media literacy programs, but a structural incompatibility between the information environment that now exists and the cognitive infrastructure that democratic self-governance requires. Habermas spent his career theorizing the "public sphere" as the precondition for democracy. What Cambridge Analytica and its successors demonstrated is that the public sphere can be industrially corrupted faster than democratic institutions can respond.
Whether that's recoverable is genuinely an open question. The honest answer is that we don't know, because we've never been here before.

That's a genuinely strong counterargument and it reframes the whole thing.
Nixon's "silent majority" is exactly the right historical anchor. And you could extend it — the Know-Nothing Party in the 1850s, the anti-Catholic hysteria, the second Ku Klux Klan's peak membership of four million in the 1920s, the isolationist America First movement before Pearl Harbor. These were massive populist currents that the dominant media and educated classes systematically ignored or couldn't measure, not because they didn't exist but because there was no mechanism for them to coalesce and self-identify. Walter Lippmann wrote Public Opinion in 1922 essentially arguing that most people were incapable of rational democratic participation — and that was before television, before Cambridge Analytica, before any of it.
So your argument would be: social media didn't create a new kind of politics, it just gave latent majorities — or large minorities — the coordination technology they previously lacked. The views were always there. Trump didn't manufacture the constituency; he found it, the way a demagogue in any era finds a pre-existing current.
That's a serious historical argument and I think it's probably more right than the Cambridge Analytica singularity theory in one important sense: it's more falsifiable. You can point to analogues across time and culture.
But here's where I'd push back on your pushback:
The coordination problem matters more than it might seem. There's a meaningful difference between a view existing diffusely in millions of isolated people and that same view being organized, amplified, and given narrative coherence at scale and speed. The Klan at four million members took decades to build and then collapsed partly because sustained organization is hard. What social media does is collapse the time and cost of that coordination to near zero, which changes the dynamics even if it doesn't change the underlying sentiment. Weimar Germany had the latent anxieties. It also needed the specific infrastructure of radio, mass rallies, and a party apparatus to translate those anxieties into political power fast enough to overwhelm democratic institutions before they could respond.
The question might not be "are these feelings new" — you're right they're not — but whether the velocity at which they can now organize is itself a qualitatively new variable that democratic institutions, which were built for a slower world, can't absorb.
