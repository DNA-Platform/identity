Fund Buckets
Bucket 1: US Volume Play (Long)
The bet is volume, not price. US shale producers and midstream companies ramp output to fill the gap — midstream earns on throughput regardless of price, making them the purest expression of this thesis.
Midstream/Pipeline: Enterprise Products Partners (EPD), Kinder Morgan (KMI), Williams (WMB) — fee-based, price-agnostic
Permian E&Ps: Diamondback (FANG), Devon (DVN), Coterra (CTRA) — these win on volume even with compressed margins
LNG Exporters: Cheniere (LNG) — Europe/Asia diversifying away from Hormuz-dependent supply
Bucket 2: Defense (Long)

Raytheon (RTX), Lockheed (LMT), L3Harris — missile defense, naval systems, ISR
Palantir (PLTR) if you want the intelligence/logistics data layer
Bucket 3: Non-Middle-East Refiners (Long)
Refiners configured for light sweet crude (US shale profile) gain input cost advantage as WTI softens while Brent stays elevated. Avoid refiners with heavy Middle East crude inputs.
Valero (VLO), Marathon Petroleum (MPC)
Bucket 4: Tanker Companies (Long, Nuanced)
Hormuz closure forces rerouting around Africa. The Cape of Good Hope route adds ~2 weeks per voyage. That means the same cargo volume requires more vessels — a ton-mile increase. This is bullish for tankers even if oil price falls.
Frontline (FRO), Nordic American Tankers (NAT), International Seaways (INSW)
Bucket 5: Oil Price Beneficiaries of Price Drop (Long)
If WTI actually falls, companies with high fuel cost exposure flip from losers to winners.
Airlines: Delta (DAL), United (UAL) — jet fuel is ~20-25% of costs
Chemical companies with oil feedstocks: LyondellBasell (LYB)
Bucket 6: Short/Underweight
Integrated majors with heavy Hormuz exposure: This is tricky because the big integrateds (Exxon, Chevron) have partially US-based production. You'd want to be short companies with concentrated Gulf/Iranian/Iraqi upstream exposure — which for US-listed stocks means being careful about names like TotalEnergies' Gulf exposure.
Pure oil price longs: Any fund or ETF (USO, OIL) that's a straight WTI long is a clean short against your thesis if you believe price drops.
Middle East shipping/logistics plays dependent on Hormuz transit
The Core Paired Trade
The most elegant expression of this thesis is probably:
Long WTI/Brent spread widening (i.e., long Brent futures, short WTI futures) + Long midstream equities + Long defense
The Brent premium captures the geopolitical disruption premium. The midstream long captures the US production ramp. Defense captures the war-continuing premium. And if WTI falls, the midstream companies absorb that because their earnings are toll-road-style, not commodity-priced.
Risk / Invalidation Conditions
The thesis breaks if:
The US can't ramp fast enough (rig count constraints, pipeline capacity bottlenecks)
Iran closes Hormuz briefly but a diplomatic resolution causes a sharp Brent reversal
Global recession hits demand and WTI falls for demand reasons, not supply ones — that's a different regime
You'd want to track US rig counts (Baker Hughes weekly) and Permian takeaway capacity utilization as real-time thesis monitors. If Permian pipelines are running at 90%+ capacity, the "US fills the gap" leg of the thesis is physically constrained.

This is where the thesis gets genuinely interesting because the positions aren't one trade — they're four distinct time horizons that happen to share the same causal trigger. You have to hold them separately in your head and monitor them with different instruments.
Horizon 1: Weeks to ~3 months — the reflexive trade
Tankers move first and fastest. The moment rerouting is confirmed, VLCC spot rates reprice within days. This is already partly in motion. The monitoring signal is the Baltic Dirty Tanker Index — specifically the VLCC component. If that's elevated and staying elevated, the rerouting is real and persistent. If it spikes and falls back, the market read is that Hormuz disruption is temporary.
This horizon also catches the WTI/Brent spread widening, which is your thesis health meter. If your scenario is playing out, that spread should be persistently wide — US benchmark soft, global benchmark elevated. You can watch this daily on any Bloomberg terminal or even free sources. If the spread narrows back to historical norms, something in the thesis is breaking down.
The risk here is sharp reversal. A diplomatic development — even a rumored one — can collapse tanker rates and the spread in 48 hours. So the position sizing on this horizon should be smaller and you need a hard exit rule, something like "if Brent/WTI spread narrows below X for 5 consecutive days, the short-term thesis is invalidated."
Horizon 2: 3 to 12 months — the earnings fingerprint
This is where the graph rewiring shows up in company-level financials. Chinese refiner margins compressing, Petrobras volume guidance increasing, Equinor contracting new European customers. These don't show up in daily prices — they show up in quarterly earnings calls and analyst guidance revisions.
The monitoring strategy here is different. You're reading earnings transcripts looking for specific language: volume guidance, crude slate changes, new customer contracts, margin commentary. Sinopec and PetroChina earnings calls are the short thesis confirmation. If their management starts talking about replacing Iranian feedstock at higher cost, you have confirmation. If they don't mention it, either the disruption isn't as severe as modeled or they're absorbing it from strategic reserves — both are important signals.
This horizon is also where US midstream volume data becomes readable. EIA publishes weekly Permian production estimates and pipeline utilization data. If Permian output is running near capacity, the volume ramp thesis is confirmed but also approaching its physical ceiling.
The risk here is that earnings are backward-looking by 90 days. The market often prices the move before the earnings confirm it, so you're either early or you're paying a premium for confirmation.
Horizon 3: 1 to 3 years — infrastructure capex commitments
This is the stickiest and most durable part of the thesis. When a country signs a 20-year LNG take-or-pay contract, or when a pipeline gets permitted and breaks ground, that is the trust realignment becoming concrete and irreversible. These decisions are being made right now in response to the current disruption, and they will generate cash flows for decades regardless of whether Hormuz reopens next month.
The monitoring here is project announcements and FID — final investment decisions. Cheniere announcing new LNG train construction, Equinor signing long-term European supply contracts, Brazil's pre-salt field development approvals. These are reported in company investor relations materials and energy industry press (S&P Global Commodity Insights, ICIS, Platts). They're not sexy real-time signals but they're extremely high conviction once they appear because capital commitments are very hard to reverse.
Oilfield services — SLB, Baker Hughes, Halliburton — are the cleanest expression of this horizon because their backlog is literally a measure of committed future drilling activity. Baker Hughes reports backlog quarterly. If it's expanding in North America, Brazil, and Norway simultaneously, the graph rewiring is being built into physical infrastructure.
Horizon 4: 3 to 10 years — structural trust realignment
This is the horizon where the NATO distancing matters most. Europe building its own supply security architecture independent of US guarantees. New bilateral relationships between consumer nations and supplier nations that bypass both traditional Western energy majors and Hormuz. This is the hardest to trade directly but it has the most persistent alpha because the market barely prices 5-year structural shifts at all.
The monitoring signal here is somewhat unusual — you're watching geopolitical relationship formation rather than financial data. Which countries sign energy partnership agreements. Which refineries get sold or restructured to accept different crude slates. Whether European utilities start signing contracts directly with Norwegian or Brazilian producers versus going through spot markets. These signals appear in government announcements and corporate strategy presentations before they appear in financial results.
The investable expression at this horizon is probably the infrastructure companies themselves — pipeline operators, LNG terminal owners, port infrastructure — rather than the producers, because producers' equity values are still heavily commodity-price-sensitive even if their volume is secured.

The second idea: Iridium Communications (IRDM)
This one is genuinely strange, which is what makes it interesting.
When tanker routes shift to the Cape of Good Hope, ships spend dramatically more time in deep ocean — far from coastal infrastructure, far from terrestrial cell networks. The only communications available are satellite. Navigation, weather routing, crew connectivity, cargo telemetry, AIS tracking — all satellite-dependent.
Iridium has the only truly polar-orbit global constellation with L-band maritime coverage. Every extra nautical mile a rerouted tanker travels is more Iridium airtime. They also hold the GMDSS maritime safety concession — legally mandated emergency communication for ships — which means this is regulatory captive revenue, not discretionary.
The spike mechanism: a sharp increase in maritime satellite data consumption from rerouting ships, coinciding with broader maritime security concerns that push fleet operators to upgrade their comms packages. Iridium sells both the hardware and the airtime. Revenue per ship per day goes up on longer voyages.
Nobody is looking at a satellite phone company in the context of a Hormuz disruption. That's the point.
Monitoring signal: Iridium subscriber count growth and average revenue per user (ARPU) in maritime segment — reported quarterly.
The third idea, and possibly the sharpest: War Risk Insurance — Beazley (BEZG.L)

What "Priced In" Actually Means
The question isn't what a stock has done. It's what the current price implies about the future — what scenario is embedded in today's valuation — and then whether reality will exceed, meet, or disappoint that implied scenario.
A stock can move 50% and still have 200% left if the event is large enough and only partially reflected. Conversely a stock can be flat and be fully priced if the market has correctly anticipated everything. The move is evidence, not proof.
The right framework has three steps:
First, reverse-engineer what the current price assumes. Work backward from market cap to implied earnings to implied revenue to implied operating conditions. That tells you what the market believes.
Second, enumerate the events that could happen and what each one implies about those operating conditions. Each event either confirms, exceeds, or contradicts the embedded assumption.
Third, estimate the earnings delta each event produces and apply the multiple change — because the multiple itself can re-rate, which is often larger than the earnings change.
