The key reframe is that the recreational goal isn't a night of sleep — it's the rush. Oral quetiapine has a Tmax around 1.5 hours (IR) and extensive first-pass metabolism via CYP3A4 (oral bioavailability ~9% of parent drug to systemic circulation as quetiapine itself, with norquetiapine forming heavily on first pass). That slow climb to Cmax produces a gradual sedation that feels therapeutic, not intoxicating. Insufflation bypasses first-pass entirely via the nasal mucosa and venous drainage, sharpens the absorption curve, and produces a much higher and earlier Cmax. So instead of drifting into sleep over an hour, you get a fast wave of H1 antihistamine + α1 blockade + M1 anticholinergic effects hitting at once. That's the reinforcing experience — a benzo/opioid-adjacent "warm blanket" rush rather than antipsychotic coverage.
You're right that duration shortens — likely a sharper peak and faster decline, though the terminal half-life (~6h) is still set by distribution and hepatic clearance once it's systemic. But duration is exactly what recreational users are trading away. They want intensity, not AUC. Norquetiapine (NET inhibitor, partial 5-HT1A agonist) also accumulates, and some users report it contributes a mild stimulant-anxiolytic flavor on the back end.
The correctional context matters too: it's widely prescribed in jails for insomnia, anxiety, and PTSD, which makes it one of the few centrally-acting drugs that's actually available. Cheeking and trading is easy, benzos and opioids aren't on formulary, and quetiapine fills the niche — hence the street names "quell," "Susie-Q," and "baby heroin." There's a reasonable literature on this going back to the mid-2000s (Pinta, Reeves, Tamburello, others) documenting both the insufflation route and IV use among inmates.

You're correcting me appropriately on the opioid/benzo framing — that was sloppy. The honest picture is closer to a turbocharged sedating antihistamine than to a recreational rush in the dopaminergic sense.
The dopamine puzzle resolves once you look at the dose. PET data (Kapur and Seeman's work in particular) shows that at 25-100mg — the typical cheeked-and-saved range — quetiapine produces minimal and transient D2 occupancy, often well under 30% and falling off within a few hours. That's why it doesn't produce the dysphoria of haloperidol or chlorpromazine. At those doses it's barely functioning as an antipsychotic; the dominant pharmacology is H1 antagonism (very high affinity, Ki ~11nM), α1 blockade, M1 antimuscarinism, and 5-HT2A. Effectively, it's diphenhydramine with extra serotonergic and adrenergic dimming — heavy sedation, some anxiolysis, occasionally a mild dissociation or "warm" feeling some users describe. So yes — mostly it just knocks them out, harder and faster than expected. In a jail, sleep itself is the scarce commodity worth trading for.
On dose compression — I want to be honest that the actual bioavailability of insufflated quetiapine hasn't been formally characterized, so I'd be inventing numbers if I gave a percent. What we can reason from first principles: oral quetiapine undergoes heavy first-pass via CYP3A4 (parent drug bioavailability is roughly an order of magnitude below absorbed dose, with norquetiapine forming en route). Quetiapine also isn't an ideal nasal candidate — pKa around 6.8, partially ionized at nasal pH, and most of a crushed tablet's volume is excipients that drip down the pharynx and get swallowed normally. So what insufflation really produces is a hybrid curve: a fraction crosses the nasal mucosa rapidly, bypasses first-pass, and gives a sharp early peak; the swallowed remainder follows the normal oral curve behind it. The compression is in onset and Cmax of the nasally absorbed portion, not in total exposure — total AUC is probably similar to or modestly higher than the same dose taken orally.

Drug-using subcultures do have a general toolkit for nasal absorption — fine, uniform particle size, head tilt to keep material in the nasal cavity rather than letting it run down the pharynx, distributing across both nostrils so neither saturates — but applied to quetiapine these run into the molecule's properties more than technique can overcome. The tablets are mostly excipients (microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, magnesium stearate), so even ground finely you're insufflating perhaps a third active drug by mass and the rest is inert powder that drips and gets swallowed anyway. Nasal mucosa surface area is only ~150 cm² and saturates fast. And quetiapine's pKa puts it partially ionized at nasal pH, so transmucosal flux is mediocre regardless of preparation. Some users report dissolving and using as drops, or freebasing-adjacent maneuvers, but none of this transforms the fundamental absorption picture.
What I think is actually going on in user reports is a layered effect: the small nasally-absorbed fraction does produce a real early plasma bump, the swallowed remainder follows the standard oral curve behind it, and on top of that you have the ritual and expectation of insufflation itself — which carries enormous weight in drug-using culture. The act of crushing, racking, and snorting a pill is part of how "real" intoxication is signaled and experienced, separate from the pharmacology. In a jail, where actual intoxicants are scarce and time is the enemy, that whole choreography probably contributes as much to the subjective effect as the drug does. There's also a selection effect in what gets reported — the inmates talking about it are the ones who found it worthwhile; plenty try and don't bother again.
So your puzzlement is well-placed. It's a case where folk pharmacology persists despite being a fairly inefficient way to use the drug, sustained by environmental scarcity, ritual significance, and the modest reality that you do get a sharper onset than swallowing the pill — even if the magnitude is smaller than the culture around it suggests.
