Great news that you're starting to bear some weight without pain - that's a positive sign at 7 weeks post-op.
Typical weight-bearing progression after calcaneal osteotomy:
The transition usually follows a graduated protocol over 4-6 weeks once your surgeon confirms adequate bone healing on x-ray:
Weeks 1-2 of weight-bearing phase:
Partial weight-bearing (PWB) at around 25-50% of body weight
Still in the CAM boot
Often using one crutch or a cane for support
Focus is on gait mechanics - heel-to-toe pattern even at reduced weight
Weeks 3-4:
Progress to weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) in the boot
Gradually wean off assistive devices
May start gentle range-of-motion exercises if not already
Weeks 5-6+:
Transition out of boot into supportive shoes (often a stiff-soled athletic shoe initially)
This is often the trickiest part - the ankle/foot feels surprisingly weak and unstable at first
Formal physical therapy typically starts here if it hasn't already
What PT usually involves:
Ankle ROM (dorsiflexion is often limited)
Calf strengthening (significant atrophy after weeks of NWB)
Proprioception/balance work
Gait training
Scar tissue mobilization
Given your wheelchair/crutches situation, ask your surgeon about whether bilateral involvement affects the typical protocol at all. The 7-week mark is right in the window where most surgeons start allowing progressive weight-bearing assuming the x-ray looks good.

That's a smart question, and the honest answer is: lack of pain is encouraging but not definitive.
Here's why:
The hardware factor: Your osteotomy is fixed with screws (and possibly a plate). That hardware can provide enough structural stability that you feel no pain even if the bone itself hasn't fully consolidated yet. Essentially, the metal is doing the load-bearing work, not the healing bone. So you can have a delayed union or even early nonunion that's asymptomatic because the fixation is holding everything in place.
What pain *would* indicate: If you had pain with weight-bearing, that would be a more reliable negative sign - suggesting either inadequate healing, hardware loosening, or hardware irritation. Pain is a better "rule-out" than lack of pain is a "rule-in."
What the x-ray is actually looking for: Bridging callus formation across the osteotomy site. At 7 weeks, your surgeon wants to see early callus - hazy bone formation spanning the cut. Full cortical consolidation takes longer (3-6 months), but visible callus is the green light for progressive loading.
So your pain-free partial weight-bearing is a good sign in that nothing is obviously wrong - no hardware failure, no gross instability. But the x-ray is genuinely necessary to confirm the biology is keeping pace with how good it feels.
Given your analytical bent, you might find it interesting to look at the x-ray with your surgeon and have them show you the callus formation (or lack thereof).

This is a logistics problem, so let's approach it systematically.
The Core Challenge
On crutches, your hands are your wheels. Anything that requires carrying, reaching down, or reaching up becomes a multi-step operation. The goal is to minimize trips and keep everything you need within a tight operational radius.
One-Crutch Mode (Your Secret Weapon)
Since you can partial weight-bear in the boot, one crutch + free hand is your high-efficiency mode. Use it for:
Short distances within the room
Any task requiring carrying something light
Bathroom trips once you've established stability
The technique: Crutch on the opposite side from your surgical foot, boot takes partial weight, free hand carries or manipulates. Move slower than you think you need to.
What Will Be Harder Than You Expect
Carrying liquids — A full cup of coffee is a two-hand or zero-hand problem. You can't crutch with a full mug.
Floor retrieval — Anything you drop is now a 30-second operation minimum. Some things become "that lives there now."
Wet bathroom floors — Crutch tips + wet tile = genuine danger. This is where most crutch injuries happen.
Fatigue — Crutching is cardio. You'll be more tired than you expect, especially in the first days of increased activity.
Doors — Hotel room doors with auto-close mechanisms are adversarial.
Getting in/out of bed — Especially if it's high or soft.
Room Preparation Game Plan
Immediately on arrival:
1) Create your "base station"
Pick a chair or bed-edge spot that becomes command central
Within arm's reach: phone, charger, water bottle, medications, snacks, remote, anything you need repeatedly
Use the desk or nightstand surface aggressively
2) Clear the floor path
Luggage against walls, not mid-room
Move any furniture that's in the crutch-path between bed → bathroom → door
Hotel rooms often have ottomans or decorative chairs that need to be shoved aside
Check for loose rugs — remove them or push them under furniture
3) Bathroom setup
Lay a bath towel on the floor in front of the sink and toilet as a non-slip surface
Put a second towel right outside the shower/tub
Move all toiletries to one accessible spot at counter height (not in your kit on the floor)
Identify grab points — towel bars are often not weight-bearing, but the doorframe and counter edge are
If there's a bath mat, make sure it's rubber-backed; if not, step ON towels, not around them
4) Water and sustenance
Fill multiple water bottles and stage them: one at base station, one in bathroom, one by door
Keep snacks that don't require prep at your base station
If you have a mini-fridge, stock it before you're tired
5) Clothing strategy
Unpack into drawers or onto a chair at waist height — nothing on the floor or in a low suitcase
Set out tomorrow's clothes tonight so morning isn't a retrieval operation
Slip-on shoes only for the good foot
6) Electronics/charging
All devices charging at base station, cords organized so nothing's on the floor to snag
Keep your phone on you or within reach at all times — falling alone in a hotel room is a real risk
Daily Operational Habits
Consolidate trips: Need something from the bathroom? Think through what else you need from that direction. Crutching back and forth is exhausting.
Pocket everything: Wear pants or shorts with pockets. Phone, key card, small items go in pockets, not in hand.
Use a small backpack or crossbody bag: For carrying multiple items between locations (e.g., lobby to room). This is a game-changer.
Sit to do things: Brushing teeth, washing face, any grooming — sit on the toilet lid or a chair rather than standing on one leg.
Ask for what you need at check-in:
A room close to the elevator
Extra towels (for floor coverage)
A chair moved if needed
Shower stool if available (some hotels have them for accessibility)
The Cat Complication
You've got two Bengals with giardia who need medication and litter management. This is a real challenge:
Litter box: needs to be somewhere you can access without bending to the floor. Can you elevate it on a low table or put it in the bathtub temporarily for easier scooping access?
Feeding: same principle — raise bowls to a surface where you can reach them
Metronidazole dosing: have syringes/meds staged at base station
Environmental decontamination: this is going to be hard. You may need to prioritize high-contact surfaces and accept some limitations until you have more mobility
Mental Prep
The first day alone will feel inefficient and frustrating. This is normal. By day two or three, you'll have optimized your patterns and it'll feel more manageable. Build in rest. Crutch fatigue is cumulative.
What's your hotel situation — do you know the room layout yet? And are the cats traveling with you or are you managing them remotely?

Bathing solutions:
Sit on the shower floor if there's no stool — dignity is overrated, safety isn't

Things people forget:
Trash: You'll generate more delivery containers than you expect. Keep a bag accessible, not in a cabinet under the desk.
Ice: If you're still elevating/icing, get a bucket filled and keep it close. Hotel ice runs on crutches are annoying.
Housekeeping: Decide if you want them in or not. If yes, coordinate timing so you're not in the way. If no, ask for extra towels and trash bags upfront.
A fall plan: Have your phone on you always. Know the front desk number. This isn't paranoia, just basic solo-recovery protocol.
The psychological part: Solo recovery in a hotel room gets monotonous. Have something to work on, watch, read, or you'll climb the walls. Sounds like you have no shortage of things demanding your attention, so this may not apply.
