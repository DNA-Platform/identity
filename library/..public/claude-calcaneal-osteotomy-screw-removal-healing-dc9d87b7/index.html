The other channels you have without seeing the wound:
Systemic signs — the most important ones. Fever, chills, night sweats, feeling generally unwell or unusually fatigued, loss of appetite. A localized wound infection with no systemic signs is a contained problem; the same wound plus fever means it's gaining ground. Take your temperature morning and evening — that's your best single instrument for the next three days.
The dressing itself. It's not a blackout curtain — it reports. Increasing strike-through (drainage soaking to the outer layer), drainage that turns from thin/pinkish to thick yellow-green, or any foul smell developing through the dressing are all meaningful. The borders. Redness, warmth, or swelling advancing past the dressing edge onto skin you can see. You can even mark the edge of any visible redness with a pen — if it crosses the line, that's objective progression.
Function. The foot getting harder to move, toes swelling or stiffening, calf pain — signs things are extending rather than settling.

What's happened so far. The channel filled with blood immediately, that hematoma organized into granulation tissue over the first week or two, and by now it's converting into dense fibrous tissue. This is the stage you're in: the tract is occupied by firm, collagen-rich material that's mechanically competent for ordinary loads — which is precisely why walking feels viable again. What happens next. Over the coming weeks that fibrous plug mineralizes and remodels from the walls inward. Woven bone — fast-forming, disorganized — creeps in first, then gets replaced by organized lamellar bone along the stress lines the heel actually experiences. The bulk of consolidation runs through roughly the 6–12 week window from removal, so you're maybe a third of the way through the meaningful mechanical timeline. Full radiographic filling of a screw tract lags behind that — on an X-ray the hole can remain visible for many months, even a year or more, without that meaning anything is wrong. The tract regains practical strength long before it disappears on imaging.
What this means for loading. Walking is exactly the right input now — cyclical, moderate load is the stimulus that tells the remodeling bone which direction to organize in. The tract remains a modest stress riser until consolidation finishes, so the same rule from before still applies: ordinary walking and daily life unrestricted, but hold off on jumping, running, and hard lateral cutting until the 6–12 week mark. There's no rehab protocol required for the bone itself; it responds to normal use.
