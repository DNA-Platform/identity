This is a fast-moving and genuinely significant conflict. Here's the full picture as of today (March 17):
How it started
The war began on February 28, 2026, when the US and Israel launched surprise airstrikes on multiple sites across Iran, killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and numerous other officials. The proximate justifications were Iran's nuclear program and domestic crackdown: massive nationwide protests had erupted in late December 2025 following the collapse of Iran's currency, and Iranian authorities responded with lethal force — the death toll is disputed, with Iran's government citing around 3,100 dead and human rights activists counting over 6,000.
The nuclear pretext was complicated. Trump stated at the State of the Union that Iran had restarted its nuclear program and was developing missiles capable of striking the US. However, the IAEA said it had no evidence Iran had an organized nuclear weapons program, while also acknowledging it couldn't certify Iran's program was "exclusively peaceful" due to denied access. And crucially, an unspecified Pentagon source told Congress in closed-door briefings that there was no intelligence suggesting Iran was planning to attack US forces first.
The strategic shift from the prior 2025 "Twelve-Day War" is what made this different. In 2025, US and Israeli forces largely concentrated on nuclear and military facilities, leaving room for negotiations — that conflict ended after Omani mediation. This time, the opening salvo assassinated Khamenei and several family members, shattering the previous rules of engagement and leaving zero diplomatic off-ramps.
NATO allies
Your question about allies is largely accurate — the picture is one of near-universal non-participation. NATO as an organization has deliberately avoided direct involvement, limiting its role to logistics and missile defense while refusing collective military action under Article 5. Secretary General Mark Rutte praised the strikes for degrading Iran's nuclear and missile capabilities but repeatedly stressed NATO will not be "dragged into the conflict."
Bilaterally, the picture is just as stark. German Defense Minister Pistorius said "This is not our war, we have not started it," which appeared to sum up the mood among US allies, with leaders from Berlin to London expressing reservations and indicating no immediate plans to provide military support. Germany, Greece, and Italy all rejected involvement; the UK's Starmer said it "won't be, and it's never been envisioned to be, a NATO mission." Japan and Australia also said they had no plans to send ships. Trump himself acknowledged on Truth Social that "most NATO allies" have refused, before claiming the US "never needed" their help anyway.
The UK is a partial exception — Starmer said the US could use UK military bases "for a specific and limited defensive purpose" to destroy Iranian missiles at source, specifically Diego Garcia and RAF Fairford. But that stops well short of combat participation.
The US outlook and danger
The situation looks difficult by most independent assessments. Several compounding problems:
The financial burden is staggering — the first 100 hours of "Operation Epic Fury" cost approximately $3.7 billion, mostly unbudgeted. Iran perceives this as an existential conflict and appears uninterested in an immediate off-ramp, calculating that a slow war of attrition serves its interests better than a ceasefire that would merely be a temporary respite before the US or Israel restarts the conflict.
Iran has effectively restricted traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, pushing Brent crude past $100 a barrel, and the G7 countries have declined to jointly tap their oil reserves. Iran has widened its retaliation to strike across nine countries, hitting US military presence and civilian infrastructure in all Gulf states including Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Oman, and the UAE.
There's also significant internal dissent. Trump's top counterterrorism official Joe Kent resigned, saying in a public letter that "Iran posed no imminent threat to our nation, and it is clear that we started this war due to pressure from Israel and its powerful American lobby."
On the nuclear stockpile specifically — arguably the entire stated casus belli — after receiving a classified briefing, US Rep. Bill Foster raised concern that the Trump administration "never had a plan for the nuclear stockpile of enriched uranium — to destroy it, to seize it, or to put it under international inspection."
European analysts note a secondary casualty: Western ammunition stocks and missile interceptors are being depleted in the Middle East, the surge in energy prices has thrown a lifeline to Russia's economy, and Trump's temporary sanctions waiver on Russian oil has weakened the European argument that aligning with Trump protects their interests in Ukraine.
Bottom line: the war is now in its third week, Israel has publicly said it plans at least three more weeks of operations, Iran has shown no sign of seeking a ceasefire, and there's no obvious off-ramp. The dominant expert framing right now is "war of attrition with no clear endgame."

So the pain distribution is almost perfectly inverted: the countries that opposed the war bear more of the economic consequence than the country that started it. That's a powerful wedge, and it doesn't require Iran to fire a single additional missile to maintain it.
Trump already did the preparatory work for them. Iran is essentially exploiting a structural weakness that pre-existed this conflict. Iran and Russia don't need to formally coordinate for this to function as a de facto joint squeeze on the transatlantic alliance.

If Iran is selectively restricting passage for US and Israeli-aligned vessels while keeping it open for Chinese ones — which is roughly what the Iranian foreign minister implied — that creates a scenario where China gains a relative economic advantage during a US-initiated conflict. That's a strategic gift.

The overall picture: Iran found the one asymmetric lever capable of making a conventionally superior adversary pay escalating costs, in a conflict where the US arrived with pre-damaged alliances and no clear endgame. Whether that translates into actual long-term economic distancing from allies is less certain — but the conditions for it are unusually favorable from Tehran's perspective.

There's a really sophisticated strategic debate happening right now among serious people, and your intuition about the Hormuz leverage maps almost exactly onto the central argument. Let me lay out the competing frameworks.
The core paradox, stated cleanly
The Washington Institute captures it precisely: while the US and Israel are clearly winning militarily, if they stopped fighting today they would be judged to have lost. That is the definition of a strategy that depends entirely on achieving a political outcome — regime collapse or unconditional surrender — that military force alone is unlikely to produce on an acceptable timeline, at an acceptable cost, or with acceptable international legitimacy.
That's the trap. The US can't win by military means alone, but stopping short of regime collapse leaves Iran's Hormuz leverage intact.
Your "open the strait, control the goods" idea — what strategists are actually saying
Iran has partially solved this problem already, and in a way that illustrates why your instinct is correct but tactically complicated. On March 5, the IRGC announced it would keep the strait closed only to ships from the US, Israel, and their Western allies — allowing a Turkish ship through, and two Indian-flagged gas carriers and a Saudi oil tanker for India. Iran is already selectively managing passage, essentially running a geopolitical sorting mechanism. They're not closing it to everyone — they're closing it to the coalition trying to destroy them while leaving it open to China, India, and neutrals. That's actually more strategically sophisticated than a blanket closure, because it maximizes economic pressure on the US-aligned world while preserving their own economic lifeline and keeping China onside.
The bypass routes exist but are badly constrained. Saudi Arabia is diverting oil through the East-West Pipeline to Yanbu, and the UAE via pipeline to Fujairah — but combined pipeline capacity handles only 3.5-5.5 million barrels per day against roughly 20 million that normally pass through the strait. And Iran has been striking those bypass nodes too — Duqm and Salalah in Oman were hit, pushing them into war-risk insurance zones.
Does the US need to "wipe Iran out" to solve this?
The pro-war strategists argue yes, basically. The argument from that camp is: you don't need to escort tankers through a strait if the adversary no longer has the means to threaten them. That's the operational trajectory — destroy Iran's shore-based assets, mines, submarines, drones, and the leverage evaporates.
The counter-argument from the Small Wars Journal piece is perhaps the most rigorous critique available right now: Iran is fighting across all five instruments of national power — diplomacy, information operations, military pressure, economic coercion, and legal pressure — while Washington has answered with more airstrikes and contradictory presidential statements. The implication being that even total military victory doesn't address the political and economic dimensions, which will persist into whatever regime follows.
The helium problem nobody is talking about
Qatar normally produces about 36% of the world's helium, which has now been largely cut off. Helium is crucial to chip manufacturing, which is central to US plans to compete with China in AI. Trump launched a $12 billion critical minerals stockpile last month, but helium isn't on the list. That's a genuinely underappreciated second-order consequence — the war's energy disruption is bleeding into semiconductor supply chains, which feeds directly into the China competition the administration claims to prioritize.
The coalition-building angle and your underlying question
A former Obama/Trump envoy who built both the ISIS coalition and the Red Sea Houthi-response coalition argues the Red Sea model — a US-led naval escort mission that eventually grew to nearly 20 countries — could be replicated for Hormuz. A UN Security Council resolution co-sponsored by 135 countries condemning Iran's attacks has already established the Article 51 collective self-defense legal threshold. But the political problem is that EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said there was "no appetite" for extending the EU's Aspides mission to Hormuz, and European leaders feel "left out of the loop" on US strategy — they want to know what the endgame is before committing forces.
That's the crux of your question answered: Iran doesn't need to defeat the US militarily to achieve a strategic win. It just needs to sustain the Hormuz disruption long enough that the economic pain on US allies — already resentful, already squeezed — outweighs whatever political cost comes from distancing themselves from Washington. A new Iranian supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei — an IRGC product with no reformist-diplomatic track — has been installed, and he is not expected to pursue the kind of diplomatic off-ramp his father occasionally balanced against IRGC militarism. That makes a negotiated solution harder, not easier, while the military campaign grinds on.
The uncomfortable strategic summary: the US entered a war whose political endgame was undefined, against an adversary that holds a geographic asymmetric lever powerful enough to impose costs on the entire global economy, in a context where pre-existing alliance damage limits the coalition that could share those costs or provide a political off-ramp. "Wipe them out" solves the military problem but not the day-after political vacuum problem — as Iraq demonstrated at considerable expense. "Open the strait and control goods" is conceptually sound but Iran has already anticipated it by running a selective access regime that keeps China and neutrals onside.

Boots on the ground — closer than the official line suggests
The official position is no ground invasion, but that's eroding visibly. The Atlantic Council assesses that Iran is a massive country with very difficult topography that would require hundreds of thousands of troops to occupy — any use of ground forces would likely be limited to special operations for specific missions. But the pressure points are driving a narrower question than full occupation. The Wall Street Journal published a detailed assessment openly discussing the option to "use ground troops to seize the territory around" the Strait of Hormuz, noting that maintaining control would require an invasion. Senator Lindsey Graham posted "He who controls Kharg Island controls the destiny of this war" — a fairly explicit signal toward a Marine amphibious operation. A Marine Expeditionary Unit of roughly 2,200 Marines has already been deployed to the region.
So the likely answer isn't "full ground invasion of Iran's interior" — that's Afghanistan squared, with a population of 90 million, serious topography, and an IRGC that has spent decades preparing for exactly this scenario. The more probable path is limited seizure of strategic chokepoints — Kharg Island, the Hormuz coastline — to break the oil leverage. That's still enormously dangerous but it's a bounded objective rather than occupation.
Your Iran-vs-Afghanistan scale intuition is exactly right
A military analyst told Al Jazeera that a traditional invasion is not viable given Iran's complex geopolitical environment, rugged geography, and demographic density — all of which give Tehran a distinct defensive advantage. Israel has previously declared a ground operation in Iran impractical. Iran is roughly four times the population of Afghanistan, has a functioning (if battered) state apparatus, has the IRGC as a parallel military force of 190,000 with decades of asymmetric warfare experience, and controls geography that directly strangles global energy. The Iraq/Afghanistan comparison vastly understates the problem.
The Russia-Ukraine-Putin axis — this is where it gets really ugly
The Trump administration eased sanctions on Russian oil to help tamp down energy prices from the Hormuz disruption — specifically a Treasury license allowing Russian oil sales to India for 30 days, with reports of additional relief being considered. This is a direct win for Russia, whose economy has been in steady decline under Western sanctions and the oil price cap. So the sequence is: start Iran war → Hormuz closes → oil spikes → relieve pressure on Russia to compensate → Russia's war chest recovers → Ukraine weakens. That's not a conspiracy, it's just the mechanical consequence of the strategic choices already made.
Whether Trump is consciously coordinating with Putin or just making decisions that happen to benefit him is the key interpretive question. The observable outcome is the same either way.
The government installation fantasy — the Pahlavi option
Reza Pahlavi, son of the former Shah, has said he's ready to have his transitional government take over should the Islamic Republic be overthrown. Kurdish Iranian dissidents in Iraq have said they would support a US invasion. The Boston Globe piece floats the Libya/Yugoslavia model — no Western boots, just air power that degrades the regime until it collapses internally and an opposition takes over.
The problem is that Libya is now a failed state, and Yugoslavia's stability required Western peacekeepers for years. Iran is orders of magnitude more complex. The most practically-minded expert assessment — from King's College London — is that Washington could still be open to a coercive settlement with elements of the regime, including IRGC-linked actors, if those actors were willing to protect the state while conceding enough on missiles, nuclear restrictions, and regional behavior to let Trump claim success. In other words: not regime change but regime modification — a deal with whoever survives the decapitation strikes that gives Trump a win narrative.
What Trump will actually do — reading the behavioral pattern
Synthesizing all of this honestly: Trump entered with maximalist rhetoric and no defined endgame, which is consistent with his negotiating style — create maximum pressure and see what cracks. The problem is that Iran's new supreme leader Mojtaba Khamenei is an IRGC hardliner with no diplomatic track record, the IRGC has pledged full obedience to him, and the regime's survival instinct at this point is stronger than its willingness to deal. Only about one-quarter of Americans support the war — well below the 55% who supported the 2003 Iraq invasion — which severely limits Trump's domestic political room for escalation.
The most probable near-term path: a limited amphibious operation around Hormuz and/or Kharg Island to break the oil leverage, combined with quiet back-channel outreach through Oman looking for a face-saving deal with whoever holds power in Tehran. Trump will want to declare victory and exit — the question is whether Iran gives him enough to work with. If the regime doesn't crack and the IRGC holds, the pressure for something more drastic builds. And without NATO alongside, the costs — financial, military, diplomatic — keep accumulating on the US alone. That's ultimately unsustainable, which is precisely what Iran is banking on.
