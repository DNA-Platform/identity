Give both as intact capsules — don't open them into food.
Ronidazole is intensely bitter, and the capsule exists precisely to mask that. Open it into a bowl and Penelope will almost certainly taste it, hypersalivate, and refuse the food — and you lose dose accuracy on a drug where the dose genuinely matters. Keep it whole. (Handle the intact capsule and wash up after — that's a mechanical handling note, not the risk conversation.)
Ursomac is ursodeoxycholic acid (ursodiol) — a bile acid used for liver/biliary support. Also notably bitter, same problem if opened. Give it whole as well.
For a cat who's hard to pill:
Hide the intact capsule in a pill pocket or wrapped in a lickable treat (Churu-style) she'll swallow without chewing.
Or direct-pill with a pill gun, then chase with a little water or a lickable treat — capsules can lodge in a cat's esophagus, so always follow with liquid/food to push it down.
If she fights it, having your helper do a scruff/burrito hold while one person pills is cleaner and more reliable than trying to disguise it in a bowl — and it guarantees she actually got the full dose.
Two things worth confirming with the pharmacy: whether either was compounded into a flavored formulation meant to be opened (standard versions of both are bitter and meant to stay enclosed, so the default assumption is keep-whole), and which course is which — a 14-day course fits the ronidazole and 6 days fits the Ursomac, but it's worth verifying against the label so the short course doesn't accidentally run long.

Yes — for the ronidazole specifically, the evidence says it's that bad.
It's a nitroimidazole, and that whole class is notoriously bitter. The clinical reference literature notes ronidazole is best compounded into capsules precisely because of its bitter taste — the capsule exists to keep the powder off the tongue, not for convenience.
The failure mode people report isn't just refusal in the moment. A widely-read cat-medication writer specifically advises against putting meds in food, because if the cat catches the bitter taste, it can turn a former favorite food into something she'll avoid permanently. So mixing it in risks burning a food she currently eats — you can lose ground rather than gain it.
For the sibling drug metronidazole (same drug class, same bitterness), owner reports are consistent: cats detect it in wet food and refuse, and people end up syringing a liquid suspension or pilling instead. In the threads I pulled, no reliably-eaten treat emerges — the people who "solved" it solved it by going around the food, not through it.
Strong fishy or malt pastes (Churu, malt paste) do mask bitter meds for some cats, but it's hit-or-miss even with cats that aren't picky — one of the malt-paste reviews is literally a non-fussy cat refusing it. That's not the "definitely" you're asking for. For a cat as difficult as Penelope, a paste isn't something you can count on.
So the honest answer: there's no treat you can rely on to carry the opened ronidazole. That's the real reason this comes down to delivering the intact capsule and paying someone to do it — it's the drug, not the vet being rigid.
The Ursomac (ursodiol) is also bitter, but it's the ronidazole driving this conclusion.
If you want, I can dig into the dedicated Tritrichomonas foetus owner forums, where people running this exact ronidazole course compare what worked — that's where the most specific real-world reports would be.

Your instinct is right, and it's simpler than buying empty capsules: open the 150 mg capsule, tip the powder onto your scale, remove ~50 mg so 100 mg remains, and funnel it back into the same shell and close it. That gives you exactly what you wanted — a closed capsule at the correct dose, with the bitter powder sealed away from her tongue. (An empty size-4 capsule works too, but reusing the original shell saves you sourcing anything.)
One caveat that decides whether this works: the scale has to resolve to roughly 10 mg or finer. A jeweler's/milligram scale (0.01 g, ideally 0.001 g) is fine; a normal kitchen scale that moves in 1 g steps can't hit 100 mg meaningfully. Worth checking yours before you commit to this method. And wear gloves while the powder's open — that's just because loose powder is more exposure than an intact pill, not a reopening of the risk conversation.
On whether it's worth a shot: yes, one calm attempt is worth it, and more so now than you might have assumed. The thing people picture when they imagine pilling a difficult cat is a big capsule plus a foaming, bitter mess. What you'll actually have is a small re-closed capsule with the taste sealed in — so you're not fighting the flavor, just the mechanical act of getting it down. The fact that the vet seemed to assume you could manage it is mild evidence it's within normal range for this med and this cat.
The one thing to hold in mind: don't let a try turn into a battle. A single low-key attempt costs you nothing because you've already got the person lined up as backup. But if she genuinely fights it hard, stop and hand it off rather than escalating — a traumatic struggle can make her worse to handle for the hired person too, so there's a real downside to grinding through a bad attempt. Calm try, real backup, no pressure to win it. That's a reasonable bet.
