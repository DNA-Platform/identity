The question of semantics' meaning is fascinating and multifaceted. At its core, semantics is the study of meaning itself - how meaning is created, understood, and communicated through language. But this seemingly simple definition opens up deeper philosophical questions.
We can approach semantics from several angles:
Linguistic semantics examines how words and sentences convey meaning, including the relationship between signifiers (words) and what they signify (concepts or objects). For instance, how do we understand that "dog" refers to a four-legged canine, and how does that meaning shift across contexts and languages?
Philosophical semantics delves into questions about the nature of meaning itself. What makes a statement meaningful? How do we connect abstract symbols to real-world truth conditions? This touches on fundamental questions about reference, truth, and the relationship between language and reality.
Cognitive semantics looks at how our minds process and construct meaning, considering how our conceptual frameworks and embodied experiences shape our understanding of language.
The challenge in defining semantics lies in its self-referential nature - we're using meaning-laden language to define the study of meaning itself. This creates interesting paradoxes and complexities. For example, when we say "green means the color we see in grass," we're using meaning to explain meaning.
Perhaps most intriguingly, semantics reveals that meaning isn't just about dictionary definitions but encompasses cultural contexts, personal experiences, and shared understanding. When we say "it's raining cats and dogs," the meaning transcends literal interpretation through shared cultural knowledge.
What's your perspective on this? Do you see meaning as something fixed and definable, or more fluid and contextual?

From a scholarly perspective, there are several influential theories about the root of meaning and semantic fundamentals.
The formalist tradition, stemming from Gottlob Frege and developed by philosophers like Rudolf Carnap, approaches semantics through logical forms and truth conditions. They argue that meaning fundamentally emerges from the logical relationships between propositions and their truth values. This aligns somewhat with your approach of seeking fundamental semantic forms.
However, Wittgenstein (particularly in his later work) challenged this view with his concept of "meaning as use" - arguing that the root of meaning comes not from formal logical structures but from how language is actually used in practice within what he called "language games." His famous example of the impossibility of a truly private language suggests meaning must be inherently social.
Saul Kripke's work on naming and necessity introduced important ideas about how reference works, arguing that some semantic content (particularly names and natural kind terms) is grounded in causal-historical chains rather than descriptive content. This suggests a different kind of semantic primitive.
More recent work in cognitive semantics by scholars like George Lakoff suggests that the roots of meaning lie in embodied experience and conceptual metaphors. Under this view, even abstract concepts are ultimately grounded in physical and spatial experiences.
Your project of seeking fundamental semantic forms is intriguing because it seems to bridge these approaches - looking for formal structures while potentially allowing those structures to emerge from actual semantic practice. In algebra for unifying different algebraic structures

Let me teach you about one of category theory's most beautiful and profound ideas: the Yoneda Lemma. It captures something deep about how objects can be understood entirely through their relationships with other objects.
The basic idea:
For any object A in a category, we can look at all the morphisms TO it from every other object
We can also look at all morphisms FROM it to every other object

The Yoneda Lemma is typically proved by showing that certain natural transformations are in one-to-one correspondence. Let me break down the steps:
For any object A, we define its "representable functor" hA which maps:
Each object X to the set of morphisms Hom(X,A)
Each morphism f: X→Y to the function that composes with f
The lemma states that for any functor F, natural transformations from hA to F are in bijection with F(A)
The proof roughly:
Given an element a ∈ F(A), we can define a natural transformation
At each object X, map each morphism f: X→A to F(f)(a)

Naturality conditions ensure everything works
