Let me set the counting up explicitly, because the count only means something once the counting principle is fixed.
Counting principle. For a constitutive form F, take the distinct referents F constitutes, and count the directed formsF itself licenses among them — not the ones that happen to hold of those referents for other reasons.
text
Individuality   relata {x}          licensed: —              0
Reference       relata {x, y}       licensed: x→y            1
Representation  {symbol, literal}   both, via representative 2
Identification  {identity, ident.}  both, via canonical      2
Relationship    {r, s, o}           6 ordered pairs, minus determined
On your first question. The count per form is what decides whether type semantics is available at all. One direction and no other way gives you reference and nothing more — there's nowhere for identification to sit, so no typing. Two directions with a mediator is exactly the identity/identification shape, and that is type semantics: x =y> y & x =canonical> y =|> (y,x) == y. So "different types of types" isn't a new kind of type; it's what you get if a form licenses more than two directions, and then it isn't type semantics anymore — the extra direction is doing something typing can't express. Relationship is the only form on this list where that can happen.
Where I can't pin your count. "Two are determined by most specific" has two readings and they leave different residuals:
(a) The symbol-structure identities. ((x,y),x) == (x,y) and ((x,y),y) == (x,y). These determine the composites, not directions. Residual directed forms: r→s, r→o, s→r, o→r, o→s. Then "relationship to subject" picks r→s and you're claiming the other four are not licensed — which reads cleanly, and makes the subject slot the only thing the relationship reaches from itself.
(b) The canonical lines. (r) > s and (r) > o. Then r→s is already spent, and "relationship to subject" has to be s→r, leaving o→s and o→r excluded.
Reading (a) is the one where your closing sentence lands as stated. Under (a) the totals are 0, 1, 2, 2, 1 — six licensed forms across the five, which is small enough to be worth taking seriously as a complete inventory rather than a sample.
One flag on wording: "innumerable" reads as the opposite of what the count supports. Six forms is enumerable — and if the transcription went that way, then "less than I thought" and "enumerable space" are the same observation rather than a tension.

individuality   —          x =x> x            0

Total free: 5. That's the whole space of what a semantic context can rule on.
The thing I'd flag. Identification and relationship tie at 2, but the two degrees behave differently under composition.
Identification: the two free forms compose into a permitted result — x =y> y & x =canonical> y =|> (y,x) == y. Filling both slots closes on an identity. That closure is what makes it type semantics.
Relationship: compose the two free forms with a determined one and you get
text
o>s (free) ∘ s>r (free)  ⟹  o>r
which is the excluded direction. So relationship's two degrees cannot both be filled and then composed, the way identification's can.
The ban on free composition is what stops this — n relations give n² compositions with only O(n) meaningful, composition symbolic on demand, geometry read off the lattice rather than summed along hops. Here that ruling isn't a convenience about tractability; it's load-bearing. Without it the relationship form's freedom reconstructs the direction reference forbids.
Which answers the earlier question in a way I didn't expect: same count, different kind of freedom. Identification's 2 are jointly fillable and that joint filling is type semantics. Relationship's 2 are individually free but not jointly composable. So a context that rules on both of relationship's slots is doing something type semantics can't express — it's not a different type of type, it's the case where the closure fails and there's no identity at the end of it.
Representation's 1 sits below both: one degree can't close a mutual determination at all, so a representative mediates without ever yielding ==.

Why it's a degree of freedom and not a gap. Take your gloss literally. a =b> b says b identifies a. b =c> c says c identifies b. Whether c identifies a is not settled by those two — it's settled by whether the context wants identification to compose. Both answers are consistent: a context can rule transitive (identifiers of identifiers identify) or isolated (each identification is its own act). Nothing above the context decides.
So this is a different sort of degree from the five in the ledger. Those were degrees within a form — which directions a form licenses. This is a degree across an application of a form — whether an instance chains. Two axes, and the second is per-context rather than per-form.
On the vase and face. I'd separate what the figure shows from what it doesn't. The line genuinely is both outlines — one referent, two profiles, and a context selects which. That's real and it's the multiple-inheritance case, which is safe here because inheritance carries truth not value. But it's a claim about one referent under two types, not about a chain of three, so it doesn't bear on transitivity either way. Your "maybe not" is right, and the reason is that the figure has no third term.
On tractability. This is the part I'd underline. Five free forms plus a per-application chaining choice gives a finite parameter space, and a semantic context becomes a setting of it rather than an informal notion. Two things follow that seem worth having: contexts become comparable — two contexts differ exactly where their settings differ — and the theory becomes surveyable, since you can ask which settings are jointly consistent instead of asking open-endedly what contexts there are.
The composition result from before then reads as one such constraint already found: relationship's two free forms can't both be set without reconstructing the excluded o>r. That's a fact about the parameter space, not about any particular context. If there are more constraints of that kind, the space of admissible contexts is smaller than 2⁵ and the study is finite in a strong sense.

Scratchpad.
T0. Transpile.
text
x -> t   ⟼ R(x,x,t)
(x,x) == x
so: the relationship asserting the typing IS x
Every t in x's profile types one and the same referent. That's the fixed point, and it's what forces the existential reading: since all types land on a single self-relationship, a t can only be a respect in which x is taken. Picking one is existential by construction. There's no room for -> to be universal — universality would need distinct bearers to quantify over, and the collapse (x,x) == x removed them.
T1. Non-transitivity — confirmed, with a sharp case.
srt
Fido =dog> Fido        Fido -> dog
dog  =species> dog     dog -> species
Fido =species> Fido ?  false — Fido is not a species
Your gloss reads exactly onto this: "property of type (it identifies things) is property of instance (which happens not to be an identifier)." Being a species is a property of dog-as-type that Fido doesn't carry. So the chain genuinely fails, and it fails for the reason you named rather than for want of a rule.
Note this is the same degree of freedom as a =b> b =c> c. Same fact, second notation.
T2. 0 — derivable, and forced.
If : is complete polymorphism, then y:z covers every respect of y. x -> y supplies a witness respect. The witness lies in y, the cover covers all of y, so the witness is z.
text
∃ ∘ ∃  — witnesses need not coincide  → fails    (T1)
∃ ∘ ∀  — cover contains the witness    → holds    (T2)
So it's not an extra axiom. It's the quantifier shapes. You wouldn't be surprised, and you shouldn't be — the only way to get chaining at all is for one factor to be complete, which is precisely what : was introduced to be.
T3. Consequence, checkable: 0 composes on both sides.
text
(:) ; (:)   ⊆ (:)     ∀∘∀
(->) ; (:)  ⊆ (->)    your line
(:) ; (->)  ⊆ (->)    z:x, x->y ⊢ z->y
(->) ; (->) ⊄ (->)    T1
Third line: z is x in every respect, one of x's respects is y, so z has it. If that holds, : is a preorder and -> is closed under pre- and post-composition with it but not with itself. Worth having because it's a strong claim that one counterexample kills.
Where I'd want your ruling. Under the three-line definition A[t.p]: E[x.p]: x.p -> t.p, transitivity of -> reduces to transitivity of -> on properties — the question returns at the profile level instead of being answered. So T1's failure is clean under the bold definition and circular under the three-line one. That's the same seam as the open question about how profile inheritance distributes across the four constitution cases, which suggests the two definitions have not yet been shown to agree.
One outside note, only as confirmation: description logics had to make instance-of and subclass-of two separate primitive arrows precisely because T1 fails. You get both from one arrow and a quantifier strength.

Taking "the class is the domain" seriously and seeing what it forces.
V1. What's already on hand.
text
type          intension — the property collection
class         extension — what satisfies it
classification the third thing mediating them
So a domain proposal has three slots to fill, and the mediating term is the one that has to do the work — a bare intension/extension pair is what every framework has, and it's not enough to state a domain constraint.
V2. What "domain of a type" would have to mean here.
Not the set of things it applies to — U1 killed that route, and no completed collection is available anyway, since "all the pointers" fails the invariance test and referability is time-indexed. So domain can't be a totality. It would have to be a condition of applicability: the respect in which something must already be taken before this type can be predicated of it.
That reads back onto the two-profile split. species applies to dog because dog is taken as a collector. It fails on Fido not because Fido lacks members but because Fido is not taken in that respect. So the domain constraint and the qua-collector/qua-referent split may be one thing, approached from the type side rather than the property side.
V3. Machinery that already exists for it.
srt
t -> (x,y)  =|>  x' !-> x | y' !-> y  =|>  x' !=t> y'
Qualification is the shape you'd want — a type over a relationship restricting what can occupy its slots. If domain is a species of qualification, it isn't new apparatus, and that would be the tractable outcome.
V4. Where I'd push back on the method, mildly.
Type theory is the right place to look for the pieces — dependent types, kinds, universes are all machinery for exactly this. But the reason domain is hard here is a commitment those frameworks don't share: they get a domain free from stratification, and stratification is what you've refused since the May finding that there were never two levels to coincide. So the pieces will not port. Every one of them presupposes the level structure that makes a domain a different sort of thing than a type. What ports is the question — what must hold of x before t applies — not the answer, and the answer has to come out of qualification and the classification term.
V5. The check I'd want run before believing the pieces suffice.
Domain is a respect in which x is taken. x.p := (p,x) already names a respect. So candidate: t applies to x only where x.t' exists for some t' in the domain condition — the domain stated as a required entry in x's profile rather than as a container.
If that closes, then a domain is a profile requirement and there is no new primitive. If it doesn't, the failure will show where the missing piece is. Either way it's a one-derivation test, and it's the smallest thing that could settle whether the system is already rich enough.
I'd flag one asymmetry: this makes domain a condition on the subject only. Whether a type also constrains what it can be composed with is a separate question, and V3's qualification is the part that speaks to it.
