Worth saying at the top: this is one of those questions where the consensus answer (P ≠ NP) is held with much more confidence than the arguments for it can strictly license, and where the most interesting content is precisely why we can't yet prove what almost everyone believes. So I'll try to keep the epistemic bookkeeping honest throughout.
The question itself
P is the class of decision problems solvable in polynomial time by a deterministic Turing machine. NP is the class where a "yes" instance admits a certificate checkable in polynomial time — equivalently, problems solvable in polynomial time by a nondeterministic machine. The question "P = NP?" is really "is finding as easy as checking?" The Cook–Levin theorem plus Karp's reductions give us NP-complete problems (SAT, and thousands of natural problems since): a single polynomial-time algorithm for any one of them collapses the whole class, P = NP. This web of equivalences is structurally important — it means the question is not about one quirky problem but about a vast interlinked family that stands or falls together.
As of now the problem is open, and has been since it was crisply posed around 1971.
The case for P ≠ NP (the majority view)
The strongest arguments are inductive and structural rather than deductive.
The empirical one is the most forceful. We have thousands of NP-complete problems arising independently across logic, optimization, biology, physics, economics — studied by enormous numbers of very motivated people for over half a century, with industrial incentives to crack any of them. None has yielded a polynomial algorithm, and because the reductions tie them together, one success would have cascaded through all of them. A web this large with zero cracks is strong inductive evidence that the cracks aren't there to find. Gasarch's informal polls of theorists track this hardening confidence — a clear minority for P≠NP in the early 2000s growing to the high 80% range by the late 2010s.
The second is Scott Aaronson's "if P = NP the world would be a profoundly different place" argument. If finding were as easy as checking, then producing a proof of a theorem would be no harder than verifying one; composing a symphony that meets a checkable spec would be as easy as recognizing that it does. Mathematical and creative discovery would be mechanizable by brute search over polynomial-time-checkable structure. Our deep intuition that creativity is genuinely harder than appreciation — that the asymmetry between generating and verifying is real and fundamental — is, if you take it seriously, an intuition that P ≠ NP.
The third is structural: P = NP would collapse the entire polynomial hierarchy to its zeroth level. Most theorists believe PH is strictly infinite, so a collapse all the way down would be a large and surprising loss of structure.
The case for P = NP (steelmanned)
This is the minority position and the honest framing is that the arguments are more about humility than positive evidence.
The historical-surprise argument is the best one. Our intuitions about hardness have been wrong before, in exactly the "this surely needs exponential search" direction. Linear programming was widely believed intractable until Khachiyan's ellipsoid method (1979) put it in P. Primality testing resisted a deterministic polynomial algorithm for a long time until AKS (2002) showed PRIMES ∈ P. Each time, a problem that "felt" like it required search turned out to have hidden polynomial structure. SAT could in principle be another such case, and our failure to find the algorithm is not a proof that none exists — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, especially given the next section's point that we don't actually have the tools to prove a separation.
A second, subtler line: complexity theory has produced genuinely shocking collapses and equalities — IP = PSPACE being the canonical example, where interactive proofs turned out vastly more powerful than anyone expected. These surprises are a standing warning that confident intuitions about what "must" be separate are unreliable.
There's also a deflationary observation worth registering even though it doesn't argue for equality per se: real-world SAT solvers are extraordinarily good on the instances people actually care about. Worst-case intractability coexists with routine practical tractability, which suggests the worst-case framing — though mathematically the right one — may capture less of what we intuitively mean by "hard" than it appears to.
Net: I'd represent the field as strongly, but not unanimously or dogmatically, on the P ≠ NP side, with the dissent grounded mostly in epistemic caution.
Why it's so hard: the three barriers
These are the crux, and they're best understood as a sequence of no-go theorems, each ruling out a broader family of proof techniques. A successful proof has to evade all of them.
Relativization (Baker–Gill–Solovay, 1975). Give every machine access to an oracle A — a set it can query for membership in one step. A proof technique "relativizes" if it goes through unchanged for machines equipped with any oracle; the classic tools of diagonalization and simulation do relativize, because they treat machines as black boxes. BGS then constructed two oracles: one A (take a PSPACE-complete set like TQBF) for which P^A = NP^A, because the oracle is powerful enough to wash out the distinction, and another B, built by diagonalization, for which P^B ≠ NP^B. The separating B works by encoding a unary language "is there a string of length n in B?" — this is trivially in NP^B (guess the string, one query verifies it), but a deterministic polynomial-time machine can only probe polynomially many of the 2^n strings of length n, so you can always set B to fool it. Consequence: since the answer to "P vs NP" differs across oracles, no relativizing technique can settle it, because such a technique would have to give one answer for all oracles. The hierarchy theorems — our best lower-bound tools — relativize, and are therefore disqualified.
Natural proofs (Razborov–Rudich, 1994/97). This targets the circuit-lower-bound route: prove NP needs super-polynomial circuits, which suffices since P ⊆ P/poly. Most known lower bounds (for AC⁰, AC⁰[p], monotone circuits) share a template — they isolate a property of Boolean functions that the hard target function has and small-circuit functions lack. Razborov and Rudich formalized this as a "natural" property, meaning it is:
constructive — computable in time polynomial in the truth-table size 2^n;
large — satisfied by a non-negligible fraction of all Boolean functions (a random function tends to have it);
useful — not satisfied by any function with small circuits.
Their theorem: if strong (sub-exponentially secure) one-way functions exist — equivalently, pseudorandom functions exist — then no natural proof can separate P from NP. The reason is gorgeous and self-undermining. A constructive + large property is exactly a statistical test that efficiently distinguishes random functions from structured (small-circuit) ones. But a pseudorandom function, computable by small circuits, is by definition indistinguishable from random by any efficient test. So a natural property useful against P/poly would break the PRF — contradicting the cryptographic hardness we believe in. The bite: the very cryptographic hardness that P ≠ NP is supposed to underwrite is what obstructs the most natural route to proving P ≠ NP. A working proof must be unnatural — sacrificing largeness (exploit a specific function's idiosyncratic structure) or constructivity (use a property that's itself hard to compute).
Algebrization (Aaronson–Wigderson, 2008). Some landmark results don't relativize — IP = PSPACE and the PCP theorem, both powered by arithmetization, replacing Boolean formulas with low-degree polynomials over a field and exploiting their algebraic structure rather than black-box query access. So the hope arose that arithmetization could crack P vs NP. Aaronson and Wigderson built a barrier capturing exactly this. Extend each oracle A to its low-degree polynomial extension Ã; a technique "algebrizes" if inclusions go through when the larger class gets access to the algebraic extension Ã (with the dual condition for separations). They showed arithmetization-based techniques algebrize — which is why they succeed at IP = PSPACE — but that P vs NP requires non-algebrizing methods: there are algebraic oracles pushing the answer both ways. Algebrization properly extends relativization: even relativization-plus-arithmetization is not enough.
The three together carve out a narrow corridor. A proof of P ≠ NP must be non-relativizing, non-algebrizing, and (for the circuit route) non-naturalizing. That such a corridor isn't empty is shown by Ryan Williams's NEXP ⊄ ACC⁰ (2011), which threads all three via the "algorithmic method" — connecting faster-than-brute-force satisfiability algorithms to lower bounds. It's far from P vs NP, but it's an existence proof that the barriers can be beaten.
What a resolution means for cryptography
The stakes here are sharply asymmetric, and the precise logic matters because it's easy to overstate in both directions.
If P = NP (with a genuinely feasible exponent — n² rather than n^100): it is essentially the end of cryptography as we know it. The clean statement is that the existence of one-way functions implies P ≠ NP; contrapositively, P = NP rules out one-way functions entirely. And one-way functions are the minimal object cryptography needs — without them you lose pseudorandom generators, secure symmetric encryption, bit commitments, digital signatures, the lot. In Impagliazzo's "five worlds" framing, P = NP forces us into Algorithmica, where no cryptography survives, and collapses Cryptomania and Minicrypt (the worlds with public-key and symmetric crypto respectively). Two honest caveats: a P=NP proof might be non-constructive, or yield an exponent so large the algorithm is useless in practice — in which case the practical apocalypse is deferred even though the theoretical foundations are gone. But a constructive, feasible P=NP would be civilization-rearranging, and not only for security — optimization, machine learning, protein folding, theorem-proving all become "merely" verification problems.
If P ≠ NP: this is necessary but not sufficient for cryptography, and the gap is the part people routinely get wrong. P ≠ NP is a worst-case statement — some instances are hard. Cryptography needs average-case hardness — instances drawn from the key distribution must be hard, essentially always. An NP-complete problem could be worst-case hard yet easy on random instances; that's Impagliazzo's Heuristica, a world with P ≠ NP and no usable crypto. So even if someone proved P ≠ NP tomorrow, RSA would not thereby be secured. We would still need the separate, stronger, still-unproven assumptions that factoring, discrete log, LWE, and so on are hard on average. Whether P ≠ NP evenimplies the existence of one-way functions is itself a major open problem, and most believe it doesn't follow easily.
Two further points sharpen this. First, the workhorses of deployed cryptography — factoring, discrete log — sit in NP ∩ coNP and are believed not NP-complete, since an NP-complete problem in NP ∩ coNP would force NP = coNP and collapse the polynomial hierarchy. So mainstream crypto doesn't actually rest on NP-completeness at all; it rests on these finer, structured hardness assumptions. (Lattice problems like LWE are attractive partly because they come with worst-case-to-average-case reductions, narrowing — though not closing — the gap that makes NP-completeness unusable for crypto.) Second, P vs NP is a classical question and is orthogonal to the quantum threat: Shor's algorithm breaks factoring and discrete log in quantum polynomial time, but factoring isn't believed NP-complete, so a proof of P ≠ NP would say nothing about post-quantum security either way.
The thread that ties the barriers back to the crypto picture is the natural-proofs irony, which I think is the single most beautiful structural fact in this whole area: the better cryptography actually works — the more genuinely pseudorandom our functions are — the more it forbids the natural route to proving the hardness that cryptography depends on. The thing we want to prove and the thing that blocks the obvious proof are, at bottom, the same assumption viewed from two sides.
