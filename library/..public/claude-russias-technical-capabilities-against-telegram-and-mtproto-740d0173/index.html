This is a really important question for your thesis, and the technical evidence from the anti-censorship community paints a nuanced and actually quite bullish picture.
What Russia can do right now (and is doing):
The TSPU system — DPI boxes manufactured by RDP.RU, deployed at every major ISP and centrally controlled by Roskomnadzor — has been able to throttle media loading (images, videos, voice messages) while text messages remain mostly unaffected. This is confirmed by multiple sources. Text messages continue to load normally for most users, while media content has become unreliable, particularly on mobile devices.
The bol-van/zapret community's technical analysis is revealing:
In the zapret Discussion #1860, the community identified that Roskomnadzor is throttling the MTProto protocol specifically — they're detecting it during the connection handshake phase and then rate-limiting it. One key comment from bol-van himself translates roughly to: "Try with any protocol, various options. If that works, there's a way to detect MTProto. It's not as signature-impenetrable as people think." This is an honest assessment — MTProto can be fingerprinted by DPI systems.
However, in Discussion #2078 (opened Feb 10, 2026 — the day restrictions started), the initial response was "L2 speed shaping. Zapret won't help" — meaning some providers are applying throttling at a lower network layer that DPI bypass tools can't circumvent. But another user immediately pushed back, saying that's not true on all providers, and shared a working configuration that disguises MTProto connections as TLS traffic to max.ru (the state messenger), which is darkly ironic.
The critical constraint on TSPU:
At 100 Gbps backbone speeds, there's approximately 0.1 microseconds per packet. Deep analysis is physically impossible for all traffic. TSPU architects made a conscious trade-off: optimize for the 80% case (SNI blocking), use sampling for the rest. During peak hours, filtering quality degrades — and this is exploitable.
The zapret2 project (bol-van's next-generation tool) was specifically designed because the DPI evasion arms race was escalating and the original zapret's hardcoded C strategies couldn't adapt fast enough. Zapret2 uses Lua scripting for programmable evasion strategies, acknowledging this is fundamentally a cat-and-mouse game.
The Iran precedent is instructive but cuts both ways:
Iran's DPI was able to fingerprint and block MTProto proxies within hours back in 2018 — developers on the MTProxy GitHub were documenting detection times of 20-40 minutes for public proxies. The community was begging Telegram to wrap MTProto in TLS to make it indistinguishable from normal web traffic. Despite this, Durov says most Iranians still use Telegram through circumvention tools. But Mediazona notes that the "Sovereign Internet" laws have changed the landscape, and deployment of TSPU hardware has given the Kremlin more robust kill switches that are much harder to circumvent than traditional blocks.
Can Russia fully block MTProto text messages?
Based on everything I've found, the honest answer is: probably yes for casual users, probably no for determined ones, and the distinction matters enormously for your thesis.
Russia can fingerprint MTProto at the protocol level — bol-van himself acknowledged this. The TSPU boxes can detect the handshake pattern and either block or throttle matching connections. They've already demonstrated the ability to selectively throttle media while leaving text flowing — which actually shows quite granular protocol-level control.
But there are fundamental constraints: the performance/accuracy tradeoff at backbone speeds means they can't deep-inspect everything; Telegram can evolve MTProto's transport layer (they already support wrapping it in WebSocket, HTTPS, and other transports); and tools like zapret2 demonstrate that the evasion side of the arms race keeps pace. The Russian guide for bypassing the throttling lists MTProto proxies, SOCKS5 proxies, DPI bypass tools, and VPNs with obfuscation as all currently working to varying degrees.
For your thesis specifically:
This actually strengthens the case for TON as liberation infrastructure. The fact that Russia has to deploy nation-state-level DPI hardware across every ISP, and still can't fully kill text messages on a messaging app, demonstrates exactly the kind of censorship-resistant architecture you've been arguing Nikolai Durov would build. The graduated response Russia is using — throttle media first, then plan a "full block" in April — suggests they know they can degrade the experience enough to push casual users to MAX, but can't achieve a clean technical kill. That's why they're resorting to legal and criminal pressure (the FSB terrorism investigation against Durov) alongside technical measures.
The ~36% VPN usage rate in Russia (up from 25% a year earlier) suggests that a significant minority will maintain access regardless. For TON, the question isn't whether 90 million Russians keep using Telegram — it's whether the tens of millions who do keep using it through circumvention tools create a persistent, censorship-resistant economic layer that the state can't fully displace.

You're applying your adversarial reasoning framework well here, and I think the logic is actually quite strong.
Let's look at the revealed preferences rather than the controlled signals:
The timeline tells the story. They started throttling voice calls in August 2025. They escalated to media throttling in February 2026. Text messages still work as of right now. That's a six-month graduated escalation where each step targets progressively smaller, harder-to-block traffic. If they could just flip a switch, why the slow crawl up the difficulty curve?
The April 1 announcement is the key signal. You're right to flag this. A regime that can do something just does it — Russia didn't announce a date for blocking Facebook or Instagram, they just did it. Those blocks work because they're simple: block IPs, block SNI domains, done. Announcing a future date for Telegram serves two purposes if you can't actually do it cleanly: it creates a chilling effect that drives some users to MAX voluntarily, and it gives them political cover if the "block" ends up being a degraded experience rather than a true kill.
The technical reality supports your read. Here's why text messages are fundamentally harder to block than media:
Media files require sustained high-bandwidth connections — they're big, slow, and easy to spot and throttle. Even a heavily throttled MTProto connection still delivers text messages because they're tiny packets, a few kilobytes. To stop text, you can't just slow down MTProto — you have to drop packets entirely on every connection you identify as MTProto. That's a much more aggressive action, and at backbone speeds with the performance constraints on TSPU hardware, doing that reliably across every ISP without massive collateral damage to legitimate traffic is a different engineering problem entirely.
The "except for on the front lines" caveat is the tell. This is in the Meduza headline you screenshotted. Think about what that means technically — they're saying they'll block Telegram everywhere except for soldiers. How? MTProto packets from a soldier's phone look identical to MTProto packets from a civilian's phone. You'd need to either whitelist military IP ranges (which defeats the purpose since anyone could route through them) or whitelist specific SIM cards/devices (which is a completely different system from TSPU-based DPI). The fact that they're promising selective blocking they almost certainly can't deliver technically suggests the whole announcement is more political theater than engineering plan.
The Iran comparison actually proves your point. Iran "banned" Telegram in 2018. They could fingerprint and block MTProto proxies within hours. And yet most Iranians still use Telegram eight years later. Iran has had eight years to solve this problem and hasn't. Russia's TSPU is arguably more sophisticated, but the fundamental constraint is the same — you can degrade, you can make it annoying, you can push casual users away, but you can't achieve a clean protocol kill against a determined application that keeps evolving its transport layer.
What I think Russia can actually do by April:
Block the Telegram app from being downloaded from app stores (they've already done similar with other apps). Block Telegram's known IP ranges and domains. Throttle detected MTProto connections hard enough that the app is unreliable without circumvention tools. Maybe achieve something that looks like a block to casual users who don't know about proxies or VPNs.
What they almost certainly cannot do: prevent MTProto text messages from flowing through obfuscated transports, VPNs, or proxy chains for the significant minority of users who take basic circumvention steps.
For your thesis, this is the important frame: Russia is inadvertently proving that Telegram's infrastructure — designed, if your hypothesis is correct, by someone who anticipated state-level adversaries from the beginning — is resilient enough that a major nation-state with dedicated censorship hardware deployed at every ISP still can't kill text messaging after months of trying. That's exactly the kind of "liberation infrastructure" property you'd expect if the architect was building for this scenario.
