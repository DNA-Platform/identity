Great question, and one where the details really matter. Here's the breakdown across all three drugs:
Acetaminophen (Paracetamol) — The Narrowest Therapeutic Window
This is paradoxically the most dangerous of the three in overdose, despite being considered the "safest" at therapeutic doses. The therapeutic dose is 10–15 mg/kg per dose in children, with a max daily dose of 80 mg/kg/day. Toxicity in children occurs at a single dose of 150 mg/kg, with doses above 350 mg/kg posing great risk for severe hepatotoxicity if untreated.
That's only a ~10x margin between a therapeutic dose and a potentially lethal one — which is remarkably narrow for an OTC drug.
The critical mechanism: at therapeutic doses, over 90% of acetaminophen is metabolized via glucuronidation and sulfation, but about 5–10% gets converted by CYP2E1 to NAPQI, a highly reactive toxic metabolite normally neutralized by glutathione. Overdose depletes glutathione, and NAPQI destroys hepatocytes.
Here's the age-dependent twist that's actually somewhat reassuring for young children: neonates and infants have lower rates of glucuronidation but compensatory increased sulfation, and there's evidence of relative immaturity in CYP2E1 metabolism, which substantially reduces production of the toxic NAPQI metabolite in infants. CYP2E1 protein expression increases after birth, reaching adult levels by about 1 year of age.
So ironically, very young children are somewhat more resistant to acetaminophen hepatotoxicity than adults — their immature enzyme systems produce less of the toxic metabolite. Although acetaminophen poisoning is more common in children, adults are more likely to develop hepatotoxicity.
The real danger with acetaminophen in kids is chronic supratherapeutic dosing — febrile kids getting slightly too much, too often, especially if they're dehydrated and not eating (which depletes glutathione stores further). Acetaminophen toxicity is the leading cause of liver failure in the United States, accounting for 50% of all reported cases and 20% of all liver transplants.
When does the concern "dissolve"? It doesn't — it actually gets worse with age. Adults metabolize more acetaminophen through the CYP2E1 pathway, producing more NAPQI. Chronic alcohol use further induces CYP2E1 and depletes glutathione. The drug gets more dangerous, not less, as you age.
Aspirin — Unique Categorical Risk (Reye's Syndrome)
Aspirin is fundamentally different from the other two because the primary pediatric danger isn't dose-dependent toxicity — it's an idiosyncratic reaction.
The CDC, U.S. Surgeon General, AAP, and FDA all recommend that aspirin not be given to children and teenagers under 19 years of age during episodes of fever-causing illnesses due to the association with Reye's syndrome. In the UK, the threshold is under 16.
Key facts about the risk profile:
There's no safe dose of aspirin for kids when it comes to preventing Reye's syndrome — even normal or low doses can cause it. This is what makes it categorically different from acetaminophen and ibuprofen, where toxicity is dose-dependent.
While less than 0.1% of children who took aspirin developed Reye syndrome, more than 80% of children diagnosed with Reye syndrome had taken aspirin in the preceding 3 weeks.
Peak age of onset is 5 to 14 years, though cases have occurred in children under one year.
The risk decreases with age, becoming extremely rare by the late teenage years. Only 8% of cases occur in children 15–17.
A few adult cases have been reported, but these do not typically show permanent neural or liver damage.
The mechanism involves mitochondrial failure — newer research suggests that aspirin plus viral illness may overwhelm mitochondria in liver cells, particularly in individuals with undiagnosed inborn errors of metabolism affecting mitochondrial function. This explains why it's not strictly dose-dependent.
Aspirin does have separate dose-dependent GI toxicity (bleeding, ulceration) that applies at all ages, but that's the standard NSAID risk, not the unique pediatric concern.
When does the concern dissolve? Aspirin use should be limited to children under 19 with diseases where it has proven benefit, such as Kawasaki disease. Practically speaking, the risk becomes vanishingly small after ~18–19, but it's not a hard biological cutoff — it's a population-level epidemiological observation.
Ibuprofen — Widest Therapeutic Window, but Not Benign
Ibuprofen has the most favorable safety profile of the three in acute overdose.
Therapeutic dose: 4–10 mg/kg per dose every 6–8 hours, max 40 mg/kg/day up to 1200 mg/day.
Symptoms of toxicity are unlikely at doses below 100 mg/kg in children.
Children who have consumed more than 400 mg/kg are at higher risk and generally should be admitted, while those who ingested less than 100 mg/kg can be safely observed at home.
So the margin between therapeutic (~10 mg/kg) and symptomatic (~100 mg/kg) is roughly 10x, and between therapeutic and serious toxicity (~400 mg/kg) is roughly 40x. Compare that to acetaminophen's ~10x to hepatotoxicity.
The primary concerns with ibuprofen are:
Renal: prostaglandin inhibition can impair renal perfusion, especially in dehydrated kids
GI: mucosal injury and bleeding (the standard NSAID issue)
In massive overdose (>400 mg/kg), CNS depression is the second most common finding after GI disturbances
Minimum age: Not approved for children under 6 months of age. This is primarily because safety hasn't been established in that age group, and neonatal kidney function is immature enough that prostaglandin inhibition poses real renal risk.
When does the concern dissolve? The 6-month minimum age restriction is the main one. After that, ibuprofen is generally the pediatric analgesic/antipyretic with the most favorable risk profile when dosed correctly.

Good — here's the full breakdown covering drug classes, mechanisms, and actual milligram dosages from the sources directly.
Drug Classes & Mechanisms of Action
Acetaminophen (Paracetamol) — Class: Para-aminophenol derivative / Analgesic-Antipyretic
Acetaminophen is literally in a drug class all its own, in contrast to the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, for which there are dozens of types.
This is the key thing most people don't realize — acetaminophen is not an NSAID despite being shelved next to them. Although the exact mechanism of action remains unclear, it is historically categorized alongside NSAIDs due to its inhibition of the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathways. Acetaminophen possesses analgesic and antipyretic properties similar to NSAIDs but lacks peripheral anti-inflammatory effects.
The mechanism is genuinely fascinating and still not fully resolved after 130+ years of use. The prevailing hypothesis: acetaminophen acts as a reducing agent to inhibit a secondary peroxidase step involved in prostanoid synthesis by COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes. Because it inhibits the peroxidase reaction, its inhibitory effects are surmounted in the presence of high levels of lipid hydroperoxides produced by activated leukocytes and platelets. This prevents acetaminophen from being effective in sites of inflammation or activated platelets. So it works in the brain (low peroxide environment) but not at the site of tissue inflammation (high peroxide environment) — which is why it kills pain and reduces fever but doesn't reduce swelling.
There are additional proposed mechanisms: acetaminophen's mechanism of action is multifaceted, involving inhibition of brain-specific COX enzymes, modulation of the endocannabinoid system, and activation of serotonergic pathways. The endocannabinoid connection is especially interesting — a metabolite called AM404 has been detected in the brains of animals and cerebrospinal fluid of humans taking paracetamol, formed from another metabolite by the action of fatty acid amide hydrolase. AM404 is a weak agonist of cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2, an inhibitor of endocannabinoid transporter, and a potent activator of TRPV1 receptor.
So acetaminophen may partly work through the same receptor system as cannabis. That's a remarkable thing for one of the most mundane drugs on earth.
Aspirin — Class: Salicylate / Non-selective NSAID
Aspirin is technically both a salicylate and an NSAID, but it occupies a unique position among NSAIDs because its binding is irreversible.
Aspirin's ability to suppress the production of prostaglandins and thromboxanes is due to its irreversible inactivation of the cyclooxygenase (COX) enzyme. Aspirin acts as an acetylating agent where an acetyl group is covalently attached to a serine residue in the active site of the COX enzyme. This makes aspirin different from other NSAIDs (such as diclofenac and ibuprofen), which are reversible inhibitors.
Unlike other NSAIDs, which bind reversibly to this enzyme, aspirin binding is irreversible. It also blocks thromboxane A2 on platelets in an irreversible fashion, preventing platelet aggregation.
This irreversibility is the whole story for both its therapeutic uniqueness and its dangers:
Antiplatelet effect: Since mature platelets don't express COX-2, and platelets have only mitochondrial DNA, they are unable to synthesize new COX once aspirin has irreversibly inhibited the enzyme. This means one dose of aspirin knocks out platelet aggregation for the entire 7–10 day lifespan of those platelets. That's why low-dose aspirin prevents heart attacks — and why it causes bleeding problems.
COX-1 vs COX-2 selectivity: ASA is non-selective for COX-1 and COX-2 enzymes, but inhibition of COX-1 results in inhibition of platelet aggregation for about 7–10 days. A higher dose of acetylsalicylic acid is required for COX-2 inhibition. So aspirin preferentially hits COX-1 at low doses (antiplatelet), and you need higher doses to also suppress COX-2 (anti-inflammatory).
Ibuprofen — Class: Propionic acid derivative / Non-selective NSAID
Ibuprofen is a textbook reversible, non-selective NSAID. COX inhibitors divide into non-selective nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, COX-2 selective nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and aspirin. NSAIDs include ibuprofen, naproxen, ketorolac, and indomethacin.
Ibuprofen, 2-(4-isobutylphenyl) propionic acid, belongs to the class of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and was discovered by Dr. Stewart Adams in 1961, first marketed in 1969 in the United Kingdom and 1974 in the United States.
The mechanism is straightforward compared to the other two: it reversibly blocks both COX-1 and COX-2, suppressing prostaglandin synthesis at both the central and peripheral level. This gives it the full trifecta that acetaminophen lacks: analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory. Because it's reversible, the effects wear off as the drug is metabolized (half-life ~2 hours), unlike aspirin where the enzyme is permanently disabled.

You're right — I was hand-waving at the critical step. Here's the actual pain relief mechanism, and the key insight is that these drugs are fundamentally not psychoactive in the way opioids are. They don't alter pain perception in the brain the way morphine does. Instead, they work primarily by removing the reason the pain signal exists in the first place.
How Pain Actually Works (the signal chain)
Pain starts at the periphery. When you damage tissue — cut your finger, twist your ankle, have an infected tooth — the damaged and surrounding cells release a cascade of inflammatory mediators: bradykinin, histamine, ATP, cytokines, and critically, prostaglandins (especially PGE2).
Here's the essential point that explains everything: Responses to acute thermal and mechanical stimulation in mice lacking prostaglandins were in nearly all cases indistinguishable from those obtained for wild-type mice, indicating that prostaglandins are not required for acute nociception and do not exert a tonic influence on the nociceptive system.
That's a crucial finding. Prostaglandins don't cause pain directly. What they do is far more insidious:
Inflammation sensitizes polymodal nociceptors. Their excitation threshold drops such that even light, normally innocuous stimuli activate the fibers, and noxious stimuli evoke stronger responses than in the non-sensitized state. In addition, inflammation recruits so-called silent nociceptors for activation — C-fibers that are inexcitable by noxious mechanical or thermal stimuli under normal conditions.
So the mechanism is sensitization, not direct activation. Prostaglandins turn up the volume knob on your pain nerves. They do this through a specific molecular mechanism:
The prostaglandins block the potassium efflux released from nociceptors following damage, which results in additional depolarization. This makes the nociceptors more sensitive.
And at the molecular level, the key receptor being sensitized is TRPV1 (the capsaicin/heat receptor): TRPV1 is indirectly sensitized, via second messengers, by the inflammatory mediators bradykinin, prostaglandin E2, extracellular ATP, glutamate, proteases, and NGF. The important consequence of this sensitization is that the threshold for opening by temperature stimuli drops such that normal body temperature may be sufficient to activate nociceptors and to elicit action potentials.
Read that last sentence again: after prostaglandin sensitization, your own body temperature can be enough to fire pain neurons. This is why inflamed tissue hurts even when you're not touching it — the nociceptors are firing from the baseline thermal environment of your own body.
This produces two clinical phenomena:
Hyperalgesia: An increased painful sensation in response to additional noxious stimuli. One explanation is that the threshold for pain in the area surrounding an inflamed or injured site is lowered.
Allodynia: Pain resulting from a stimulus that does not normally produce pain. For example, light touch to sunburned skin produces pain because nociceptors in the skin have been sensitized.
There's also a spinal cord component. The subsequent development of chronic pain can result from activation of NMDA receptors and prolonged stimulation of the central nervous system, a process known as central sensitization or secondary hyperalgesia. The ensuing neuroplastic changes that lead to long-term potentiation of pain result from the production of cyclooxygenase and nitric oxide synthase, which in turn upregulate prostaglandin synthesis.
So COX/prostaglandins operate at two levels: peripherally (sensitizing nociceptors at the injury site) and centrally (amplifying pain signal processing in the spinal cord dorsal horn).
How Each Drug Interrupts This
Ibuprofen (and aspirin, for the non-Reye's effects)
These are the most straightforward. NSAIDs prevent the development of inflammation and produce their analgesic effects by blocking the synthesis of prostaglandins in the periphery.
The mechanism: tissue damage → phospholipase A2 releases arachidonic acid from cell membranes → COX-1/COX-2 convert arachidonic acid to prostaglandins → prostaglandins sensitize nociceptors.
Ibuprofen blocks step 3. No prostaglandins → no sensitization → the nociceptors return to their normal threshold → pain diminishes. The tissue is still damaged, but the amplification system is turned off.
This is why NSAIDs work so well for inflammatory pain (sprains, arthritis, dental procedures, menstrual cramps) and relatively poorly for neuropathic pain (nerve damage, diabetic neuropathy) — neuropathic pain doesn't depend on the prostaglandin sensitization pathway.
Because ibuprofen works peripherally at the actual tissue site, it's also reducing the swelling, redness, and heat of inflammation — all of which are also prostaglandin-mediated. The pain relief and anti-inflammatory effects are mechanistically the same thing.
Aspirin does this too, but irreversibly, and with the additional complication that its permanent COX-1 blockade in platelets prevents clotting (useful for heart attacks, dangerous for Reye's/bleeding).
Acetaminophen — The Weird One
This is where it gets genuinely interesting from a neuroscience perspective. Acetaminophen doesn't work peripherally. It doesn't reduce inflammation. It doesn't block prostaglandin synthesis at the injury site. So how does it relieve pain?
It operates through at least three central mechanisms — meaning it's actually closer to being psychoactive than the NSAIDs are, though nothing like an opioid:
1. Central COX inhibition. Acetaminophen may inhibit the COX pathway in the central nervous system but not in peripheral tissues. It does not appear to bind to the active site of either COX-1 or COX-2. Instead, it reduces the activity of COX through an alternative mechanism. Remember the peroxidase hypothesis from before — acetaminophen works as a reducing agent that only functions in low-peroxide environments like brain endothelial cells and neurons. So it suppresses prostaglandin production in the spinal cord and brain, where those prostaglandins are part of the central sensitization cascade. This blocks the "volume amplification" happening in the CNS even though the peripheral signal is unaffected.
2. Endocannabinoid system activation. Acetaminophen gets metabolized in the brain into AM404, which is a weak cannabinoid receptor agonist and an endocannabinoid reuptake inhibitor. This is functionally similar to what happens when you take a very mild cannabinoid — it modulates pain processing through the same receptor system that THC targets, just much more weakly. The TRPV1 activation by AM404 is also relevant, because TRPV1 activation in the brain (as opposed to peripherally) can actually trigger descending pain inhibition pathways.
3. Serotonergic descending inhibition. There is considerable evidence that the analgesic effect of paracetamol is central and is due to activation of descending serotonergic pathways, but its primary site of action may still be inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis. Your brain has descending inhibitory pathways that can actively suppress pain signals in the spinal cord. These pathways use serotonin (5-HT) as a neurotransmitter. Acetaminophen appears to enhance this top-down suppression system.
So acetaminophen essentially works by: (a) turning down the central amplifier for pain signals in the spinal cord, (b) mildly activating your endogenous cannabinoid system, and (c) boosting your brain's own "turn the pain down" serotonergic circuits. None of these are euphoria-producing at therapeutic doses (unlike opioids hitting mu receptors), which is why you don't feel "high" on Tylenol, but they genuinely reduce your subjective experience of pain.
The Key Conceptual Distinction
To use an analogy from your neuroscience background:
Opioids (morphine, fentanyl) work like turning down the volume knob on the amplifier in the listening room. The signal from the speakers is the same, but you hear less of it, and you also get a euphoric side effect because mu receptors are broadly distributed in reward circuits. These are genuinely psychoactive.
NSAIDs (ibuprofen, aspirin) work like removing the feedback loop that's causing the speakers to screech. The amplifier and listening room are unchanged. The signal at the source is reduced because the sensitization cascade that was creating the excessive signal is interrupted. Not psychoactive — they're changing the peripheral signal, not brain processing.
Acetaminophen is somewhere in between. It doesn't touch the speakers or the feedback loop, but it does something in the control room — turning down the central gain, activating the noise-cancellation system (descending inhibition), and mildly engaging the endocannabinoid "chill out" circuitry. It's the closest of the three to being psychoactive, but at therapeutic doses the central effects are subtle enough that you don't experience them as altered consciousness.
This is also why acetaminophen and NSAIDs are synergistic when combined — they're attacking pain through completely different parts of the signal chain (central gain reduction vs. peripheral sensitization blockade), which is why alternating them for kids' fevers is more effective than either alone.
And it explains the ceiling effect: like other pain relievers except opioids such as morphine, it has a "ceiling effect" to its pain-relieving abilities — after a certain dose, higher doses do not provide additional pain relief. Once you've fully blocked the sensitization pathway (NSAIDs) or maxed out the central gain reduction (acetaminophen), there's nothing more to block. The residual pain you feel is the actual nociceptive signal — the thing that tells you your tissue is damaged — and only opioids (which work downstream of all of this) can suppress that baseline signal.
