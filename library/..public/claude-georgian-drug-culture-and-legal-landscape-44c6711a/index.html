Short version: Georgia is a study in contrasts. Weed is effectively legal for personal use (the only post-Soviet state to have done this), the Tbilisi techno scene is world-famous and explicitly political, but hard drug possession — including MDMA, LSD, psilocybin — can still trigger mandatory minimum sentences of 5+ years. No indigenous entheogenic religious tradition.
Entheogens / psilocybin / peyote
Nothing. Georgian religious culture is Orthodox Christian with pre-Christian pagan substratum in the highland regions (Svaneti, Khevsureti, Pshavi), and those traditions center on wine (Georgia has the oldest continuous winemaking tradition in the world, ~8,000 years), animal sacrifice, and ritual feasting — not psychoactive plants or fungi. No mushroom cult, no peyote analog, no ayahuasca-equivalent. There's a small modern psychedelic-curious subculture (GeoPsy / Georgian Psychedelic Community has a Facebook presence of a few thousand), but it's an import from Western psychonaut culture, not rooted in anything local. Psilocybe atlantis is actually a species described from the country, but there's no traditional use.
Legally, psilocybin, LSD, MDMA, and ketamine are all treated as hard drugs. Georgian law has historically had a brutal quirk: for about three-quarters of illicit substances, there's no defined threshold for "small amount," so even trace residue can be prosecuted as "large quantity," triggering mandatory minimums. A 23-year-old DJ named Kote Japaridze was charged in 2016 with possessing two grams of MDMA and faced 8–20 years; he plea-bargained to six (five suspended).
The rave scene
This is where Georgia actually shines, internationally. Bassiani (inside the drained Olympic swimming pool under Dinamo Arena) is spoken of alongside Berghain. Khidi, Left Bank, Mtkvarze, TES, Café Gallery — there's a real ecosystem. The scene is explicitly tied to queer rights, anti-conservatism, and drug policy liberalization. White Noise Movement is the NGO pushing decriminalization; they're embedded in the club scene.
In May 2018, Georgian Spetsnaz raided Bassiani and Café Gallery with machine guns under a "drug enforcement" pretext. The response was a 15,000-person protest rave on Rustaveli Avenue that became known internationally as the "Raveolution." The minister of internal affairs publicly apologized, the prime minister resigned a month later, and two months after that the Constitutional Court legalized cannabis consumption. The clubs are still operating. MDMA is absolutely present in these venues but the legal risk is real.
DnB scene — "dw / dread"
I'm not certain which specific collective or artist you meant — there's a UK DnB MC named Dread MC, and "dw" doesn't map to anything I recognize in the Georgian context. Happy to dig if you clarify. The local DnB scene is nascent but real: DNB Georgia is the main collective/label, running nights at TES and elsewhere. Sioni Hi-Fi Crew runs a bass-heavy sound system. The genre is dwarfed by techno here — if you came for 174 BPM you'd find it, but the center of gravity is four-on-the-floor.
Drug problems
Georgia has had serious, sequential drug epidemics driven largely by enforcement whack-a-mole:
Late 90s–early 2000s: heroin (traditional trafficking route from Afghanistan via Iran → South Caucasus → Europe)
2004–2008: Subutex (buprenorphine) diverted from EU substitution programs, at one point supposedly 70% of ~200,000 Georgian users
2009 onward: home-cooked stimulants ("Jeff," "Vint") from pseudoephedrine-containing cold medicine
2011–2013: krokodil (homemade desomorphine from codeine tablets) at peak, ~40%+ of injectors
Now: alpha-PVP, methadone diversion, MDMA, cocaine, plus ongoing heroin transit
Recent police reporting (2025–2026) has ~77% of tested suspects testing positive, with Tbilisi at 84%. The Baku-Tbilisi-Kars railway is reportedly becoming a new narcotics corridor. HCV prevalence among people who inject drugs is 50–70%.
Versus Turkey
Practically speaking, Georgia is more permissive at the social/cultural level — weed is legal to use, the club scene is visible and internationally plugged in, queer nightlife exists in a way it really doesn't in Istanbul anymore. But the legal exposure for hard drugs is worse than Turkey in the tail, because of the mandatory-minimum structure and the no-threshold problem. Turkey's enforcement is strict and the social conservatism is heavier, but Turkish law has clearer proportionality between quantity and sentence. So: more freedom on the upside, sharper cliff on the downside.

Your intuition is actually inverted — the harsh penalties are exactly why Georgia has one of the most developed darknet drug economies per capita in the world.
The numbers are striking. A CRRC Georgia study of the Russian-language cryptomarket Matanga found it generated ~$1.5M in Georgian drug sales over just 6 months in 2020 — exceeding the monthly darknet drug revenue of Spain and Belgium combined, in a country of 3.7 million people. On an average day, ~$35,000 worth of substances were stashed in dead drops across Tbilisi, Batumi, and Kutaisi. Tbilisi alone accounted for ~87% of listings. 120 active vendors, 1,409 substance listings, with cannabis, cocaine, MDMA, methadone, alpha-PVP, and 25i-NBOMe making up ~95% of activity.
The logic: when both buyer and seller can catch 5–20 years for a gram, the anonymity premium becomes enormous. The risk of a face-to-face transaction is catastrophic, so the marginal inconvenience of Tor + crypto + GPS-coordinate-to-buried-package is worth it. In the EU, where penalties are lower and street dealing is less risky, darknet is proportionally smaller because it's just annoying to use. In Georgia it's the dominant channel.
But Dread specifically — no, probably not much. Dread is the English-language Reddit-clone forum that grew up around Western DNMs (AlphaBay, Empire, ASAP, now DrugHub, etc.). Georgia operates almost entirely within the Russian-language ecosystem, which is a separate universe — it runs on its own forums (Rutor, Legalizer, etc.), its own markets (Matanga, Hydra before its takedown, Mega, Kraken, BlackSprut), its own slang. This ecosystem generates ~97% of global darknet drug revenue and is structurally different from the West: Hollywood-quality promo videos, TikTok-style marketing, enforcers called "sportsmen" who beat up kladmen (couriers) who steal or misplace drops. Sometimes beatings posted to Telegram as warnings. Mega's front guy "Moriarty" has 2.8M Telegram followers. It's organized crime with a consumer-tech UX.
The Russian darknet model has explicitly spread into Tbilisi — it's named as one of the cities where this ecosystem has taken root, alongside occupied Ukraine and Seoul. The dead-drop delivery system (the buyer gets GPS coordinates after paying and has to find the stash — taped under a park bench, buried near a tree, magnet-stuck to a railing) means buyer and seller never meet. Georgian kladmen are recruited from the low-prospects young male demographic, paid in crypto, photographed at their drops for accountability. Some end up in prison when police triangulate them from the photos.
Telegram is now at least as big as the darknet side. A lot of the retail trade, especially cannabis (which is weirdly legal to possess but illegal to sell, creating a bizarre grey market), has migrated to Telegram and Signal. Vendors run channels, post menus, take crypto, use the same dead-drop model. The barrier to entry is lower than Tor, so it's reached the non-technical demographic. After Durov's arrest in 2024 there was some migration but the platform is still heavily used.
So the picture is: Georgia looks like a hard-line prohibition state on paper, but because of that, it's a surprisingly mature darknet/dead-drop economy where a Bassiani-goer's Friday night supply chain probably runs through a Russian-language cryptomarket and a GPS coordinate in a park rather than a person in a bar. It's a very different underworld architecture than what you'd have encountered in Turkey, where street-level dealing under a less draconian legal regime looks more like the Southern European model.
The counterintuitive takeaway: severity of penalties correlates with darknet share, not with total drug volume. Prohibition doesn't suppress demand; it just changes the topology of supply.

Good questions, all three with answers.
No takedown required. The CRRC Georgia study was pure web scraping — they ran a Python scraper against the live Matanga site over Tor, five pulls per day for 194 days (Feb 5 – Aug 16, 2020), filtered to Georgian cities. ~116k records across 1,480 unique listings. The code is on GitHub (crrcgeorgia/matanga). This is standard academic DNM methodology pioneered by Nicolas Christin with Silk Road back in 2013 — listings, prices, stock, vendor handles, and feedback are all publicly visible to any browser, so you just automate the browser. The researchers estimate sales by watching stock decrement between scrapes. It's observational econometrics, essentially. Matanga itself kept operating; it's still one of the regional players.
Yes, and the Russian forum ecosystem is more mature than the English one — but architecturally different. The equivalents are RuTor, LegalRC, and WayAWay (and historically RAMP before it was taken down in 2017). Key structural difference from Dread:
Dread is deliberately independent of any marketplace — it's a Reddit-style watchdog/consumer-advocacy layer, and its "superlist" of trusted markets functions as the closest thing to regulation the Western DNM scene has.
The Russian forums are vertically integrated with specific markets. WayAWay + LegalRC literally co-founded Hydra in 2015. After the 2022 Hydra takedown, RuTor partnered with OMGOMG, while WayAWay re-emerged as the forum arm of Kraken. During the 2022 succession wars, these forums DDoS'd each other, hacked each other, doxxed each other — it mapped onto the Russia/Ukraine split, with RuTor/OMG viewed as pro-Ukraine and WayAWay/Kraken as pro-Russia. Not a neutral review layer at all; it's the propaganda arm of the market it's married to.
There's also DrugStat, a Telegram-based survey channel that polls buyers on market share — basically a Nielsen ratings service for the Russian DNM economy.
So: more commercially entangled, bloodier, more tribal than Dread. Dread is Consumer Reports; RuTor is a trade association that sometimes launches cyberattacks on rivals.
Dead-drop vs mail: net risk reduction for buyer, net risk increase for the courier.
The shift to dead drops in the post-Soviet ecosystem happened for a specific reason: in 2014 Russia passed a law requiring postal services to inspect packages for narcotics, which gutted the mail-order model. Once that happened, the vertically-integrated dead-drop system (Hydra's innovation, copied by every successor) became dominant. Georgia inherited this architecture from the Russian ecosystem.
For the buyer, dead drops actually reduce most risk vectors:
No delivery address tied to the purchase — the single biggest de-anonymization vector in Western DNM busts just doesn't exist
No package to intercept, no controlled delivery stings, no postal forensics
No courier who knows your face or apartment
The buyer's new risk is concentrated in a single narrow window: the retrieval moment. You're digging in a park, feeling under a bench, unscrewing a hydrant cover, with GPS coordinates on your phone — in public, often in daylight, sometimes in snow where your footprints are visible. If a cop or neighbor clocks you at that moment and you've already picked up, you're caught with drugs in hand and no plausible story. But this is a compressed, controllable risk — minutes long — versus mail, where the risk window extends over days and routes through institutions you don't control.
The courier (kladmen — "treasure-placer" from Russian клад) absorbs most of the system risk. They're paid in crypto, photograph each drop for accountability, and if a buyer can't find the package the "sportsmen" enforcers find them. A meaningful fraction of Russia's drug prisoners are kladmen, and during the Ukraine war some were offered Wagner/prison contracts as an alternative to sentencing. So the architecture doesn't eliminate risk — it reallocates risk from customer to low-paid courier, which is what makes it commercially scalable.
You're right that Russian penalties are harsher in practice. Russia's Article 228 is notorious — "narcotics article" — and roughly one in four Russian prisoners is in for drugs, with minimums that escalate brutally with quantity. Georgia's laws look comparable on paper (no-threshold rule, 5+ years for trace amounts of some substances), but the enforcement machine is smaller, the 2018 reforms softened things somewhat, and the Georgian prison population is a fraction of Russia's per capita. So yes: whatever Georgia is, Russia is worse — and that's precisely why the Russian ecosystem engineered dead drops in the first place, and why Georgia inherited the template.

Nicolas Christin (with an h) — professor at Carnegie Mellon, head of CMU's Software and Societal Systems Department, core faculty at CyLab (their security institute). French-trained engineer (École Centrale de Lille), PhD from UVA in 2003, at CMU since 2005.
He's basically the guy who invented academic darknet-market measurement. In 2012 he ran daily crawls of Silk Road for about six months and published "Traveling the Silk Road" at WWW'13 — the first systematic quantitative analysis of a cryptomarket. He used mandatory buyer feedback as a proxy for completed sales (since feedback rates on DNMs are near-100%), and estimated Silk Road was generating ~$1.2M/month in seller revenue at the time, with the operators taking ~$92k/month in commission. That technique — scrape listings + feedback + stock deltas → infer sales — is the template everyone has copied since, including the CRRC Georgia team when they did Matanga.
His follow-up with his student Kyle Soska in 2015 extended the method across ~35 markets in the post-Silk-Road ecosystem and showed the scene was surprisingly resilient to takedowns. CMU CyLab now runs a public portal (arima.cylab.cmu.edu/markets) with anonymized data from a dozen markets — Silk Road 1 & 2, Agora, Evolution, Hydra, AlphaBay, Dream, etc. — over 22,000 vendors and 5.8M transactions worth of data for researchers.
He's testified before Congress on cybercrime, and has a cameo in the Deep Web documentary (the Alex Winter one about Silk Road), which he notes gives him a Bacon number of ≤3.
So when I said "standard academic DNM methodology" — that's his standard. The CRRC Georgia paper explicitly cites Soska & Christin's sales-estimation approach.
