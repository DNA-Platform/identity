The pickleball and artist examples are exactly the adoption pattern that builds durable consumer platforms. Niche affinity groups pick it up for a use case the incumbents handle poorly — event coordination, portfolio sharing, direct creator-to-fan, community moderation — and it spreads laterally through their social graphs. WeChat followed exactly this trajectory in China before exploding into payments and mini apps. Telegram is doing the same thing in the US, just slower because it's competing rather than leading.
What makes this matter for the investment thesis is that the bottoms-up consumer experience and the top-down institutional commitment are pulling in the same direction simultaneously. Allocators see "Telegram is the entity behind TON now"; consumers see "this messaging app I just started using is actually delightful"; developers see "this is where a billion users actually are." Each one reinforces the others rather than competing. The regulatory framing is the part that's out of step, and regulatory framings lose to product experiences over time when the product is good enough that millions of people actively prefer using it. WhatsApp had identical regulatory tensions a decade ago; nobody remembers them now because the product won.

Right now compliance happens per-transaction — every crypto interaction is potentially a taxable event, a KYC trigger, an AML flag. With USD-recognized stablecoin rails, compliance moves to the gateway: KYC happens once at the on-ramp, AML monitoring happens at the issuer/exchange layer, taxes happen on net realized USD gains rather than every coffee. That's structurally compatible with how Americans already interact with money. The friction that's kept crypto out of US mainstream adoption isn't the technology — it's the per-transaction overhead, and properly implemented stablecoin legislation removes it.
