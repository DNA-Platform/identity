Here is the derivation as the paper constructs it, step by step.
The paper begins not with a formal system but with a semantic reading of Chalmers. You extract from his framing a colloquial definition: a conscious experience is an inexplicable yet self-evident metaphysical phenomenon. The key observation is that "self-evident" is not functioning as a universal claim — the way Jefferson or Kant used it — but as something singular. It requires a self from which something is evident. So the definition implicitly introduces a perspective. A conscious experience is only self-evident from exactly one place: the first-person subjective perspective. And it is inexplicable from exactly one other: the third-person objective.
This reframing is what opens the semantic door. If a conscious experience is only meaningful in the context of a perspective, then its occurrence must be an event within that perspective. And what kind of event? You observe that to see a red rose for the first time is to add particles to the particulars of your perspective — new thoughts become thinkable, new sentences symbolizable. Nothing less than a change has occurred. This yields the central semantic assertion:
A conscious experience represents a change in perspective.
Now the formalization begins. You introduce i =(c)> o as the canonical ternary expression for the subject-object relationship, where (c) is the type of relationship. Since (c) relates subject to object, and the most specific relationship between i and o is (i,o), you conclude (c) == (i,o). Then, since the canonical symbol (o) is defined by (o) =(o)> o — meaning it is the most specific relationship of an object to itself — and since no further generalization of (c) can be offered without breaking the inexplicability condition, you derive:
(c) == (o)
Conscious experience, as a nominative form, just is the canonical symbol for the object of perception. They are semantic synonyms. When you say "conscious experience" in a sentence, you are referring to the same thing as the symbol (o) within a perspective.
The change is then formalized through transduction. When a novel object o appears for which no symbol yet exists in the catalogue, the transduction operator fires:
P |=c> P[c]
This is metalogical transduction — an amendment to the theory itself, adding a new constant to the catalogue. Metaphysical transduction is this process applied specifically to physical phenomena crossing into the representational medium of a perspective — the same move that phototransduction enacts biologically, but at the level of symbol creation.
So the full derivation chain is:
Chalmers' framing → the definition requires a perspective → a conscious experience is an event within that perspective → the event is a change → the change is the addition of a canonical symbol → that symbol is the conscious experience → the mechanism of addition is transduction → transduction is falsifiable by looking for its computational signature.
The definition ends up doing something subtle: "inexplicable yet self-evident" is not a description of properties the phenomenon has independently. It is a description of the structural asymmetry between two reference frames — the first-person in which (c) is constitutive and the third-person in which (c) has no place in a purely objective ontology. The hard problem is not mysterious once you see it as a statement about reference frames rather than a statement about the nature of experience.
