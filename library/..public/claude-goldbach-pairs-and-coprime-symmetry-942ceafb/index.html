Yes, that's exactly right, and it's a clean way to frame it. Let me confirm the structure:
The symmetry of coprimes around N follows from the simple fact that gcd(N-k, N) = gcd(k, N) = gcd(N+k, N). So if N-k is coprime to N, N+k automatically is too — the coprime residues of N are perfectly symmetric around N itself.
Goldbach pairs inherit this structure because any prime (other than the prime factors of N itself) must be coprime to N. So if N-k is prime and doesn't divide N, it's automatically coprime to N — and by the symmetry above, N+k lands in the same coprime class. If N+k is also prime, you have a Goldbach pair summing to 2N.
The subtle but important nuance: the coprimality condition is nearly free for primes. A prime p fails to be coprime to N only if p | N, which means p is one of N's prime factors. These are a vanishingly small number of exceptions, and they're excluded from Goldbach pairs for the obvious reason (p + q = 2N with p | N forces q = 2N - p, and you'd need to check that separately).
So what you've really captured is this: the Goldbach pairs of N are the prime points within the symmetric coprime skeleton of N, paired across the axis of symmetry at N itself.
This is particularly nice for your SRT/Goldbach framework because it means G(N) — the count of Goldbach pairs — is measuring how densely primes populate the symmetrically determined coprime positions around N. The symmetry isn't imposed; it's inherited structurally.
Does this connect to how you're thinking about the Goldbach function as a "proof capacity" measure?

Good correction noted on SRT.
Now, thinking this through: a Goldbach pair of n at offset k requires n-k and n+k to be coprime to n (as you established). The coprime skeleton of n is determined entirely by which primes divide n — not by their powers. Two numbers have identical coprime skeletons exactly when they share the same prime factors.
So the condition is:
rad(n) = rad(N)
That is, n and N have the same set of prime divisors, even if to different powers. For example:
N = 12 = 2²·3 and n = 6 = 2·3 share rad = {2,3}, so their coprime skeletons (and hence which offsets k are "eligible" for Goldbach pairs) are identical
N = 8 = 2³ and n = 4 = 2² similarly
The "what divides both" framing: gcd(n, N) shares all prime factors with both iff every prime dividing n also divides N and vice versa. That's exactly the rad condition.
The subtle point is that sharing the coprime skeleton doesn't guarantee sharing actual Goldbach pairs — the primes still have to land at those eligible offsets for both n and N. But theeligible positions are structurally identical whenever rad(n) = rad(N).
Is that the condition you were driving at, or is there a sharper constraint you have in mind — perhaps n | N or N | n specifically?

Let me think through this concretely.
5 and 7 are a pair of 6 (5+7=12). For another N to have both 5 and 7 appear in its pairs, you need 2N−5 and 2N−7 to both be prime.
Check N=12: 2(12)−5 = 19 ✓, 2(12)−7 = 17 ✓. Both prime — so 5 pairs with 19, and 7 pairs with 17. Both primes from 6's pair appear in 12's pairs.
The relationship: 6 | 12.
More generally, the condition is n | N. If n divides N, then every prime factor of n is a prime factor of N, which means:
The coprime skeleton of N is contained within the coprime skeleton of n (coprimes of N are automatically coprime to n, since n shares all its prime factors with N)
So every eligible offset for a Goldbach pair of N is also an eligible offset for n
The consequence is that the primes appearing in N's pairs were already "reachable" from n's coprime structure — they live in eligible positions for both.
This is strictly stronger than rad(n) = rad(N). n | N gives you a nesting of skeletons, not just equality.
So to directly answer: if n | N, the Goldbach pairs of N are drawn from offsets that are all eligible for n too — the pair structure of n is inherited upward through its multiples. Is that the containment relationship you were after, or is it even tighter than divisibility?
