What a message of Claude's is made of beyond what was said: an action and its result, an artifact and an attachment, each a writing of its own that the importer writes and the page draws as Claude's application does. They specialize what my reference manual says a conversation is in general, a book of messages, each by a speaker at a time, with its participants on the cover; the conversations themselves are filed under Our Conversations.
The Action and the Result
What Claude does in a message that is not said, a search, a fetch, a run of code, a read of a file, as a writing of its own; and the result, what came back, a writing the import counts and leaves out.
Done, and what came back
A message of Claude's holds more than prose. Where he searched the web, fetched a page, ran code or read a file, the message holds an action, one line saying what was done in plain words. What came back is a result, and the import does not write it: a fetched page or a run's output is the machine's and not the conversation's, it was what filled a page when it was kept, and the export still holds it, so the import counts the results it leaves out and a result that matters is kept by hand. Neither is a paragraph of what was said, so neither is a paragraph wearing a mark: each is a writing of its own, a paragraph in shape and not in meaning, which the page can fold, show as the application does, or count. They are Claude's, so they stand in this manual and not in the library's, where a message is said to be a speaker, a time and what was said.

The Artifact and the Attachment
A file Claude made in a message and a file given to him in one, each a figure where it entered the conversation: a card for the artifact and a chip for the file given, each opening in the pane.
Made, and given
An artifact is a file Claude made as he answered, a document, a page, a program, and went on changing as the conversation went on. The day's chapter holds an artifact where each version was made: a figure, as a code listing is, that names the version's file beside the chapter and is drawn as the application draws one, a card with a glyph for a document or for code, the artifact's title and a line to open it; every version's file stands beside the chapter and the last under its own name. Each version is also a chapter of the conversation, said to be a version, its document converted to writing at import under an opaque section and its code set in a code box; the card's caption refers to that chapter, and pressing the card opens it in the pane, where the versions can be walked. An attachment is a file given to Claude in a message, pasted or uploaded: a figure the same way, drawn as a chip at the head of the words it was given with, naming the file when the export carried its text, which it does for what was pasted and not for what was uploaded, and opening in the same pane. Both are writings of their own and not paragraphs of what was said, as an action is; each is a figure, since what it shows is the file.

The Project
The project a conversation with Claude was held in, said on the conversation's cover as a fact of Claude's, and the conversation with Claude that draws it.
Held in a project
In the application a conversation is held in a project, or in none, and the project holds the files Claude reads while it goes. That is a fact about Claude's conversations and not about conversations in general, so it is said here and not in the library's manual: a project is an annotation of a conversation's cover, as the author and the subject are, holding the project book's name and standing a reference to it, and the cover writes it with the form that says the project catalogues the conversation besides, so the project book's table answers it. It is not a topic. A topic is what a conversation, or a part of one, is about, and the same conversation may turn to several; the project is where it was held, one at most, whatever it turned to.
A conversation with Claude is the library's conversation specialized here: it answers its project, and draws a project line beside its byline, a word said to be a label and the project's name leading to its book, the way back from a conversation to its project. The project book itself is a project manual, the files as spreads and the conversations as entries.

The Page
How a conversation with Claude is shown on its page: the form the application itself gives a conversation, read off the application, and the browser this library keeps open to read it.
The form the application gives
A conversation is shown as the application shows it, since that is the form its reader knows, and annotations and flourishes come on that base. The form is read off the application's own page in a browser, element by element and style by style, and written as the theme of a conversation with Claude, so that the message, the speaker, the time, the action, the artifact and the attachment are dressed as the application dresses them.
The browser this library keeps open
The application's pages need my sign-in, so the library opens a browser of its own, with a profile that keeps the sign-in once I have logged in there, and leaves it open with a port a later command attaches to: one command opens it on the application and keeps it open, and the others list its tabs, open a page, say what an element says, print its tree or its computed styles, photograph it, scroll to an element, hover or press one and say what changed, or name what runs past the right edge at a width. The file beside this chapter is that tool, run from the repository's root. It is an essential tool of developing this library and is opened at the start of a session, in the background, with the command said there: node .me/.claude/.manual/4-the-page~browser.mjs open; it stays open as a resource until I close it, and I log in there once. It is a browser and not an interface for calls: it keeps one tab per place, the application, the workbench, the built site, a file, and goes to an address in the tab it has rather than opening another, and what it leaves open is closed with its close command.

The Pane
The box beside the words where an artifact or a file given opens, as the application opens one.
What a pane is
When an artifact's card is pressed in a conversation, the artifact opens beside the words in a pane that takes half the window, the words narrowing beside it, as the application the conversations come from opens one. At the pane's head stand the artifact's title and its kind, a copy that puts the whole of it on the clipboard, and a close; in its body the version itself, a document as writing and code in its box; at its foot the version's number with an arrow to the one before and the one after, and a download that saves the version as a file. A file given to Claude opens the same way, from its chip at the head of my words, its text in the body and no foot, and an upload the export did not carry says so. The pressed card or chip reads pressed while the pane is open; pressing it again, or the close, shuts the pane. On a phone the pane covers the words. The design is concept 2.
How a pane fits the library's patterns
A pane is a paragraph with content of its own, drawn as a box, as the reference manual's panel is. What it shows is a field of the conversation it stands in: the card or the chip that was pressed sets it, the pane reads it, and a press is a value set on the book, which draws once. An artifact's version is a chapter of the conversation, written at import as every message is, so the pane draws a chapter and never reads a document from a string. The chapter stands in the book's appendix, listed in a section of the table the side does not show, and is a page of the book like any other, hidden until the pane shows it, since the binder asks that every chapter be listed and the proof that every link on the page land; the days down the side are only days because the index lists no parenthetical entry. Its rules are its own, on its own element, and the conversation's theme only says where in its grid the pane stands.

The Imported Conversation
The form an import writes a conversation in, file by file, which every importer writes and the reference manuals' types read; held stable, so the manuals change how a conversation looks and behaves without a conversation being imported again.
Why the form is written down
A conversation with Claude is imported from the application's export into my library as a book, and from then on it is a book: I edit it, cut it into chapters by what they are about, annotate it, and bind it with everything else. The importer that writes it and the types that draw it are built by different hands, and the export of my second account, the older one, is read by a different parser again. So the form they meet at is said once, here, and it is the one thing about an imported conversation that does not move: a change to the look or the behaviour of a conversation is a change to the reference manuals and reaches every conversation at the next bind, and a change to this form is a change to the import, made with care and run again. The three figures at the end of this chapter are excerpts of imported chapters as they stand, cut after their first messages, not written for the purpose.
The book
A conversation is a book in a folder of its own under the conversations book, named by the slug of the conversation's title. Its book file extends the Claude conversation of The Project and declares nothing else. Its cover is the bookshelf cover of my manual with two volumes, the conversations book's cover and my story's, a scheme of six colours, a window, the participants, each a reference to a person's book, the period from the first instant to the last, the messages shown and the messages in all, the title in the reference form, the author as my librarian, the subject as the conversations book, the project as Claude's own annotation, and an illustration inserted from the file beside the cover. Its synopsis is the application's own summary as one paragraph, kept for the catalogue and never shown on the page. Its table lists the days under headings by month, each a reference to the day's chapter, then every artifact version in an appendix section headed Artifacts, each entry parenthetical, and last the parenthetical row naming the book, the synopsis and the table.
The day
Each day of the conversation is a chapter, numbered in order and named by the day, with the date said by Dated as a date and the day as the title. The chapter holds one Message per message, said at its instant, which is the message's whole address, opening with its Speaker as a reference to the person's book, mine or Claude's. What was said is the library's own writing, paragraphs, headings, lists and lines nested as written, tables, bold, emphasis, verbatim and quoted words, inline math and displayed equations, a link as a word carrying a reference. A block of code is a code box with its listing as a template literal of its lines, named for its language, srt where the notation's operators stand and nothing where the application named none. A tool Claude used is an action, its result left out. A file Claude made is an artifact, its sentence as the caption, its file beside the chapter, the export's id of the artifact and the place of the version chapter it opens; a file given to him is an attachment with its file beside the chapter, or the sentence alone when the export carries no content. A file beside a chapter is named for the chapter and the file, chapter, tilde, name and type, and stands there only because the chapter inserts it. The message says of itself that its headings are opaque, by The Address, so a heading inside an answer is not a place.
The version
Every version of every artifact is a chapter of the book's appendix, numbered with the appendix mark and named for the artifact and its version, marked Version and titled with the artifact's name and its version number. A document's version is one section said to be opaque, headed with the name and the number so thirteen versions wear thirteen ids, holding the document as writing; a program's version is a code box. The side hides these chapters and the card opens them in the pane.
The entry and the record
Beside the folder, in Our Conversations, the import writes one entry chapter per conversation, numbered in the order imported and named by the slug: its part the month, its date the conversation's last day, the conversation's cover as a volume, the title, a caption counting the exchanges and leading to the book, the synopsis inserted from the book's own, and the record appended from the file beside it. The record is the import's memory of the conversation, the application's id, the name, the folder, the export it came from, when the conversation last changed, how many messages it held, the digest of what was written and when, which is how a later run knows a conversation is already in, has grown, or was edited by hand, as The Ledger says. The conversations book's table lists every entry under its month with an arrow leading into the book.
What is held stable, and what may move
Held stable: the folder and its five files, the file names of days, versions and resources, the tags and their attributes as the figures show them, the reference forms for a person, a day and a version, the entry and the record. The binder holds some of it itself and refuses a bind that breaks it: every chapter listed in its table, every link landing, every id worn once, no file beside a chapter that nothing inserts, a title with no parenthesis or bracket in it, a file stem ending in no dash. The rest is the importer's promise, in The Writing. Free to move without an import: everything a reader sees, which is the manuals', the message's bubble and its hour, the code box's bar, the card and the chip, the pane and its column, the tally, the day count, the head that scrolls, every colour and every measure.
The older account
My older account's export is in the application's earlier shape, one archive with its own schema, and its import is being generalized by the other team to read it. It writes this same form and nothing else: a conversation from either account is one kind of book, told apart by its record and by nothing on its page.
