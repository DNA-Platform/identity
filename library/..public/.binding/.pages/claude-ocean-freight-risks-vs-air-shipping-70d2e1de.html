Yes, ocean freight carries materially more risk than air, and the fact that they didn't want to engage on that question is itself worth noting. The main differences:
Transit time and exposure. Ocean is weeks rather than days, which compounds every other risk — more handling events, more time in port, more weather exposure, more chances for delay from congestion or strikes.
Physical damage. Salt air, humidity swings, container condensation ("container rain"), and vibration over long voyages can degrade goods that travel fine by air. If your cargo is LCL (less-than-container-load, shared with others), it gets handled at consolidation and deconsolidation warehouses on both ends, which is where a lot of damage and pilferage happens.
General Average. This is the one most shippers don't know about and the one I'd push them on specifically. Under maritime law, if the ship encounters a casualty — fire, grounding, having to jettison cargo — all cargo owners are required to contribute proportionally to the salvage and rescue costs, even if your goods were untouched. Your container can be held hostage at port until you post a General Average bond, which can be a percentage of cargo value. This happened to thousands of shippers on the Ever Given and the ONE Apus. Without all-risk marine cargo insurance that includes GA coverage, you can be on the hook for tens of thousands on a small shipment.
Loss. Roughly a thousand containers are lost overboard per year. Rare per-shipment but nonzero. Theft at origin and destination ports is more common.
Practical recommendation: ask Navis specifically (1) whether your shipment is FCL or LCL, (2) whether they're offering all-risk marine cargo insurance with General Average coverage, and (3) what the declared value basis is for any liability they carry as a forwarder — their default carrier liability under the Hague-Visby rules is trivially low, often around $500 per package or a per-kilo amount. If they can't answer those, that tells you something.
What's the route and roughly what's being shipped? I can give you a more specific read on which of these actually matter for your situation.

Air has different risks, generally less severe but not zero:
Physical damage is lower — shorter transit, fewer handling events, no salt or sustained humidity. But cargo holds get cold (modern freighters mostly have climate control, but not all do), pressure changes at altitude can affect sealed containers and stretched canvases, and theft at hub airports is real though typically less than at ports. The bigger gotcha is liability: under the Montreal Convention, air carrier liability caps at about 22 SDR per kilo (~$30/kg). For art worth far more than its weight, you need declared-value insurance regardless of mode — the carrier's baseline coverage is meaningless for valuable pieces.
On savings: ocean LCL typically runs 40–70% cheaper than air for household goods, because LCL is priced by volume and household goods are bulky-but-light. Your $7K air quote probably comes back as $2.5–4K by ocean, depending on cube-to-weight ratio. Dense cargo (books, tools) saves less; bulky cargo (furniture, frames) saves more. Add roughly $300–600 for marine cargo insurance with General Average coverage, which you should not skip.
The complication is that you're shipping art. Weeks in a container through the Black Sea route to Poti or Batumi — humidity swings, temperature cycling, vibration — is rough on canvas, paper, and wood, even well-crated. I'd push back on ocean for the art portion specifically. The play worth asking Navis about is splitting it: art and sensitive items by air, bulk household goods by ocean. That usually beats either single-mode quote on total landed cost, and it isolates the risk to the cheaper-to-replace stuff.
