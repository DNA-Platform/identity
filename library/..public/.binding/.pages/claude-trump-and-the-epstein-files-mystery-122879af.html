That's the sharper version of the argument, and it's harder to dismiss.
The weak form of the distraction theory is: Trump did X to distract from Epstein. That's episodic and hard to prove — you need tight timing and intentionality.
The strong form — which is what you're pointing at — is structural: the news cycle has been in a state ofcontinuous manufactured emergency since roughly January 2025. Greenland, Canada, Panama, Venezuela, tariffs, Iran, whatever comes next. The specific trigger for each move may or may not be Epstein. But the cumulative effect is that no single story — including one that might otherwise be existentially damaging — ever gets more than 48-72 hours of sustained national focus before the next thing lands.
That's a fundamentally different machine. It doesn't require Trump to have said "launch the Greenland thing to kill the Epstein story." It just requires that the administration has discovered, or stumbled into, a mode of operation where the throughput of alarming geopolitical news exceeds the media's capacity to sustain any single thread.
And the Epstein story is specifically vulnerable to this because it requires sustained attention to do damage. It's not a simple fact — "he did X." It's a complex document story, 3.5 million pages, unfolding across months, requiring people to hold context across news cycles. That kind of story gets destroyed by noise. A tariff war, a territorial threat, a military action — those reset the front page immediately.
The question you can't fully answer from the outside is whether this is strategy or just Trump's natural operating mode that happens to have the same effect. Probably both, and probably the distinction doesn't matter much.
