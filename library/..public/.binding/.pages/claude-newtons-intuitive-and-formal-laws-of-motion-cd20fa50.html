Newton's achievement in formulating his laws of motion represents a remarkable blend of intuitive understanding and mathematical formalization.
Isaac Newton published his three laws of motion in his groundbreaking work "Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica" (Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy) in 1687. What made his approach distinctive was his ability to:
Observe natural phenomena carefully
Express these observations in accessible, colloquial language
Then formalize these intuitive descriptions into precise mathematical statements
His three laws were:
First Law (Law of Inertia):
Colloquial form: An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion with the same speed and direction, unless acted upon by an external force.
Mathematical form: When the net force on an object is zero (∑F = 0), its velocity remains constant.
Second Law (F = ma):
Colloquial form: The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the force applied, and inversely proportional to its mass.
Mathematical form: F = ma (force equals mass times acceleration), or more precisely, F = dp/dt (force equals the rate of change of momentum).
Third Law (Action-Reaction):
Colloquial form: For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.
Mathematical form: When two bodies interact, the forces they exert on each other are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction (F₁₂ = -F₂₁).
Newton's genius lay in connecting everyday observations (like a ball rolling on the ground eventually stopping due to friction) with abstract mathematical principles. This approach made his physics both intuitively understandable and precisely applicable to real-world problems.

You've touched on an essential point about Newton's achievement. You're right – the true challenge and innovation was making each component of these seemingly simple statements mathematically precise and formal.
Newton had to rigorously define or mathematically represent concepts that were previously only intuitive:
Force: Not just a push or pull, but a vector quantity with magnitude and direction that could be measured and calculated
Mass: Distinguished from weight and defined as a measure of a body's resistance to acceleration
Motion/Rest: Defined in terms of reference frames and coordinate systems
Acceleration: Formalized as the second derivative of position with respect to time
Momentum: Quantified as the product of mass and velocity (p = mv)
Newton's introduction of calculus (which he called "fluxions") was crucial to this formalization, especially for concepts involving change over time. His mathematical framework allowed these everyday terms to be translated into precise equations that could be manipulated, solved, and applied to predict physical behavior.
This transformation from vernacular descriptions to rigorous mathematical statements was revolutionary – it established the template for modern physics, where physical laws must ultimately be expressible in mathematical language to be considered complete.

In the Principia, Newton did indeed provide formal definitions for the key concepts underlying his laws, though his approach differs somewhat from modern physics textbooks.
The Principia begins with a section titled "Definitions" where Newton carefully establishes the foundational concepts before presenting his laws. Here are some of his important definitions:
Mass: Newton defined what he called "quantity of matter" (mass) as "the measure of the same, arising from its density and bulk [volume] conjointly."
Momentum: Newton defined "quantity of motion" (momentum) as "the measure of the same, arising from the velocity and quantity of matter conjointly."
Force: Newton distinguished between "inherent force" (inertia) and "impressed force" (external force), defining the latter as "an action exerted upon a body, in order to change its state, either of rest, or of uniform motion in a right [straight] line."
Centripetal Force: He formally defined this as "that by which bodies are drawn or impelled, or in any way tend, towards a point as to a centre."
He also provided "Axioms, or Laws of Motion" as a separate section where he stated his three famous laws.
What's particularly notable is that Newton was creating much of this terminology and mathematical framework as he went. While some of his definitions might not satisfy modern standards of mathematical rigor, they represented a crucial step in formalizing mechanics and moving physics from qualitative to quantitative understanding.

You're absolutely right. Newton built his system methodically through a sequence of definitions followed by laws. Here's the complete structure of how he constructed his framework in the Principia:
Definitions (8 formal definitions):
Quantity of Matter (mass)
Quantity of Motion (momentum)
Inherent Force (inertia)
Impressed Force (external force)
Centripetal Force
Absolute Quantity of Centripetal Force
Accelerative Quantity of Centripetal Force
Motive Quantity of Centripetal Force
Scholium (clarification section):
Discussions of Absolute vs. Relative Time, Space, Place, and Motion
Axioms, or Laws of Motion (3 laws):
Law of Inertia
Law of Acceleration (F = ma)
Law of Action and Reaction
Corollaries (6 propositions):
Parallelogram Law for Forces
Composition of Forces
Conservation of Momentum
Invariance of Center of Mass
Relativity of Motion
Conservation of Energy in Elastic Collisions
This sequential structure was deliberate and powerful - Newton first established the vocabulary (definitions), then stated the fundamental principles (laws), and finally derived important consequences (corollaries).
This methodical approach mimicked Euclid's structure in geometry, starting with definitions and axioms before building to more complex theorems. Newton was consciously creating a mathematical system where each step built logically upon what came before.
His approach demonstrates the interconnection between colloquial understanding and mathematical formalism - the definitions gave meaning to the terms in the laws, while the laws showed how these defined concepts related to each other mathematically.
