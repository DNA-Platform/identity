Short answer on labeling: no. Semaglutide's approved indications are T2D glycemic control (Ozempic/Rybelsus), chronic weight management (Wegovy), MACE reduction, and more recently CKD and HFpEF. Antipsychotic-induced weight gain (AIWG) is entirely off-label — but it's now the best-evidenced off-label use in psychiatry, and in your case the T2D indication makes the prescription on-label regardless of the motivating reason.
The evidence base, in order of strength:
The pivotal trial is Danish (Ganeshalingam et al.), published in JAMA Psychiatry in September 2025: 154 adults with schizophrenia, prediabetes, and overweight/obesity on second-generation antipsychotics, randomized to 30 weeks of semaglutide titrated to 1 mg/week or placebo; the semaglutide arm lost roughly 9 kg more than placebo, with greater reductions in glucose and triglycerides and improved physical quality of life. Notably that's at 1 mg — below the 2.4 mg obesity dose — so the ceiling is probably higher.
Before that: liraglutide 1.8 mg in olanzapine/clozapine-treated patients (Larsen 2017, JAMA Psychiatry) produced ~5.3 kg placebo-adjusted loss over 16 weeks and normalized glucose tolerance in ~64% vs 16%. Exenatide trials were largely negative.
Observational: the CAMH inpatient chart review and case series, plus MOSA, an open-label Dutch cohort comparing oral semaglutide (4.5 kg at 16 weeks) against metformin (2.9 kg) — small, non-randomized, but directionally consistent. A 36-week clozapine RCT (COaST) at 2.0 mg is also running.
Quetiapine specifics. Quetiapine sits in the intermediate tier for metabolic liability — below olanzapine and clozapine, above aripiprazole/lurasidone. The mechanism is mostly H1 antagonism (near-saturating even at 25–100 mg, which is why low-dose "for sleep" quetiapine still drives appetite), plus 5-HT2C antagonism via norquetiapine and M3 effects. Importantly, part of the glycemic hit from this class is weight-independent: direct impairment of hepatic insulin sensitivity and beta-cell function, demonstrated acutely in healthy volunteers. GLP-1 agonism addresses both arms — hypothalamic AgRP/POMC signaling and satiety on one side, incretin-mediated insulin secretion and glucagon suppression on the other.
Two things worth flagging for your combination: semaglutide slows gastric emptying substantially, which can alter Cmax/Tmax of oral psychotropics — relevant for IR quetiapine where sedation timing tracks peak levels, less so for XR. And the suicidality signal that circulated in 2023–24 was reviewed by both FDA and EMA, which found no causal association; the psychiatric-symptom data in the trials above were stable across arms.
Metformin remains the cheaper first line (~3–5 kg, plus DPP-grade diabetes-progression benefit), but with established T2D and family history, semaglutide gives you the weight, glycemic, renal, and cardiovascular endpoints in one agent, and the two are frequently combined.

That reframes it: 400–600 mg is a full therapeutic dose, not the sedative-range exposure, and lifetime use means the metabolic insult is chronic and non-negotiable rather than something to wait out.
The lipid piece is probably the most informative signal here. Quetiapine's dyslipidemia effect runs partly independent of weight. In vitro and rodent work shows quetiapine, like clozapine and olanzapine, activates SREBP-1c/SREBP-2 transcriptional programs in hepatocytes and glia, upregulating lipogenic and cholesterogenic gene expression directly. Clinically this shows up as triglyceride elevation appearing early and out of proportion to BMI change, often with low HDL — the classic mixed/atherogenic pattern rather than isolated LDL. Which pattern your panel shows matters for what tool you reach for: semaglutide drops triglycerides substantially (roughly 20–30% across SUSTAIN and STEP) but moves LDL only marginally (~3–5%). If it's TG-dominant, semaglutide hits the target; if LDL/ApoB-dominant, that's a statin question and semaglutide is adjunctive.
On the diabetes-risk side, your setup — first-degree paternal history plus bilateral second-degree, chronic 5-HT2C/H1/M3 blockade, and quetiapine's direct beta-cell and hepatic insulin-sensitivity effects — stacks genetic and pharmacologic mechanisms that converge on the same pathway. Two prevention datapoints worth knowing: metformin in DPP cut progression to T2D by ~31% over 2.8 years, and in SELECT semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced progression from prediabetes to diabetes by roughly 70%+ alongside a 20% MACE reduction. If you're not yet diabetic, prediabetes/HbA1c 5.7–6.4 is the threshold that gets you into on-label territory in most systems by way of the weight indication rather than the glycemic one.
Because exposure is indefinite, so is the countermeasure. The STEP 1 extension found roughly two-thirds of lost weight regained within a year of stopping semaglutide, with cardiometabolic markers reverting toward baseline. Against a permanent drug-induced drive, discontinuation just re-exposes you.
Things I'd want on a baseline panel before starting, given the specifics: fasting lipids with ApoB (not just calculated LDL, which is unreliable when TGs are high), HbA1c plus fasting insulin for HOMA-IR, ALT/AST and a hepatic steatosis assessment, and eGFR/UACR. The last one matters because if albuminuria is present, FLOW-trial renal benefit becomes an additional argument.
One dose note relevant to your regimen: at 400–600 mg the sedative load is significant, and semaglutide's delayed gastric emptying can shift IR quetiapine's Cmax later and lower — occasionally reported as altered next-morning grogginess. XR formulation is less affected.

Mechanism — two separate things worth separating:
The molecule. Semaglutide is a GLP-1 analog with three modifications from native GLP-1: Ala8→Aib substitution to block DPP-4 cleavage, Lys26 acylated with a C18 diacid via a γGlu-2×OEG spacer, and Arg34 to prevent acylation at the wrong site. The fatty-acid chain drives reversible albumin binding, which both shields it from renal clearance and provides a depot — hence the ~165-hour half-life. At the receptor (class B GPCR, Gs-coupled) it drives cAMP/PKA and Epac2 signaling. Peripherally that means glucose-dependent insulin secretion via closure of KATP channels and enhanced exocytosis, glucagon suppression from alpha cells, and slowed gastric emptying. Centrally — and this is where the weight effect lives — it acts on arcuate nucleus POMC/CART neurons (activation) and indirectly inhibits NPY/AgRP via GABAergic interneurons, plus area postrema and NTS signaling. Given your quetiapine question, the interesting overlap is that this hypothalamic circuitry is downstream of the same feeding pathways that H1 and 5-HT2C blockade dysregulate, so the antagonism is fairly direct rather than merely additive.
The oral formulation. This is the part that's genuinely clever. Peptides don't survive the stomach. Rybelsus co-formulates semaglutide with ~300 mg of SNAC (sodium N-[8-(2-hydroxybenzoyl)amino]caprylate), which does two things at the tablet's immediate microenvironment in the gastric mucosa: it buffers local pH upward, protecting the peptide from pepsin, and it transiently and reversibly increases transcellular permeability of gastric epithelium. Absorption is essentially all gastric and highly localized around the eroding tablet.
The practical cost of that mechanism: bioavailability is roughly 0.4–1%, and it's fragile. Rybelsus must be taken fasting, on waking, with no more than ~120 mL water, and nothing else for 30 minutes. Any food or additional fluid in the stomach dilutes the SNAC gradient and absorption drops sharply. Inter- and intra-patient variability is high. 14 mg oral gives systemic exposure roughly comparable to 0.5 mg injectable — so oral tops out where the injectable begins to be interesting for weight. Also relevant to your 400–600 mg nightly regimen: the dosing window is morning-fasted, which is at least well separated from evening quetiapine.

Circuit level, then:
Magnitudes are not comparable in the way the acute experience suggests. Dextroamphetamine's anorectic effect is large but transient — trials from the era when it was used for obesity showed brisk early loss (~0.5 lb/week) with near-complete tachyphylaxis to appetite suppression within 4–12 weeks, while cardiovascular and arousal effects persisted. Lisdexamfetamine in binge-eating disorder produces roughly 5–6% body weight loss over 11–12 weeks, and that's the most favorable modern dataset. Semaglutide 2.4 mg gives ~15% at 68 weeks with the curve still descending to ~60 weeks and no meaningful tolerance to the satiety effect. That asymmetry — transient vs durable suppression — is the single most important difference, and it's a receptor-trafficking and circuit-adaptation story, not a potency story.
Where they overlap. Both converge on the arcuate nucleus. The clearest evidence is nomenclatural: CART is cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript, named because amphetamine induces it, and CART is co-expressed with POMC in exactly the arcuate neurons semaglutide activates. Both endpoints reduce orexigenic AgRP/NPY tone. Second overlap: mesolimbic reward. GLP-1 receptors are expressed in VTA and nucleus accumbens, and GLP-1 agonism reduces incentive salience of food — that's the same axis amphetamine hits from the dopaminergic side, and it's why semaglutide has signals in alcohol and nicotine use. So "reduced wanting" is a shared phenomenology arrived at from opposite directions: amphetamine by flooding the reward circuit until food is comparatively uninteresting, semaglutide by attenuating the circuit's response to food cues specifically.
Where they diverge, and it matters for your profile. Amphetamine works substantially through energy expenditure — sympathomimetic thermogenesis, increased spontaneous activity — plus intake suppression. Semaglutide is essentially pure intake reduction; resting expenditure falls with lost mass as usual. And metabolically they point opposite directions: catecholamine drive raises hepatic glucose output via glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, increases lipolysis and FFA flux, and does nothing for beta-cell function or lipids. For someone with paternal and bilateral avuncular T2D history, quetiapine-driven SREBP activation, and elevated lipids on panel, a chronic sympathomimetic is working against three of your four problems even while it moves the scale.
There's also the direct pharmacodynamic tension: 400–600 mg quetiapine carries meaningful D2 occupancy plus heavy H1 load, and stimulant plus that combination tends to produce a blunted, unpleasant middle rather than either drug's intended effect.

Correct — none, in the pharmacological sense. No monoamine transporter affinity, no releasing activity, no adrenergic or dopaminergic receptor binding. It's a 4,113-dalton acylated peptide; it doesn't have the physicochemistry to interact with a DAT or NET binding pocket at all. And its CNS penetration is limited and indirect: too large to cross an intact blood-brain barrier, so it acts on circumventricular organs where the barrier is fenestrated — area postrema, subfornical organ, median eminence adjacent to the arcuate — plus vagal afferents. Signal propagates inward from there rather than the drug distributing through cortex.
The subjective profile reflects that. Nothing like arousal, alertness, or drive. If anything the reported experience is flatter — some fatigue early in titration, and "food noise" reduction that people describe as an absence rather than a stimulation. Heart rate does rise modestly (~2–4 bpm across the SUSTAIN/STEP programs), but that's mediated through sinoatrial GLP-1 receptors and autonomic effects, not catecholamine release — and it coexists with blood pressure reduction, which is the opposite direction from any sympathomimetic.
The one place the distinction gets blurred is reward. Semaglutide does modulate mesolimbic circuitry — that's real, and it's why the alcohol- and nicotine-craving signals exist — but modulating a dopaminergic circuit's response to a cue is a different operation from raising synaptic dopamine. Amphetamine raises tone; semaglutide narrows what the tone responds to.
Two practical implications for you: there's no stimulant-side interaction with quetiapine to reason about — the interactions that matter are the gastric-emptying/absorption one and the additive fatigue during titration. And nothing here will offset quetiapine's sedation at 400–600 mg. If daytime blunting is part of what you're weighing, semaglutide isn't a lever on it.

The premise "doesn't cross" is doing too much work — I stated it too flatly. The accurate version is that it doesn't distribute broadly through parenchyma, but the arcuate is one of the places it reaches directly.
Why the arcuate is a special case. It sits immediately dorsal to the median eminence, a circumventricular organ with fenestrated capillaries. The barrier there is not endothelial tight junctions but tanycytes — specialized ependymal glia lining the third ventricle whose end-feet form the actual diffusion boundary, and which are permissive and dynamically regulated. Ventromedial arcuate neurons, including a subset of POMC/CART cells, extend processes into this permeable zone or sit close enough to sample it. So a large peptide can engage arcuate GLP-1 receptors without ever crossing an intact BBB. The same applies to the area postrema and subfornical organ.
Gabery et al. (JCI Insight, 2020) mapped this directly with fluorescently labeled semaglutide in rodents: uptake concentrated in circumventricular structures — area postrema, subfornical organ, and the arcuate/median eminence region — with essentially no broad parenchymal signal. Regions like the paraventricular nucleus and parabrachial nucleus showed neuronal activation without drug present, i.e. recruited transsynaptically rather than pharmacologically.
So there are three access routes, not one:
Direct, at fenestrated sites — arcuate POMC/CART is here.
Vagal afferents → nodose ganglion → NTS, entirely peripheral receptor engagement.
Downstream projection targets activated by (1) and (2), with no drug exposure.
The AgRP/NPY suppression I mentioned earlier is category 3-adjacent — largely indirect, via GABAergic inhibition from activated POMC-adjacent circuitry and local interneurons, rather than direct receptor action on AgRP neurons.
Worth adding: there's a separate endogenous central GLP-1 system — preproglucagon neurons in the NTS projecting to hypothalamus and limbic regions — which is anatomically distinct from what peripheral semaglutide accesses. Some of the reward-circuit effects are likely propagated through that network rather than by the drug reaching VTA directly.

On energy and focus: no, and if anything the vector points slightly negative. Fatigue is a recognized adverse event in the STEP program, running around 10–11% versus placebo, concentrated during titration and partly attributable to the caloric deficit itself. There is no arousal signal, no procognitive signal, no measured attention effect.
The largest test of anything cognition-adjacent was the EVOKE program, and it was negative. Oral semaglutide 14 mg daily for up to 156 weeks in 3,808 biomarker-confirmed early Alzheimer's patients failed to slow clinical progression across global, cognitive, and functional measures, despite producing significant CSF reductions of up to 10% in p-tau181, p-tau217, YKL-40, total tau, and neurogranin, plus reduced plasma hs-CRP. The pooled event analysis for progression to dementia gave a hazard ratio of 0.96 (95% CI 0.86–1.06). That's about as clean a null as the field produces — real biomarker movement, no cognitive translation. Some commentary reads it as evidence that reducing peripheral inflammation isn't sufficient to alter central disease course, which is consistent with the access constraints we were just discussing.
Discovery — three separate threads that converged:
The incretin gap. Perley and Kipnis, 1967: oral glucose produces substantially more insulin release than an intravenous load matched for plasma glucose. That difference — the incretin effect, roughly 50–70% of postprandial insulin — implied an unidentified gut-derived signal.
Finding GLP-1. Cloning of the proglucagon gene in the early 1980s (Habener's group at Mass General; Bell at Novo) revealed the precursor encoded additional glucagon-like sequences. Svetlana Mojsov synthesized and identified the truly bioactive fragment — GLP-1(7-37), not the full-length peptide, which is inert. Her contribution was underrecognized for decades and became a patent dispute. Native GLP-1 turned out to be therapeutically useless: DPP-4 degrades it in 1–2 minutes.
The Gila monster. John Eng at the Bronx VA, working on the observation that venomous lizards have unusually large pancreases, isolated exendin-4 from Heloderma suspectum venom in 1992. It's ~53% homologous to human GLP-1, a potent agonist, and — critically — has a glycine at position 2 that DPP-4 cannot cleave. That became exenatide (Byetta, 2005), the first GLP-1 drug.
Semaglutide is not in the venom lineage. Novo Nordisk took the alternative route: engineer the human peptide for protease resistance and albumin binding. Lotte Bjerre Knudsen's group produced liraglutide (2010, once-daily, C16 acyl chain), then extended the fatty-acid strategy — C18 diacid, longer spacer, Aib8 substitution — to reach a 165-hour half-life. Semaglutide, 2017.
The weight effect was a side finding. The entire program was aimed at glycemia; consistent weight loss in the diabetes trials was noticed, then pursued deliberately, which is how Saxenda and later Wegovy happened.

That framing captures the real structural difference: semaglutide is a hormone mimetic rather than a xenobiotic. Amphetamine's anorexia is a byproduct — you elevate catecholamines globally and appetite suppression falls out alongside arousal, pressor effects, and reinforcement, none of which can be separated because they share the same mechanism. Semaglutide is amplifying a signal the body already uses for exactly this purpose, so the effect profile inherits the endogenous system's anatomy. The dissociation isn't clever engineering; it's what you get when the target is a satiety pathway rather than a neuromodulator pool.
The reward-circuit specificity has a nice experimental signature: GLP-1 agonism reduces the incentive salience of food cues without flattening reward capacity generally. Rodent work shows reduced progressive-ratio breakpoint for palatable food and diminished cue-induced reinstatement, without the anhedonia you'd see from D2 blockade. Patients describe "food noise" quieting rather than pleasure disappearing — food still tastes good, it just stops occupying working memory. That's a fairly unusual pharmacological operation.
Two things that complicate the picture, worth having: some of the intake suppression is genuinely aversive rather than satiating — area postrema GLP-1R mediates nausea and conditioned taste aversion, while hypothalamic and vagal signaling mediate the satiety proper. Separating those is the explicit goal of the next generation: cagrilintide (amylin analog) combined with semaglutide as CagriSema, and GDF15/GFRAL work, both aiming for satiety without the emetic arm. And the reward effects aren't fully food-specific — the alcohol- and nicotine-craving signals suggest the circuit modulation is broader than the metabolic indication, which is either a feature or an unexamined liability depending on what turns up.
The comparison class where this all sits is instructive: leptin was the obvious hormone mimetic and failed on resistance; amylin worked but weakly as pramlintide; PYY couldn't be dosed tolerably. GLP-1 is the one where the pharmacokinetic engineering and the physiology happened to cooperate.

Frequency: once daily, every day. This is the one real trade-off against the injectable — semaglutide's 165-hour half-life means the shot is weekly, but oral bioavailability is so low and so variable that daily dosing is needed to hold steady-state exposure. Miss a dose and you skip it entirely; you don't double up.
The dosing window, which is non-negotiable:
First thing on waking, before anything else — no coffee, no water beforehand.
One tablet with no more than ~120 mL (4 oz) of plain water. Not more, not less. More water dilutes the SNAC gradient at the gastric mucosa and absorption drops; too little and the tablet doesn't disperse properly.
Wait 30 minutes. Nothing at all in that window — no food, no other liquids, and no other oral medications.
After 30 minutes, eat and drink normally.
The 30 minutes is a genuine minimum, not a rounded suggestion; longer is somewhat better for absorption but 30 is where the label lands. Swallow whole — don't split, crush, or chew, since the tablet is engineered to erode in a controlled way that keeps the semaglutide and SNAC co-localized.
Titration. Standard schedule is 3 mg daily for 4 weeks (a tolerability dose, not therapeutic), then 7 mg for 4 weeks, then 14 mg. Don't compress it — the GI adverse effects that make people quit are almost entirely a titration-rate problem. If nausea is significant at a step, hold there an extra 2–4 weeks rather than pushing.
Diet around it. No restriction on what you eat once the window closes — the constraint is purely about the absorption window. But three practical patterns matter once you're at dose: large meals become uncomfortable rather than merely unnecessary, because delayed gastric emptying means volume tolerance drops sharply. Smaller, more frequent meals work better. High-fat meals are the worst offenders for nausea. And protein intake becomes something to be deliberate about, since a substantial fraction of GLP-1 weight loss is lean mass — roughly 1.2–1.6 g/kg is the range usually cited for preserving it during caloric deficit.
One thing specific to your regimen: the 30-minute no-other-medications rule includes anything else you take orally. Since your quetiapine is nightly at 400–600 mg, the two are naturally separated — morning dose, evening dose, no conflict. Anything you currently take with breakfast would need to shift 30 minutes later.

That's the actual question, and it's a specific one: does GLP-1 signaling counteract H1/5-HT2C-driven hunger, or just add a general appetite reduction on top of it?
The evidence says it engages the same circuitry. Antipsychotic hyperphagia works substantially through hypothalamic AMPK activation downstream of H1 blockade, plus loss of 5-HT2C-mediated POMC tone — and GLP-1 agonism acts on POMC/CART neurons directly and suppresses hypothalamic AMPK. So it's antagonism at the node, not a parallel offset. The clinical data are consistent with that: the Danish semaglutide trial produced ~9 kg placebo-adjusted in patients who were on ongoing antipsychotics throughout, and Larsen's liraglutide trial did the same in olanzapine/clozapine patients. The drug wasn't fighting the antipsychotic to a draw; it was producing near-normal weight response in people whose drive was pharmacologically elevated.
What that doesn't tell you is whether it covers the specific nocturnal phenotype you're describing. Those trials measured weight, not evening-specific eating, and quetiapine at 400–600 mg drives a sedation-adjacent, compulsive-feeling hunger in the hour before sleep that's phenomenologically distinct from daytime appetite. The flat 24-hour exposure means coverage is at least continuous, which is the right property. But nobody has published on that phenotype specifically.
The sleep-med substitution point is the part I don't have literature for. If what you previously relied on was sedation that got you past the window rather than appetite suppression per se, semaglutide replaces a different mechanism than the one you lost.
