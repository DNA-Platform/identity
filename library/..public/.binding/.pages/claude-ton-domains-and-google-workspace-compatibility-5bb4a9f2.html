Short answer: No, .TON domains won't work with Google Workspace.
.TON is a blockchain-based naming system on The Open Network (Telegram's blockchain), not an ICANN-recognized top-level domain in the traditional DNS system. TON DNS allows you to assign short, human-readable names to crypto wallets or websites — it translates names like test.ton into TON smart contract addresses and ADNL addresses. These domains exist on-chain as NFTs and don't resolve through the standard internet DNS infrastructure that Google Workspace relies on.
Google Workspace requires a domain that lives in the global ICANN DNS system, because it needs you to add MX records, TXT verification records, SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records to prove ownership and route email. Since .ton domains don't have traditional DNS zone files you can edit with an ICANN registrar, there's no way to complete that setup.
As for which TLDs Google Workspace supports: Google doesn't publish a restrictive whitelist. It works with essentially any ICANN-recognized TLD — that's over 1,500 domain endings including all the classics (.com, .net, .org, .edu), country codes (.uk, .de, .jp, .io), and the newer gTLDs (.tech, .ai, .xyz, .app, .dev, .cloud, .store, etc.). The only requirements are that the domain exists in the standard DNS, you can modify its DNS records, and it's not in a country Google doesn't serve.
The specific things that won't work include:
Blockchain domains (.ton, .eth, .sol, .crypto) — these aren't in ICANN DNS
Domains containing "gmail" or "google" in the name
Brand TLDs restricted to their owner (.google, .apple, .bmw)
Domains already associated with another Google Workspace account
So if you're interested in a TON-related presence, you'd need a traditional domain (like something.com or something.ton.xyz) for Google Workspace, and could use your .ton domain separately for wallet/blockchain purposes.

Ah, got it — two different questions then.
How many TLDs exist? There are currently over 1,500 ICANN-recognized TLDs. IANA (the authority under ICANN) maintains the definitive root zone database at https://www.iana.org/domains/root/db — that's the complete, canonical list of every suffix that exists in the DNS. Can you create your own custom suffix? Yes, but it's extremely expensive. ICANN runs a New gTLD Program where organizations can apply to operate their own TLD. The last round was in 2012, and the next application window is expected to open around April 2026. The application fee alone is around $185,000, plus you need to demonstrate the technical and financial capacity to run a registry. That's how all those newer TLDs like .love, .app, .dev came into existence — companies applied to ICANN and became registry operators.
So for practical purposes, you're limited to picking from the ~1,500 existing suffixes. You can't just invent dna.whatever at a normal registrar — the suffix has to already exist in the root zone. The good news is that with 1,500+ options, there's a huge selection, and for a strong three-letter name like "dna," many of the more obscure or newer TLDs might still have it available at standard registration prices (typically $5–$50/year), unlike the premium TLDs where three-letter names are long gone or cost thousands on the aftermarket.

Claude
16:37 UTC, 11 February 2026
You can't, really — and that's kind of the uncomfortable truth of the domain system. Nobody owns a domain. Every domain in existence is rented from a registry on an annual basis. Even Google rents google.com from Verisign year after year.
But there are two very different kinds of "renting":
1. Renting from the registry (the normal way) You register dna.cool or whatever directly through a registrar like Namecheap or Porkbun. You're the registrant on record. You pay $10–50/year depending on the TLD. You have full control, can transfer it, and as long as you keep renewing, it's yours. This is as close to "owning" as it gets.
2. Renting from another person (your dna.love situation) Someone else is the registrant. They're charging you a premium to use it. You're dependent on them. If they decide not to renew the arrangement, you lose it.
So the real move isn't "how do I own a domain" — it's "how do I stop being in situation #2 and get into situation #1." Which is exactly what you're already doing by looking for a new TLD where you can register dna.{something} directly at standard pricing.
That's why finding the right suffix matters so much here. Once you register it yourself, you're the registrant, you pay the registry rate, and nobody can take it from you as long as you renew.

Yes, you can. Google Workspace supports up to 600 domains on one account. Here's how the transition works:
Add your new domain (say dna.codes or whatever you pick) as a secondary domain in Admin Console → Account → Domains → Manage Domains
Verify it by adding a TXT record at your new registrar
Set up MX, SPF, DKIM, DMARC records so email flows properly on the new domain
Change your primary domain — once the new domain is verified, you can swap it to be the primary. Go to Manage Domains and promote the secondary to primary.
Update each user's email — in the Admin Console you can rename users to the new domain. So doug@dna.love becomes doug@dna.codes (or whatever).
The important things to know:
The old emails still work. After you swap, Google automatically creates aliases so that mail sent to your old @dna.love addresses still arrives. This keeps working as long as you still control that domain.
Once you stop leasing dna.love, those aliases die. So you'll want a transition period where you have both — send from the new one, still receive on the old one, and update your contacts/accounts everywhere.
Google doesn't charge extra for adding the second domain.
So the play is: add the new domain now while you're still leasing dna.love, run both in parallel for a few months, update everything that matters, then drop the lease.

Mailing lists and newsletters — you'll just have to resubscribe

Change the important stuff first — banking, cloud services, anything with 2FA

Use your old @dna.love inbox as a detector — every time something still arrives there, that's a reminder to go update that service
Once the old inbox goes quiet, you know you've caught everything
