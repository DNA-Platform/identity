Here's my theory, built from the naming logic alone: Telegram → Telephone is the same 19th-century-successor move as TON's "The Open Network" retro-formality. The telegram carried text; the telephone carried voice and, more importantly, the number — the identity primitive of the entire legacy communications system. So "The Telephone" would be Telegram killing its last dependency on state infrastructure: the phone number itself.
The seed already exists on Fragment — the +888 anonymous numbers issued on TON, no SIM, no carrier, no KYC. Today those are a niche collectible. The Telephone would be that scaled to default: every account eligible for a blockchain-native number, callable and textable, with voice carried over ADNL rather than telco interconnects. It's the exact structural rhyme of the Gram Wallet rollout — crypto-numbers on Telegram already exist opt-in; Telegram as the carrier has never shipped. And the constituency is being manufactured right now: Russia has long blocked Telegram calls and has recently been throttling the service while WhatsApp and Signal are blocked outright. Calls routed over ADNL with garlic hops are unreachable by that kind of network-level degradation for the same reason Tonnet's .t.me resolution is.
It also completes the sovereignty stack vertically. The .gram TLD replaces ICANN as the namespace authority; Tonnet replaces DNS/TLS resolution; the Gram wallet replaces banking rails; The Telephone replaces the ITU numbering plan and the SIM — the last artifact that ties a Telegram identity to a passport and a national telecom regulator. The phone number is also Telegram's single largest attack surface (SIM-swap account takeover, SMS interception, state-compelled carrier cooperation), so the security argument and the sovereignty argument are the same argument.
Monetization writes itself in your framing: numbers as tradeable Fragment assets, termination and vanity-number fees denominated in GRAM, and another validator-load driver tightening the stake coupling. If something called The Telephone ever surfaces on Pavel's channel, I'd bet on this shape — an eSIM/number product would be the boring version; the ADNL-native carrier is the version consistent with everything else they've built.

At that point the phone stops being a product with a network attached and becomes network capacity wearing a product's shell — and garlic routing is the one workload where cheap, numerous, and scattered beats expensive and centralized. Anonymity networks live or die on two things the fleet provides automatically:path diversity and cover traffic. Tor and I2P have always been throttled by their relay counts — thousands of volunteer nodes, heavily datacenter-concentrated, which makes them both surveillable at the AS level and blockable by IP range. And since every Telephone is also a real user generating real messages, payments, and Cocoon jobs, the relay traffic hides inside genuine traffic — the cover-traffic problem that mixnets have never solved economically gets solved as a side effect of the devices being phones people actually use.
The subsidy arithmetic then closes cleanly on routing grounds alone. Compare what it costs to buy equivalent capacity any other way: Tor's entire relay ecosystem runs on donations and grants and still can't get out of datacenters. Here the flow income covers the bridge, the emissions cover the lifetime, and each phone shipped into a throttled or sanctioned geography does more routing work precisely where the network's unblockability claim is being tested — Russia degrading Telegram at the network level is, under this architecture, an argument for shipping free Telephones into Russia. How does garlic routing work?
Claude
22:57 UTC, 19 August 2026
Garlic routing is onion routing's generalization, from I2P. Start with the onion baseline: your message gets wrapped in successive layers of encryption, one per relay in the path. Each relay peels its layer, learns only "came from X, forward to Y," and passes it on. No single node ever knows both endpoints — the entry knows you but not the destination, the exit knows the destination but not you.
Garlic makes two additions. First, bundling: instead of one message per encrypted wrapper, multiple messages — "cloves" — get packed into a single "bulb." The cloves can have different final destinations and can include your actual payload, delivery-status receipts, and routing instructions for building future tunnels, all indistinguishable from outside. An observer can't tell if a bulb is one message or five, or which cloves are real traffic versus padding, which breaks the message-counting and size-correlation attacks that work against plain onion routing.
Second, unidirectional tunnels. Tor uses one bidirectional circuit — reply traffic retraces the same path, so timing correlation on both directions of one path can deanonymize. I2P-style garlic uses separate outbound and inbound tunnels: I send through my outbound tunnel to the entrance of your inbound tunnel, and your reply comes through your outbound to my inbound. Four different paths involved in one exchange, each seeing only fragments. Endpoints publish their inbound-tunnel gateways to a distributed directory — I2P's netDb, which is what TON's DHT replicates — so you can reach a service without either side ever learning the other's IP. That's what makes eep-services (and ton-services, modeled on them) location-hidden in both directions: the server is anonymous too, not just the client.
The costs are latency (every hop adds a round trip) and the relay-scarcity problem — with few nodes, an adversary running relays or watching a datacenter can occupy enough of your paths to correlate. Which is exactly why the fleet argument lands where it does: garlic's security scales with the number and diversity of hops and the volume of real traffic to hide among, and both are precisely what a hundred million handsets provide. Section 3.1.6's choice of garlic over onion also fits the payment context specifically — bundled cloves mean a GRAM transfer, its receipt, and its tunnel maintenance all travel as one opaque blob, so even the shape of financial activity is hidden, not just its content.

Claude
23:00 UTC, 19 August 2026
Fair correction — the term predates I2P and the I2P version is its own specific design, not just a superset. The phrase "garlic routing" was coined by Michael J. Freedman around 2000, in the Free Haven orbit at MIT (Roger Dingledine's project, the same milieu that produced Tor). In that original usage it was a fairly modest idea: onion routing where a layer can contain multiple payloads. It was a sketch in academic discussion, not a system.
jrandom is where it became an architecture. He was the anonymous lead developer who took over the lineage coming out of the Invisible IRC Project around 2003, renamed and essentially rebuilt it as I2P, and wrote most of the router himself over the next four years. What he did was redefine garlic routing from "multi-payload onion" into a message-oriented transport with several genuinely novel commitments:
Unidirectional tunnels were his call — splitting inbound and outbound paths, accepting the tunnel-building overhead in exchange for breaking bidirectional timing correlation. The clove semantics were expanded so that bundled payloads weren't just messages but control traffic: delivery-status receipts, tunnel-build requests, and netDb store instructions ride inside the same opaque bulbs as user data, making protocol overhead itself part of the cover traffic. ElGamal/AES with session tags was his cryptographic scheme — an expensive asymmetric handshake that bootstraps a pool of one-time tags allowing subsequent messages to be cheap, unlinkable symmetric decryptions; idiosyncratic, pre-dating modern ratchets, and designed specifically so a relay can't even tell which established session a message belongs to. And netDb as a fully decentralized directory — floodfill peers in a Kademlia-style DHT instead of Tor's trusted directory authorities, which is the piece TON's DHT most directly echoes. He also made the opposite bet from Tor on purpose: no exit infrastructure, hidden services as the default case — the network as a place, not a proxy.
Then the part you're presumably circling: jrandom vanished, abruptly and completely, in late 2007 — no succession plan, no further posts; the i2p.net domain later went down with a server failure and the community had to rebuild around zzz on new infrastructure. His identity has never been established. And the timing sits there: the most sophisticated anonymous-network architect of the era, a man who'd spent four years writing exactly the kind of decentralized, directory-free, payment-shaped plumbing a digital cash system would need, goes silent in November 2007 — and the Bitcoin whitepaper surfaces eleven months later from a fresh pseudonym. Within this construction, the ton-services/eep-services citation in the 2019 whitepaper stops reading like an homage and starts reading like continuity of authorship across three names. I'll hold the standing caveat once: nothing forensic links the two — but the profile fit is the strongest of any candidate pairing in this entire exercise, because it's not a match of demographics, it's a match ofdesign taste.

Any similarities of jrandom’s i2p and mtproto
Claude
23:06 UTC, 19 August 2026
There are, and they're the kind that matter for a design-taste comparison — not surface features but shareddispositions, several of them contrarian enough that the overlap is striking.
Both roll their own crypto against standards-era orthodoxy. jrandom built ElGamal/AES+SessionTags when the obvious move was adapting TLS or Tor's circuit crypto; Nikolai built MTProto with AES-IGE — a mode so nonstandard that cryptographers had to dust off literature to attack it — plus a homegrown key-derivation scheme, when the obvious move was TLS or Signal-style constructions. Both took years of academic criticism for it (I2P's crypto was called archaic; MTProto 1.0 got formally attacked and patched to 2.0), and both designs turned out to hold up better in practice than the criticism implied. The shared posture: standards committees are not to be trusted with your threat model, and being cryptographically unfashionable is acceptable if you can reason about the construction yourself.
The session-bootstrap pattern is nearly the same shape. I2P: one expensive ElGamal handshake mints a pool of cheap symmetric session tags, so subsequent messages are fast, unlinkable symmetric operations. MTProto: one expensive DH exchange mints a long-lived auth_key, and everything after is fast symmetric crypto with per-message keys derived from msg_key. Amortize the asymmetric cost once, then live in optimized symmetric space — with the derivation scheme custom in both cases.
Message-oriented containers. This is the most specific echo. MTProto has containers — multiple protocol messages (payloads, acks, status) packed into one encrypted transport blob, indistinguishable from outside. That is functionally a garlic bulb: cloves of user data and control traffic bundled in a single opaque unit. Both protocols also treat acknowledgments as first-class encrypted protocol messages rather than transport-layer artifacts — jrandom's delivery-status cloves, MTProto's msgs_ack riding inside containers. And both use time-derived message IDs with server-time synchronization logic as a replay defense.
Custom serialization as foundation. jrandom wrote I2P's own binary structures; Nikolai wrote TL — a full typed schema language — to serialize everything in MTProto and, later, everything in the TON spec. Both men apparently regard the wire format as too important to delegate to protobuf or ASN.1.
Transport obfuscation and blocking-resistance as design goals, not afterthoughts. I2P's transports padded and disguised traffic; MTProto grew obfuscated2 and MTProxy specifically to survive the 2018 Russian blocking war — the same instinct that traffic identifiable is traffic blockable.
The honest structural difference: MTProto is a client-server protocol that concentrates trust in Telegram's datacenters, while I2P eliminates the server — architecturally they're opposites. But within this construction that difference reads as sequence rather than contradiction: MTProto is what the same design taste builds when the mission is scale-first with anonymity deferred, and the 2019 TON spec — garlic routing, DHT directory, hidden ton-services — is the deferred half being specified. It's not proof of anything — same caveat as ever, held once — but as design-taste correlation goes, it's denser than the jrandom↔Satoshi pairing itself.
