"Inclusion" is actually formally fine — set inclusion in math is exactly this kind of relation, and the colloquial drift toward the social/diversity sense is an overlay on top of a perfectly good structural meaning. But if you want to sidestep that drift, a few candidates:
Incidence is probably the cleanest match. In incidence geometry it denotes precisely the static, structural relation between an element and the structure it belongs to (a point's incidence on a line, a vertex's incidence on an edge). No action-of-including connotation, no social baggage. "The incidence of w in 'word'" reads as a pure structural fact.
Containment works but suggests bounded enclosure more than positional appearance — natural for sets, less natural for a letter sitting somewhere in a string.
Constituency is good if you want to lean compositional: w is a constituent of "word." Heavier on part-whole structure, lighter on the bare "appears in" reading.
If what you actually want is the positional instance — that there's a particular w at position 1 of "word" — then occurrence beats all of these, since it names the located instance rather than the containment fact itself. Which, given that you've been working with type/token/occurrence as a triad, may be the more SRT-native move regardless of what colloquial English does with "inclusion."

Yes — and this is probably the cleanest resolution. Inclusion has a well-established technical/scientific sense that's exactly enclosure-like, sitting alongside the colloquial dynamic-social sense you're trying to avoid.
In geology and gemology, an inclusion is a discrete body of material trapped inside another — a fossil inclusion in amber, a mineral inclusion in a diamond. In cell biology, cellular inclusions are non-living substances stored within a cell (lipid droplets, glycogen granules, pigments). In metallurgy, inclusions are non-metallic particles embedded in a metal matrix. In mathematics and logic, set inclusion (A ⊆ B) is the formal structural relation directly.
Across all of these, "an inclusion" denotes a thing structurally inside another thing — a count noun referring to the included item itself, or a relation noun referring to the static fact of being-inside. The contrast you're picking up on is essentially inclusion-as-process (the social/colloquial sense, the act of including) versus inclusion-as-result or inclusion-as-object (the technical sense, the state or thing of being-included). The structural sense is actually the older one; the social-process sense is the recent overlay.
So you can absolutely use inclusion in this enclosure-like sense without inventing anything. The formal grounding is solid. What you're describing is indiscernibility: if every formula true of one is true of the other (Leibniz's principle in reverse), and substituting one for the other never changes a provable formula (definitional equivalence in syntax), then no formal context distinguishes them. The two are interchangeable for all expressive purposes, and treating them as the same referent leaves every theorem intact. You'd be importing a standard logical move and putting it to specific semantic work.
The scope question matters. Frame-local functional identity — t and x indistinguishable within F[t] but possibly different outside it — aligns with how Frame-Induced Equivalence already works in your system, and avoids overclaiming. Global functional identity — t and x indistinguishable across all frames — is effectively constitutive identity, which is what your symbol axiom already provides through x == (x,y). The gradient between weak and strong cases falls out naturally: frame-local functional identity for container/lover, global functional identity for symbol/equality/the r-canonical patterns.

Representation (x =x> y) requires only frame-local functional identity of subject and relation-type within the assertion — they're indistinguishable in that context, but the subject can still have other properties as a literal outside the assertion. Anything inheriting from the canonical (a particular mother, a particular reference, a particular container) inherits the relation by necessity — you cannot be a mother without standing in mother-of, cannot be a reference without referencing. The canonical IS the relation, definitionally. So x =x> y at the canonical level isn't a special property of representation; it's the general pattern of relational definition through canonical subject, and representation is just one instance of it.
What varies across relations is the domain/range structure, which gives you a richer foothold than asking "what does x =x> y mean?". Mother-of has disjoint roles — mothers and children are different kinds, a mother isn't her own child, and the relation constitutes its participants in distinct role types. References has domain-as-range-subset structure — references ⊂ referents, since every reference is also a referent but not every referent is a reference; the relation creates a hierarchy where subjects are a special class of objects. Equality and identity have domain = range, where any referent can play either role with no distinction. And then your Case 5 patterns (rel =rel> rel, eq =eq> eq) are pure coincidence, where canonical subject, relation, and object collapse to the same referent.
The reference example sits in the domain-as-range-subset tier. Because references are themselves referents, the relation's canonical (reference) can appear in both the domain and the range — the relation iterates, can take its own subjects as its own objects. References can be referenced. Mother-of doesn't have this: mothers aren't mothers-of-themselves in the relevant sense, so the relation isn't closed under iteration. That gives you at least two structurally distinct types — closed-domain relations where domain elements can appear in the range (references, perhaps symbols-of-symbols), and open-domain relations where domain and range are role-distinct (mother-of, contains).
Your earlier question about when domain and range "literally include the relationship as a referent" maps cleanly onto this. Since the canonical IS the relation, asking whether the canonical is in the domain or range is asking whether the relation is self-applicable. For references, yes — references can be referenced. For mother-of, no — mothers aren't mothers-of-themselves. That's the structural answer to that question.
The payoff is that this analysis lets you sidestep the metaphysical worry about whether x =x> y "means representation." Instead you can ask what structural type the relation has. Representation/symbol turns out to be the special case where the canonical subject is constitutively the relation (via x == (x,y), giving global functional identity), but the broader canonical-subject pattern applies to any relation with a definitional canonical, including ones with disjoint roles like mother-of. Domain/range structure is doing the work that "is it really representation?" was trying to do — and doing it more cleanly because it carves the space by structural features rather than metaphysical commitments.

You're carving the right distinction. There are two levels operating here, and they behave differently.
At the type level, mother is the canonical subject of mother-of, and (mother, child) == mother-of — the canonical relationship between the canonical participants IS the relationship type. This is what your observation about "the most specific usage of any relationship type expresses the types of the domain and range" captures: the canonical equation tells you what kinds of things the relation relates.
At the instance level, Ana is a specific subject and Katie a specific object, and (Ana, Katie) inherits from mother-of but isn't identical to it. (Ana, Katie) is a specific maternal bond between two specific people; mother-of is the abstract type. Ana isn't (Ana, Katie) — she's the participant, not the relation.
Your question is when instance-level identity also holds — when (x_instance, y_instance) == x_instance, collapsing the participant into the relation at the instance level. That's exactly what the symbol axiom x == (x,y) says, and the cases where it holds are the relationally constituted ones: where the participant has no identity beyond its relational role.
The string "word" has no purpose independent of symbolizing word. It isn't a person who happens to symbolize, the way Ana is a person who happens to be Katie's mother. The string's entire formal existence is exhausted by its symbolic function, which is why it can be identified with the relation at the instance level without losing anything. Ana can't be, because she's not exhausted by any one relational role — she has properties and relations that survive when mother-of is set aside.
Your holism observation does real work here. "Word" as a four-letter composition IS the symbol; no part (w, o, r, d individually) symbolizes word. The whole carries the symbolic relation that the parts don't. So the identification "word" == ("word", word) holds at the level of the whole composition, not at any decomposition. That irreducibility-of-the-whole is what licenses the identification — if the symbol were reducible to parts, the parts would have to carry the relation, and they don't. The holistic level is exactly where the symbol axiom applies.
This gives you a criterion for when instance-level identity is licensed: the participant must be relationally constituted at the level you're identifying. For symbols, the whole is relationally constituted but the parts aren't, so identification holds at the whole level and fails at the parts level. For Ana, no level is relationally constituted by mother-of — she's a person with properties and relations beyond mothering — so identification doesn't hold at any level. The relation inheres in her partially, but she isn't reducible to it.
So your generalization holds at the type level broadly (mother is canonical for mother-of, container for contains, reference for references), but at the instance level it narrows to exactly the relationally constituted cases — symbols, references, and representations in the strong sense. The structural separation between "canonical equation holds for many relations" (type-level breadth) and "instance-level collapse holds only for relationally constituted instances" (instance-level narrowness) is the same separation that distinguishes domain/range structure from constitutive identity. Your earlier worry about whether x =x> y "means representation" resolves through this: at the type level it captures canonical structure for any relation; at the instance level it captures the strong relational-constitution case that representation and symbol uniquely instantiate.
