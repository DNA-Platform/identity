Great question. There are several safe, government-backed options in the US, and yes — there's a specific instrument that fits your description perfectly.
The "special" one: Series I Savings Bonds (I Bonds)
This is almost certainly what you're thinking of. Key features:
Each person can purchase up to $10,000 per year electronically through TreasuryDirect.gov (plus an additional $5,000 via tax refund in paper form). The rate is a composite of a fixed rate plus a variable inflation-adjustment rate that resets every six months. Your money is locked up for 1 year minimum — you literally cannot redeem them before that. If you redeem before 5 years, you forfeit the last 3 months of interest. After 5 years, no penalty. They mature at 30 years. They're backed by the full faith and credit of the US government, and the interest is exempt from state and local taxes.
They became extremely popular in 2022 when inflation spiked and the composite rate hit ~9.6%. The rates fluctuate, so they're more or less attractive depending on the inflation environment.
Other safe options in the same family:
Treasury Bills, Notes, and Bonds — You can buy these directly through TreasuryDirect or via a brokerage. T-Bills are short-term (4 weeks to 1 year), Notes are 2–10 years, Bonds are 20–30 years. All pay fixed interest and are backed by the US government. These are more liquid than I Bonds.
TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) — Similar inflation protection to I Bonds but tradeable on secondary markets. The principal adjusts with CPI. Available in 5, 10, and 30-year maturities. No annual purchase cap like I Bonds.
CDs (Certificates of Deposit) — Offered by banks, FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor per institution. You lock money up for a set term (3 months to 5 years typically) at a fixed rate. Less flexible than Treasuries, and the interest is subject to state tax. Rates vary by bank — online banks and credit unions often offer better rates than the big banks.
High-Yield Savings Accounts — Not locked up at all, but worth mentioning since FDIC-insured accounts at online banks have been offering competitive rates recently.
Given your situation living outside the US, a few things worth noting: TreasuryDirect requires a US address and SSN/TIN. I Bonds specifically require you to be a US citizen, resident, or civilian employee of the US government. If you're maintaining US tax residency, you should have access to all of these — but the logistics of TreasuryDirect from abroad can occasionally be clunky.
The I Bond is the classic "buy a little each year, it's cheap, it's locked up, and it's safe" instrument. Is that what you were thinking of, or were you looking for something with different characteristics?

Good catch, and yes everything quoted is annualized.
What you're seeing is called an inverted yield curve, and it's one of the most watched signals in finance. Normally you'd expect to get paid more for locking your money up longer — that's the intuitive "term premium." But right now the short end is paying more than parts of the longer end, and there's a straightforward reason.
The Fed funds rate is currently 3.50–3.75%. Short-term instruments like 3-month T-Bills track very closely to that rate — they're basically lending money to the government for 90 days, and the going price for short-term money is set by the Fed. So you get ~3.70% on a 3-month bill, which makes sense.
But the 2-year note is at ~3.50%, which is actually below the current Fed rate. That's because the bond market is pricing in that the Fed will cut rates further over the next couple of years. If traders expect the Fed to bring rates down to, say, 3% by 2027, then a 2-year note that pays 3.50% for the whole period actually looks fair — you're locking in a rate that will be above where short-term rates end up.
The same logic applies to CDs. Banks offering a 3-month CD at 4.1% know you'll be back in 3 months and they'll reprice. But a bank offering a 3-year CD at 4.1% is taking the risk that they're paying you more than they need to if rates fall — so they're more cautious about offering high long-term rates.
The 10-year and 30-year yields are higher again (4.14% and 4.85%) because at those horizons you're dealing with more uncertainty — inflation risk, fiscal risk, term premium — and investors demand compensation for that. So the curve dips in the middle and then rises at the long end.
This is actually relevant to your timing decision: if the market is right that rates are heading lower, locking in a longer-term CD or buying I Bonds now captures today's rates before they fall. If the market is wrong and inflation stays sticky, the I Bond is better positioned because its variable component will adjust upward.

Two separate things here.
IB cash interest — yes, you're probably already earning something. IB automatically pays interest on idle cash balances. The rate depends on your account type and balance size. They pay the benchmark rate (Fed funds) minus a spread. For balances over $10K, you're likely getting somewhere around 3.0–3.3%. Below $10K they pay less or nothing. On "guaranteed" — important distinction. T-Bills themselves are guaranteed in the sense that the US government will pay you back at par when they mature. If you buy a 3-month T-Bill at a 3.7% annualized discount, you will get that return if you hold to maturity. That specific bill's return is locked in at purchase.
But the rate on the next T-Bill you buy is not guaranteed. Three months from now when that bill matures and you go to buy another one, the rate could be 3.7%, or 3.0%, or 4.5% — it depends on where the Fed is and what the market is doing. The market currently expects rates to drift lower over the next year or two.
An ETF like SGOV is even less "guaranteed" in that sense — it's constantly rolling into new bills at whatever the current rate is, so your yield floats with the market. Right now that float is around 3.7%, but it's not locked in.
So the honest framing is: right now you're earning roughly 3.7% annualized on short-term Treasuries, which is about 0.9% per quarter. That's what the market is paying today. It's as safe as anything on earth in terms of getting your money back, but the rate you earn going forward will move with Fed policy.
If you want to actually lock in a rate for a longer period, that's where you'd step into a 1-year or 2-year Treasury Note or a CD — you sacrifice some yield today (since the curve is inverted) but you get certainty about what you'll earn over that whole period regardless of what the Fed does.

Okay, here's what's actually structurally interesting — organized by the shape of the return curve, which is what matters.
1. Buffered / Defined Outcome ETFs — piecewise linear payoff functions
These are the most mathematically elegant. Under the hood, they're a put-spread collar built from FLEX options on SPY or QQQ, packaged as an ETF with a 12-month outcome period.
The payoff structure is literally a piecewise linear function with three regimes:
Below buffer: you absorb losses (e.g., market drops 25%, buffer is 15%, you lose 10%)
Within buffer zone: your losses are absorbed, you lose nothing
Above cap: you're capped, market gains beyond the cap go to zero for you
So a typical product might be: 0% buffer to -15% (you lose nothing), capped at +15% upside. The cap level is set by the options market at inception and depends on volatility and rates. Higher vol = higher caps. Higher rates = higher caps (because the risk-free component of the collar is worth more).
The key insight: the protection range is communicated as two numbers like "5/20" — the first indicates where the investor starts losing, the second where the buffer ends and you're exposed again. So a 0/15 product absorbs the first 15% of losses entirely. A 5/20 product lets you eat the first 5%, protects the next 15%, then you're exposed again below -20%.
Tickers: Innovator (BJUL, BAPR, etc. — one per month), First Trust (various), BlackRock iShares (IVVB series), PGIM, AllianzIM. There are now 100+ of these.
The structural quirk that matters: if you buy mid-period, your buffer and cap are different from the stated values. The options have already moved. Every issuer publishes real-time remaining buffer and cap on their website. If you buy a fund with a cap of 8.5% on reset day but it's already risen 1%, you only have 7.5% of potential upside remaining. This is essentially like buying a structured note at a different price than par.
BlackRock also offers 2x accelerated return ETFs — you get 2x upside participation on the S&P up to a cap, with 1x downside. So if the market goes up 5%, you get 10%. If it goes up 15%, you might be capped at 12%. If it drops 10%, you drop 10%. The leverage is on the upside only.
2. Managed Futures / Trend Following — convex crisis alpha
This is the strategy class with the most interesting correlation properties. Managed futures funds go long and short global futures across equities, bonds, commodities, and currencies based on trend signals (typically moving average crossovers).
The mathematical property that makes these special: they exhibit positive convexity — they tend to make the most money during the periods when traditional assets lose the most. In 2022, when stocks fell ~20% and bonds fell ~13% (the worst year for 60/40 in modern history), KMLM returned +25% and DBMF returned +20%. That's the "crisis alpha" phenomenon.
The mechanism is simple: when markets trend strongly in any direction, trend followers profit. Markets tend to trend most violently during crises. The strategy loses money in choppy, mean-reverting markets.
DBMF (0.85% fee): replicates the SG CTA Index, which tracks the top 20 managed futures hedge funds. You're basically getting hedge fund exposure in an ETF by reverse-engineering their positions from returns.
KMLM (0.90%): pure trend-following index dating back to 1988. Trades 22 futures contracts across commodities, currencies, and bonds. Does NOT trade equity futures, which makes it more diversifying. Runs 200-300% gross exposure.
CTA (0.75%): Simplify's version, adds fundamental reversion for commodities and carry for rates on top of trend. Has been the best performer recently.
The return profile: long-term expected returns are positive (maybe 5-8% annualized historically), with low or negative correlation to both stocks and bonds, but with long stretches of flat or negative performance between the sharp gains. You need conviction to hold through the boring periods.
3. Return Stacking — leveraged diversification, not leveraged beta
This is the newest and most conceptually interesting category. The core idea: RSSB invests 100% into global equities and 100% into US Treasury bonds, effectively giving 2:1 leverage on a diversified stock/bond portfolio. But unlike TQQQ (3x Nasdaq), the leverage is across uncorrelated assets, so the risk is much lower than it appears.
RSSB packages the core stock/bond base at 100/100, allowing allocators to stack whatever they want on top — managed futures, commodities, gold, macro, hedged strategies — so long as the overlay outperforms the embedded financing cost.
The family:
RSSB: $1 → $1 global stocks + $1 Treasury bonds
RSST: $1 → $1 US stocks + $1 managed futures
RSBT: $1 → $1 bonds + $1 managed futures
RSSY: $1 → $1 US stocks + $1 carry strategy (harvest yield spreads across markets)
RSSX: $1 → $1 US stocks + $1 gold/bitcoin
RSBA: $1 → $1 Treasuries + $1 merger arbitrage
The "Quad Core" portfolio someone described — 50% RSSY + 50% RSBT — gives you simultaneous exposure to stocks, bonds, trend, and carry, all at 50% notional each, totaling 200% gross with four uncorrelated return streams. That's an institutional-grade risk parity portfolio in two ETFs.
The WisdomTree version: NTSX does 90% US stocks + 60% Treasury futures = 150% notional. Been around since 2018, longer track record. Also GDE (90% stocks + 60% gold exposure).
The embedded cost of this leverage is roughly the T-Bill rate — so right now ~3.7%. The second asset class needs to clear that hurdle to add value. Treasuries and managed futures have historically cleared it.
4. Covered Call / Options Income — selling volatility for yield
These trade upside participation for current income. They own stocks and systematically sell call options against them.
JEPQ (JPMorgan Nasdaq equity premium income): ~9-11% distribution yield. Owns active stock portfolio, sells OTM call options on the Nasdaq 100. You get income but cap your upside in strong rallies.
XYLD: sells calls on S&P 500, ~10% yield.
QYLD: sells ATM calls on Nasdaq, ~12% yield but virtually zero price appreciation.
The structural insight: you're being paid the volatility risk premium. Options are systematically overpriced relative to realized volatility (this is well-documented in academic literature). These ETFs harvest that premium. The tradeoff is you underperform sharply in strong up-markets. In flat or mildly down markets, the option premium cushions you.
5. Merger Arbitrage — market-neutral spread harvesting
MNA (IQ Merger Arbitrage): buys announced acquisition targets, shorts the acquirer or an index. Earns the "deal spread" — the gap between current price and announced acquisition price. Returns are 4-6% with very low equity correlation. It's essentially lending money to deal completion timelines.
RSBA (return stacked version): $1 Treasuries + $1 merger arb. You get your safe bond yield PLUS the merger arb spread.
6. Long/Short and Anti-Beta — negative correlation by design

The map, organized by what you actually care about:
If you want guaranteed positive nominal returns: T-Bill ETFs (SGOV, BIL) — you will make money, period, as long as short-term rates are positive. Currently ~3.7%.
If you want positive expected returns that go up when everything else goes down: managed futures (DBMF, KMLM, CTA). Positive expectation, negative correlation, but lumpy returns.
If you want equity-like returns with a defined worst case: buffered ETFs. You know your maximum loss before you buy.
If you want more return per dollar of capital deployed: return stacking (RSSB, RSST, NTSX). Leverage across uncorrelated assets.
If you want high current income with reduced upside: covered call ETFs (JEPQ, XYLD). 9-12% yield.
If you want pure insurance against market crashes (and will accept negative drag): BTAL or tail-risk strategies.
The really sophisticated play is combining several of these. Something like RSST (stocks + trend) + SGOV (safe yield) + a buffered ETF gives you equity exposure with trend-following crisis protection, guaranteed yield on the safe portion, and defined downside on the equity portion.
