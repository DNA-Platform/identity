Short version: no — not as a permanent fixture. But the reasoning matters here, because the standard advice you'll find online doesn't apply to your situation.
The usual "keep the old domain as a secondary/alias for 3–6 months" guidance assumes you still own and control the old domain. That cushion works because you keep DNS control, so mail to the old addresses still forwards while people update their contacts. You're losing dna.love — so that assumption breaks. Once you no longer control the domain, you may stop receiving email at it because the MX records get reset by the host or whoever acquires it next. The forwarding value of keeping it evaporates the moment ownership lapses.
And Google doesn't actually give you the "park it forever" option anyway. Their policy is explicit: if your expired domain is bought by someone else, you can no longer use it with Workspace and must remove it from the account — and if it were your primary domain, you'd have to delete the whole account. That last clause is exactly why you're switching primary to atlas.love first: it demotes dna.love to a secondary domain so that losing it is a "remove the domain" problem instead of a "lose the account" problem. Good sequencing.
The real reason to keep it temporarily isn't email — it's identity. Your actual exposure is the OAuth audit you already flagged: services where a @dna.love address is the "Sign in with Google" identity, and some of those won't let you change the linked Google identity. As long as the user accounts under dna.love still exist in Workspace and the domain stays verified, those login identities keep functioning even after DNS control is gone — right up until someone re-registers the domain and Google forces removal. So dna.love is worth keeping in the account as a bridge only until that audit is complete and every service has either migrated to an atlas.love identity or has an alternate login path.
After that, remove it, and treat removal as something to do deliberately rather than let happen to you. The security argument is the sharp one: if a stranger re-registers dna.love, they control @dna.love mail. Any lingering alias, recovery address, or login identity on that domain becomes a live account-takeover vector — password resets and verification mails route to them. A dead domain you don't own is pure attack surface with zero upside once forwarding is gone.
One practical constraint on the mechanics: you can't remove a secondary domain until you've reassigned every user, group, and alias off it (renaming them to atlas.love, in bulk if needed) and cleared any associated alias/test domains. So removal falls out naturally as the last step of finishing the migration — once nothing depends on dna.love, deleting it is a two-click confirm, and you can typically reuse the name within about 30 minutes if you ever needed to (you won't).
So: keep it as a bridge through the identity audit, then purge it. Don't keep it because it feels safer to leave things in place — for a domain you're losing, leaving it in place is the riskier option.

Good news: those aren't two separate actions. Changing the primary domain is what demotes the old one — when you promote atlas.love to primary, Google automatically reclassifies the current primary as a secondary domain. So it's one operation, plus the forwarding.
First, one check decides your path. Open Admin console → Account → Domains → Manage domains and find atlas.love:
If it's listed and shows verified as a secondary domain, you're ready to switch.
If it's not there — or it's there as a domain alias rather than a secondary domain — add it first (Add a domain → enter atlas.love → choose "secondary domain" → verify via the TXT record at your registrar). You can only promote a verifiedsecondary domain to primary; an alias can't be promoted. That distinction is the one common gotcha here.
The "different secondary domain" you think is sitting in there is almost certainly fine to ignore — it doesn't interfere. Just confirm atlas.love specifically is present and verified.
Then the switch and the forwarding:
Manage domains → Change primary domain → select atlas.love → confirm. The old primary drops to secondary automatically. Give it a few minutes to propagate.
Important: this does not rename your users. doug@dna.love stays doug@dna.love until you rename it — the primary change only sets the org default and lets you reassign.
For each person (you, Ana, anyone else): Directory → Users → the user → rename their address to @atlas.love, then use Add Alternate Emails to put their old @dna.love address back onto the same account. Now mail to either address lands in one inbox — no forwarding rule to build or maintain.
That aliasing approach is the "forwarding" you want, done the robust way. A forwarding rule would be a second thing to manage and would break the same moment aliasing does — so skip the rule.
The caveat that's specific to you: all of this for dna.love only works while its MX records still point to Google, i.e. while you still own the domain. The aliases are a bridge, not a permanent forward — once dna.love lapses, DNS (not Workspace) stops routing the mail and nothing in the console can save it. So this window is exactly your runway to move senders and the OAuth logins over to atlas.love before you let the domain go.
If you specifically want a true catch-all — mail to any @dna.love address, including ones with no matching user — that's a separate "default routing" rule in Gmail's admin settings. For a handful of mailboxes the aliases above cover everything, so I'd only reach for it if you know you're getting mail to addresses that aren't real accounts.
