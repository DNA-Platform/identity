It could be as simple as abaked good that I can deliver warm or duty free exports that I can buy or cuban cigars I can buy and bring to the country for up to a certain amount. The theory holds, and the evidence sorts neatly by who the buyer is. Ranked by bulletproofness:
Tier 1 — no buyer needed (you're paid by the airline or the state)
UK261 delay/denied-boarding compensation. Any flight departing the UK, any carrier: £520 per person if you arrive 3+ hours late on a >3,500 km leg. Both LHR→JFK and LHR→TBS qualify. Not controllable, but expected value is nonzero and the payout is statutory — the closest thing to a free option you'll hold on this itinerary. Choose a UK/EU carrier for the inbound legs too, so the arriving-into-London legs are also covered.
Voluntary bump auctions on the NY departure. US carriers routinely pay $500–$1,500+ in vouchers/cash for volunteers on oversold flights; Delta agents have discretionary authority into the thousands. Only worth it if your return date is soft.
Your own consumption gap. Anything you'd buy anyway — electronics at US retail (or zero-sales-tax if routed through NH/Delaware/Oregon) vs Tbilisi retail. Georgia's duty-free personal allowance by air is 30 kg and GEL 3,000 once every 30 days, so a laptop and phone come in clean. This is arbitrage with yourself as counterparty, which is why it's bulletproof.
Tier 2 — pre-committed buyer with escrow (this is what you asked for)
Grabr. Shoppers post a link (Amazon/eBay), travelers bid; rewards typically run 15–20% of the item's price, and payment is held in escrow and released when the item is in the shopper's hands. Their own figure is ~$300 per trip from a handful of items. Buyer, price, and settlement are fixed before you buy — the Cuban-cigar problem solved by design. Two caveats: the NY→Tbilisi lane is thin (the platform's mass is LatAm/CIS), and there are App Store complaints about delayed payouts, so it's escrow with counterparty risk on Grabr itself. Capital tie-up is yours, which means credit-card points on top.
Onboard courier (OBC). time:matters/airmates, Air Charter Service — freelance hand-carry of urgent B2B parts and documents; they pay the flight plus a fee. US routes are among the most-served, and requirements are just frequent-flyer experience, a clean record, and a valid passport. The catch is inversion: OBC paysyour whole flight but on their schedule and route, so it fits the "traveling anyway" clause only if you register in advance and a LHR→JFK mission happens to match your window. Registering is free and the option is real.
Tier 3 — liquid marketplace, no pre-commitment StockX/eBay for region-gated items (US-only drops, LEGO retirements) going east; mymarket.ge for iPhones/MacBooks going east. Real spread, real venue, but you carry inventory risk and Georgian resale is small-market.
Dead on arrival
Cuban cigars into the US: since September 24, 2020, travelers cannot bring Cuban tobacco or alcohol into the US even for personal use, from any third country. Into Georgia the allowance is 50 cigars, but Habanos pricing is set by distributor and Tbilisi isn't cheaper than Heathrow duty-free, so no spread.
Warm baked goods / Georgian food to the Brighton Beach diaspora: legal in the US (no meat; hard cheese OK) and there's a real buyer population, but no venue with escrow — Facebook-group counterparties, which is exactly the "questionable" bucket.
Net: the round trip's expected offset from Tier 1 + Grabr is plausibly a few hundred dollars against ~$1,500–2,500 in airfare, which matches your prior — offset, not profit. The one thing that breaks the "only if traveling anyway" constraint is OBC, and it does so by taking control of the itinerary.

Here's what searching for the problem as others frame it turns up.
The term "travel arbitrage" mostly means something else. In the travel-hacking world it refers to fare arbitrage — hidden-city ticketing, routing via Istanbul or Cairo to cut a $3–4k transatlantic fare to ~$2k, exploiting carriers whose pricing reflects currency devaluation — i.e. reducing the cost side, not generating income. Nobody prominent uses the term for what you're describing.
The income side has a small, well-defined ecosystem, and its history is the evidence. The luggage-space marketplaces that launched 2015–2017 mostly died:
Airmule paid ~$150 per bag, focused on US–China, raised $1.25M, and ceased operations in February 2022.
Ouibring, PiggyBee (tip-based, no escrow) — dormant or defunct.
Pabbler survives but requires you to drop the items at DHL on arrival and enter the tracking number — so no in-person buyer; you're a cross-border leg for a shipper.
Grabr is the one survivor with escrow, and its own data says the lane matters: the model works where import markups exceed 100%, e.g. Argentina, and the most profitable routes are Buenos Aires, São Paulo, and Rio. A search for Grabr activity in Tbilisi returns nothing — no lane evidence at all.
OBC networks (airmates/time:matters, ACS) are the only mature, professional form, and they push you to their routes and windows: a US mission means 4–7 days abroad on their schedule.
What that record says about your theory. The survivorship pattern is the finding: the spread exists mainly where a country has tariff/VAT walls of 50–100%+ (Argentina, Brazil, historically Russia), and the platforms that tried to monetize generic luggage space on rich-to-rich lanes (NY–London) didn't clear the trust and coordination cost. Georgia has neither the walls (GEL 3,000/30 kg comes in duty-free by air) nor the population to support a lane, and the US inbound leg is the wrong direction — the demand is for US goods going out. So the empirical answer to "is there a bulletproof buyer for Tbilisi↔NY" is: the market has looked and not built one. The remaining offsets — UK261, bump vouchers, self-consumption on electronics — are real but they're not arbitrage in the sense you posed; they're option value on the ticket itself.
The Russian-language "с оказией" informal channel (Telegram/Facebook groups) is the actual mechanism for this region, but it's exactly the no-escrow bucket you excluded. If you want, I can search those groups specifically for the Tbilisi–NY pair to see if anyone is posting paid requests.

You can — I understated it. The spread is real; what's missing is scale and a clean exit.
Numbers: iSpace lists (cite index="95-1">iPhone 17 Pro 256 GB at 4,269 GEL</cite> — roughly $1,580 at current rates. US retail is $1,099 (+8.9% in NYC, or zero in Delaware/NH/Oregon). That's a $380–480 gross spread, ~30%, before you sell.
Where it gets eaten:
Realization discount. You're not selling at iSpace's price; you're selling a sealed US unit on mymarket.ge against other grey imports. US iPhones are eSIM-only and carry no local warranty, and Georgian buyers price that in. Realistic clearing price is maybe 3,600–3,900 GEL, which cuts the net to roughly $150–300 per unit.
Scale cap. The duty-free allowance is (cite index="15-1">30 kg and GEL 3,000 once every 30 days</cite> — one Pro phone. A second unit exceeds the threshold, and multiple identical sealed units is the textbook commercial-import signal; Georgian customs charges 18% VAT on the excess, which erases the spread on the second unit and beyond.
Time cost. Listing, haggling, meeting a buyer in Tbilisi — a couple of hours per unit for a couple hundred dollars.
So the honest statement is: one unit per trip, ~$150–300 net, with you as the market maker. That's the same 15–20% Grabr pays, except Grabr gives you the buyer and escrow up front and the electronics play doesn't. The reason I filed it under "self-consumption" is that if the phone is for you, the whole $400–500 is captured with zero execution risk; if it's for resale, half of it goes to the grey-market discount and you inherit a buyer search. Both are legitimate; the first is just cleaner.
Where this scales is MacBooks — higher ticket, same allowance bound, but the GEL 3,000 cap bites harder (a MacBook Pro exceeds it alone). If you want, I can price the grey-market clearing level on mymarket.ge for a specific model so the net isn't an estimate.

Here's the vocabulary map first, then what each handle actually turned up.
20 terms, and why each one is a good search handle
Crowdshipping — the academic/logistics-research term for travelers carrying parcels. Unlocks peer-reviewed papers with measured economics instead of startup marketing.
Daigou (代购) — Chinese "buy on behalf." The most professionalized, highest-volume version of exactly this: travelers buying abroad for domestic buyers. A multi-billion-dollar industry with its own playbook.
Shuttle trade / suitcase trade — the post-Soviet 1990s term (челноки), tracked by the IMF in balance-of-payments data. Georgia–Turkey is a canonical corridor, so this is the local ancestor.
Bavul ticareti — the Turkish name for the same thing; Turkish-language sources cover the Georgian side of the corridor.
Courier flights / air courier travel — the 1980s–2000s system where you gave up your baggage allowance for a discounted ticket (IAATC, Air Courier Association). Battle-tested for decades; question is whether anything survives.
Onboard courier (OBC) / hand-carry — the professional freight term; already covered.
Parallel import / grey import — the legal-economic name for the iPhone play. Surfaces how grey-market channels into Georgia actually work and what they clear at.
Personal shopper abroad / shopping agent — the English-language daigou analogue; catches Airfrov, Backpackbang, and Instagram-based operators.
Social delivery / collaborative logistics — European startup phrasing (PiggyBee's self-description); catches EU-based platforms that "peer-to-peer delivery" misses.
Luggage-space marketplace / monetize your luggage — consumer-app phrasing; catches new entrants and app-store listings.
С оказией / передать с попутчиком — the Russian informal courier idiom; the Tbilisi lane runs in Russian-language Telegram, not English apps.
Voluntary denied boarding (VDB) / oversale compensation — FlyerTalk terminology; gets you documented payout ranges rather than anecdotes.
Duty-free allowance arbitrage / VAT-refund arbitrage — tax-mechanism framing: claim VAT back on exit (Global Blue) and bring in under the entry allowance. Catches the legal edge.
Geographic price arbitrage / regional pricing arbitrage — the generic economics phrase; catches consumer-electronics price-comparison datasets by country.
Border effect / law of one price deviations — academic trade economics; quantifies how big cross-border retail spreads actually are and why they persist.
Trip subsidization — Grabr's own marketing phrase; catches competitors using the same pitch.
Airline mystery shopping / in-flight audit — already covered; the term "audit" catches B2B agency pages.
Diaspora hand-carry / "Georgians in New York" groups — community-network framing; the actual demand source for TBS↔NYC.
Excess baggage arbitrage / baggage allowance monetization — freight-cost framing: your free 23 kg vs. $8–15/kg air cargo rates.
Get paid to travel / paid to fly — the mass-blog phrase. Highest noise, but sometimes surfaces obscure programs the others miss.

Findings, by handle. The terms that paid off were crowdshipping, daigou, VDB, and parallel import; the ones that didn't are noted at the end.
Crowdshipping (academic). The literature formalizes your prior exactly: (cite index="105-1">the efficient case is a traveler who already has a planned trip agreeing to carry a package en route, and carriers trade off pickup/delivery effort against the fee.</cite> The sobering result is scale: in a last-mile study, (cite index="108-1">only about 1% of parcels qualified as outliers worth outsourcing to the crowd, and of those only 3–43% were cost-efficient depending on scenario.</cite> Translation: even in the best-designed systems, the crowd carries the residual the professional network can't serve cheaply. That's the ceiling.
Daigou (the one place it scaled). (cite index="116-1">An $81 billion informal industry in China, running from students reselling on the side to firms running large operations,</cite> with (cite index="122-1">resellers buying 20–30% below Chinese official prices and adding fees for authenticity guarantees.</cite> Why it works there and not TBS↔NYC: (cite index="120-1">high taxes on luxury goods at home plus deep distrust of the domestic supply after the 2008 melamine scandal, so foreign-bought goods carry a safety premium.</cite> Two walls — tax and trust. Georgia has neither against US goods. The structural lesson is that the arbitrage lives where a wall exists, and the wall adjacent to you is Russia, not America; Georgia's role in that corridor is conduit, which is a different business and not on your itinerary.
Courier flights (the old battle-tested model). Dead. (cite index="123-1">The author of the definitive book on it concedes that post-9/11 changes to air freight and security made courier travel effectively a thing of the past, with remaining "courier" fares rarely competitive with consolidators.</cite>
VDB — this is the one you can sign up for. Delta lets you pre-register at online check-in: (cite index="141-1">the check-in flow asks whether you want to join the volunteer list for a gift card, with choices of $200, $300, $400, $500, or a custom amount.</cite> Ranges: (cite index="140-1">typical compensation is airline credit from $200 to $1,000 depending on delay length, competing volunteers, and how oversold the flight is,</cite> and after the 2017 United incident (cite index="144-1">Delta authorized agents up to $2,000, with supervisors able to approve nearly $10,000.</cite> Delta also runs the most auctions by far — (cite index="142-1">29,893 voluntary denied boardings in Q1 2023, the most of any carrier.</cite> Practical rule: (cite index="141-1">confirm you get the same amount as the final offer needed to fill the volunteer quota, even if you signed up lower.</cite> So: fly Delta out of JFK, pre-list at check-in with a high custom number, hold a soft return date.
Parallel import (the iPhone reality). Georgian chains already retail US-spec units: Zoommer and Myline list (cite index="146-1">iPhone 17 Pro Max "e-SIM Only" at from 112 GEL/month</cite> versus iSpace's 120 GEL/month for the official unit — roughly a 7% grey discount at retail. So the grey channel is institutionalized by bulk importers; you'd be competing with them on mymarket.ge, one unit per 30 days. Net per unit stays in the $150–300 range I gave.
The Russian-language channel. The network exists and is structured: the ЭМИГРАНТЫ 360 group runs (cite index="134-1">a "Russia–Europe parcels–travelers" chat (@popytchiki_360) among 500+ chats across 110 countries,</cite> and the RU↔GE channel posts hashtag-formatted carrier listings that (cite index="133-1">explicitly advertise picking up Wildberries/Ozon parcels.</cite> The US-lane equivalent almost certainly exists in the same network. Still no escrow, so it stays in your excluded bucket, but it's where the Tbilisi demand actually lives.
Handles that returned nothing useful: VAT-refund arbitrage (UK abolished tourist VAT refunds in 2021, and Georgia's refund threshold is trivial), border-effect economics (measures spreads but doesn't locate buyers), "get paid to travel" (noise), luggage-space marketplaces (only the already-covered survivors), bavul ticareti (historical, land-border, Turkey-specific).
Net update to the ranked list: VDB moves to the top because it's the only item that's both pre-registrable and has a documented $200–2,000 payout distribution; OBC and mystery shopping stay as free lottery entries; the goods-arbitrage leg is bounded at one phone.
