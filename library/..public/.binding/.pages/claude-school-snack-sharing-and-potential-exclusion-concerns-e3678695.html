This is a very common school social dynamic, and you're right to notice both possible interpretations.
What's likely happening: Kids using food/treats as social currency is extremely well-documented in school settings across all ages. It's one of the more subtle but effective tools children use to signal in-group membership. The pattern usually looks like a small cluster of friends coordinating to bring something and share it visibly, which implicitly signals who is "in" — even if that's not the explicit intent of every child involved.
Is it a problem? It can be, depending on execution. Research on childhood social exclusion consistently identifies food-sharing as one of the more psychologically impactful forms because it's highly visible and tied to something basic (eating together = belonging). The kids not included watch it happen in real time, often during lunch or break.
Katie's crying is worth paying attention to. Children that age (around 8-10) typically don't cry when asked a neutral logistical question unless there's some anxiety attached — either she knows it's a bit rule-bending, she felt accused of doing something wrong, or she's under real social pressure to participate and fears missing out on the group moment. That reaction suggests this matters more to her than a casual snack exchange.
On the school side: Most schools actually do have policies about outside food for allergy, fairness, and distraction reasons. The fact that you checked is the right move. If the school says it's fine and it's genuinely just a small friend group thing, it may be low-stakes. But if it's happening in a way that's designed to be seen by excluded kids, that's worth the school knowing about regardless.
The most useful next step is probably a low-pressure conversation with Katie — not about the rules, but about whether she feels good about how it's happening and whether anyone is being left out that she's noticed.

Common school food/snack policies:
Most schools fall into one of a few camps. Outright bans on outside food sharing are increasingly common due to allergy liability — many schools prohibit any food brought for group sharing unless it goes through the teacher with advance notice and ingredient review. Others allow it for designated occasions (birthdays, class parties) but require parent-submitted forms. A smaller number are effectively permissive in practice even if rules exist on paper, especially at the peer-to-peer level during breaks where supervision is lighter.
At Happy School specifically, given its Russian-curriculum orientation, food-sharing culture tends to be more relaxed than in Western schools — bringing treats to share is a fairly normalized part of the social fabric in that tradition. Worth factoring in when the school responds.
On the more important thing you're flagging:
The "made it sound official to get it under Mom's radar" read is worth taking seriously. Children that age are quite capable of strategically framing requests to exploit the path of least resistance — and the fact that she brought it to you rather than Ana could mean several things:
She calculated you'd be easier to ask (new dynamic, wants to build rapport, less established enforcement history)
Ana has said no to similar things before and Katie is routing around that
Or Ana has allowed similar things frequently, and Katie expected a yes but got an unexpected question instead, hence the tears
The crying fits best with the second or third scenario — not guilt about the framing, but surprise that it became a bigger deal than expected.
The cleanest move is a brief, private check-in with Ana before anything is decided: "Katie mentioned this — did you already know about it?" That question alone tells you a lot depending on Ana's answer, and it closes the routing-around dynamic without making it a confrontation with Katie.
