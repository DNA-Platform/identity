Looking at these examples through SRT's lens, several profound patterns emerge:
The Containment Test: Only certain relationships support the "in" metaphor. "In a relationship" works for romantic partnerships because they're conceptualized as bounded spaces where people coexist. But "in a special type of relationship" fails because business relationships aren't container spaces - they're functional connections, not cohabitation spaces.
Canonical vs. Non-Canonical Relationships: When we say "John is his grandfather," we're using the canonical familial representation directly. But "there is a relationship between them, John is his grandfather" treats the canonical as if it were just one instance of a general category, which feels redundant and semantically off.
The Subject-Object Structure: In SRT terms, familial relationships like "grandfather" create clear subject-object patterns:John =grandson> grandfather. The relationship type itself (grandson) specifies the directionality and nature of the connection.
Poetic vs. Functional Relationships: "There is a relationship between the sun rising and the stillness of the lake" works because it's poetic/metaphorical - we're creating a representational connection through aesthetic or causal observation. But business relationships are functional, not spatial, so the container metaphor doesn't apply.
The Specificity Principle: SRT reveals that we prefer the most specific relationship type available. "John is his grandfather" uses the specific canonical rather than the generic "they are related," which would be informationally weaker.
This connects to SRT's insight that every relationship automatically creates representation through (x,y) =(x,y)> y. The canonical relationship types have achieved such consensus that they function as immediate, transparent representations rather than requiring explicit relational specification.
