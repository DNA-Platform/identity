Here's what I've found, Doug — the short answer is it's very unlikely to work, and there are sanctions-related risks to consider.
How Wildberries Georgia operates: Wildberries started operating in Georgia in October 2024, running through a local franchise entity rather than directly as the Russian parent company. In Georgia, the number of pickup points has doubled since late 2024, and they now have 90+ locations across Tbilisi, Batumi, Kutaisi, and other cities.
Payment processing — the core problem:
Wildberries processes payments through its own Wildberries Bank, which is a Russian-incorporated entity (formerly Standard Credit Bank). Even though the Georgian operation runs via a local franchise, the payment infrastructure ties back to Russian banking systems. There are several layers of issues:
Visa/Mastercard suspended Russian operations in March 2022. Visa and Mastercard announced that they were voluntarily suspending all operations in Russia. This means transactions routed through Russian payment processors won't clear through Visa/Mastercard networks internationally.
Wildberries has its own troubled history with Visa/Mastercard. The company has repeatedly clashed with both networks, at one point charging a 3% surcharge on Visa/MC transactions even within Russia, and now heavily pushes its own WB Wallet and Russian domestic systems (Mir, SBP, SberPay).
Georgian payment upon receipt model. From user reports in Russian-language forums, the primary payment method in Georgia appears to be "payment upon receipt" at pickup points using a linked bank card. Users have been linking Russian-issued bank cards. There's no clear evidence that foreign-issued international cards (especially US-issued) are supported.
Sanctions risk for you specifically:
Even if the transaction somehow went through technically, using a US-issued card to transact with a Russian marketplace entity raises OFAC compliance concerns. In April 2022, Kim and Wildberries were added to Poland's sanctions list, and while Wildberries isn't currently on the US SDN list specifically, the payment flow through Russian banking infrastructure could trigger your US bank's sanctions compliance filters and potentially block or flag the transaction.
Bottom line: Your US Visa/Mastercard almost certainly won't work with Wildberries in Georgia — the payment processing infrastructure either won't accept it technically, or your US bank will likely block it as a Russia-connected transaction. If you need to order from Wildberries in Georgia, you'd likely need a Georgian-issued bank card or possibly a card from another CIS country. Given the sanctions landscape, I'd also suggest considering whether using the platform at all creates any compliance exposure for you as a US person.

You're exactly right about the "payment upon receipt" model. Let me clarify what that actually means in Wildberries' system and then get to practical options.
How "payment upon receipt" actually works on WB:
It's not truly "pay when you get there" in a cash-at-the-door sense. Users select the "pay on site" option and the order is accepted for processing, but you still need a card linked to your account. When you pick up at the ПВЗ (pickup point), the payment is made from the buyer's bank card, which the user must link in advance in their personal account. So it's essentially a deferred card charge — the linked card is your guarantee, and they auto-charge it at pickup. You're right that they wouldn't ship cross-border from Russia on pure faith.
What payment methods actually exist on Wildberries:
The Russian platform supports Mir cards, SBP (Russia's fast payment system), SberPay, and WB Wallet. The WB Wallet is an online wallet that requires verification through Gosuslugi (Russia's government services portal) — obviously a non-starter for you. Even for Russian users, since February 2024 there have been problems linking Mastercard cards, with periods where only Mir, SBP, SberPay, and WB Wallet worked.
What would realistically work for you in Georgia:
The most plausible path is a Georgian-issued bank card from TBC Bank or Bank of Georgia. These are locally-issued Visa/Mastercard cards, but the transaction would process through Georgian banking infrastructure rather than routing through your US bank. The Georgian franchise entity presumably has a local acquiring bank relationship for the Georgian market specifically. The card would be "international" by network but "local" by issuer.
However, there's a second barrier beyond payment: orders can currently only be placed by Georgian citizens or those with a residence permit (ВНЖ). You need to enter a name and surname through the Georgian site (wildberries.ge), and registration requires Georgian residency documentation.
So the practical calculus is: even if you solve the payment problem with a Georgian bank card, you'd need either Ana's Georgian residency status or someone local with proper documentation to place the order. This is essentially a two-gate problem — identity verification AND payment method — and a US card fails on the payment side while US citizenship without Georgian ВНЖ fails on the identity side.

You're asking exactly the right question, and the answer is essentially yes — the structural pieces are all there for this to function as a geolocation and identification tool for Russian citizens abroad.
Here's the chain:
Wildberries Bank is fully under Russian state supervision. Wildberries Bank (formerly Standard Credit Bank, OGRN 1020100002340) is a Russian-licensed LLC regulated by the Central Bank of Russia. There is no such thing as a Russian-licensed bank operating independently of the state apparatus.
Rosfinmonitoring reports directly to Putin. Rosfinmonitoring has been a separate federal service since 2007, and since 2012 financial intelligence of Russia reports directly to the President of the Russian Federation. It coordinates with the FSB, Ministry of Internal Affairs, and Prosecutor General's Office.
Russian banks must share customer data with security services. Rosfinmonitoring works with state government agencies, such as the Federal Security Service (FSB), the Ministry of Internal Affairs, and the Prosecutor General's Office, in order to exchange information and provide support to investigations. All financial institutions must maintain customer identification data and transaction records.
The FSB is actively expanding its access powers. A law was adopted by the State Duma allowing the FSB to obtain free and without a court order copies of the databases of any company operating in the country, coming into effect on April 1, 2026. And separately, the FSB has ordered banks to install surveillance equipment known as SORM (System for Operative Investigative Activities), which enables security services to intercept and analyze messages, voice recordings, videos and other user-generated content, with implementation required by 2027.
Now apply this to the Georgia operation specifically. When a Russian citizen who fled to Georgia registers on wildberries.ge, the system captures: their phone number, their name, their delivery address (a specific pickup point in a specific Georgian city), what they're buying, and their payment method. Even though the Georgian franchise entity handles local logistics, the platform infrastructure, user accounts, and payment processing all flow through the Russian parent. The app itself is unified across all countries.
So the data pipeline looks like: Russian citizen registers → Wildberries (Russian entity) holds the account data → Wildberries Bank processes financial data → CBR and Rosfinmonitoring have supervisory access → FSB has (or will have by April 2026) unrestricted database access without court order.
The particularly clever part is that this doesn't even require active surveillance. Wildberries even requires new sellers to open accounts at Wildberries Bank for "vetting" purposes, since the bank "has access to databases for more careful verification" — they're openly acknowledging the bank serves as an identity verification layer connected to Russian state databases.
Whether this is intentional intelligence infrastructure or just a convenient byproduct of Russian corporate structure is an open question. But the effect is the same: Russia's largest e-commerce platform is now operating pickup points across Georgia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Armenia, and other countries that happen to be exactly where hundreds of thousands of Russians relocated after mobilization — and every transaction generates a data trail that flows back to entities under direct Kremlin oversight.
Your instinct is right. The "independent bank" framing is functionally meaningless in the Russian regulatory context.
