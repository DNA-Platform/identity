Fair points, and you're right — I was reading too much into the consciousness mention. Setting that aside.
What's in it for the university (the honest answer):
Tuition revenue at low marginal cost. This is the biggest one. A PhD by Published Work is a 6–12 month registration where the university provides one supervisor/adviser, runs a viva, and processes paperwork. They don't pay you a stipend, don't allocate lab space, don't run coursework. International fees range from £14,900 for the 2026–2027 academic year for international candidates at York St John down to roughly £2,500–£5,000 part-time at places like Manchester Met (where the tuition fee would be 50% of the fee noted, to reflect the standard six-month period of registration). Compared to a 4-year funded PhD that costs them ~£100k+ in stipend/overhead, you're nearly pure margin.
REF credit (UK Research Excellence Framework). UK universities are ranked and partly funded based on the research output associated with them. Your publications, once your degree is awarded, are forever tied to that institution's alumni record. Mid-tier universities especially benefit from being associated with a Nature Neuroscience paper.
Filling a market niche. Mid-tier universities (Westminster, York St John, Hull, Northampton, Swansea, Ulster, Anglia Ruskin, etc.) explicitly market this route to mid-career people who never finished. It's a deliberate strategy to differentiate from research-heavy Russell Group universities. Russell Group schools mostly restrict this route to their own staff — The University of Manchester has published its own Guidance for the PhD By Published Work, with eligibility only extending to current members of staff, and Warwick is similar.
What they expect from you:
5–8 publications, not one. Generally, a minimum of five to eight published pieces are required, but this varies between institutions and depends on their length. One Nature Neuroscience paper, however landmark, will not pass most committees on its own.
A coherent body of work, not a greatest-hits list. The publications have to tell a unified scientific story — you need a thread (e.g., "neural population dynamics in motor cortex" or "oscillations and information transfer"). This is what your critical commentary argues.
A critical commentary. 5,000 words for science and technology subjects at Westminster; up to 15,000 elsewhere. It frames the publications, explains your contribution, and positions the work in the field.
Currently relevant work. This is the part that may bite you. Warwick is explicit: A PhD by Published work is not awarded in recognition of excellent research completed in the past which is not currently impactful, relevant or significant. A single 2006 paper plus one new one in 2026 is a gap committees will scrutinize. Citation count helps you here — the field is clearly still building on the work — but they'll want to see you're an active researcher, not someone reviving a dormant CV.
A viva (oral defense). Same rigor as a traditional PhD defense.
An adviser at the institution who agrees to supervise you. This is the practical bottleneck — you need to find a faculty member doing related work who'll sponsor your application.
Practical implication for your plan: writing one more paper is probably not enough. Realistically you'd want 4–6 more pieces — they don't all need to be Nature-tier (review articles, methods papers, computational/theoretical pieces, even strong preprints in some programs) but they need to exist and cohere. If you can collaborate remotely with a former lab or a new one to land your name on 3–4 papers over 2–3 years, then apply, you've got a defensible case at a place like Westminster, York St John, or Swansea.

Hava Siegelmann's work on analog recurrent neural networks as super-Turing computers (the question of whether continuous dynamical systems can compute functions Turing machines can't)
Maass and Markram's liquid state machines / reservoir computing — showing that generic recurrent dynamics can be read out to perform arbitrary computations, with the dynamics acting as a high-dimensional projection

More recently, the deep learning theory crowd (Ganguli, Sompolinsky, Pennington) on how dynamics in high-dimensional networks support function
A theoretical piece staking out a position on how Turing computability maps onto (or fails to map onto) nonlinear neural dynamics, framed with the Stevens-era physics-minded sensibility, is not a niche curiosity.
