Yeah, this is absolutely a thing - it's one of the most common destructive patterns in couple conflicts.
The dynamic you're describing is where someone might start with a legitimate complaint or reaction, but then escalates it so drastically that the original issue becomes irrelevant and now their reaction is the problem. It's like they had 60% of the valid point, but then torched it by going nuclear.
A few things that feed this pattern:
Emotional flooding - When someone gets triggered, their nervous system can get so activated that their response becomes completely disproportionate. The rational "this is how upset I should be about this" part of their brain goes offline.
Feeling chronically unheard - Sometimes people escalate because past attempts to raise issues more calmly got dismissed or minimized. So they've learned (incorrectly) that they need to be dramatic to be taken seriously.
Poor conflict skills - Many people never learned how to express "I'm hurt about X" without it becoming "and you're a terrible person and here are 17 other things you've done wrong."
Protest behavior - In attachment terms, when people feel threatened in a relationship, they sometimes ramp up negative behavior to force attention/resolution, even though it backfires.
The really frustrating part is that it derails any chance of actually resolving the original issue - now you're fighting about how they fought rather than what they were upset about.

Based on the research, here's a comprehensive therapeutic framework for this pattern:
The Pattern: "Protest Escalation" or "Disproportionate Protest Behavior"
In attachment therapy terms, this is a form of protest behavior - specifically when anxious attachment drives someone to escalate dramatically to get attention and reconnection, but the escalation becomes so extreme it completely invalidates their original legitimate grievance.
Why It's So Destructive (Your Ear Flick/Knife Analogy)
This is the critical therapeutic insight: When the response is disproportionate to the trigger, it creates a "validity inversion."
The original issue (ear flick) might have been genuinely problematic and deserved attention. But once the response reaches a certain level of severity (knife stab), the conflict dynamics fundamentally shift:
The escalation becomes the primary issue - The disproportionate response is now the more serious violation
The original grievance becomes unaddressable - To address it would require implicitly validating that the extreme response was justified
The escalator loses moral standing - Even if they had a legitimate complaint, their credibility is destroyed
The cycle perpetuates - The original issue never gets resolved, so it resurfaces later with even more resentment
As one therapist puts it: "Rather than the root issues (e.g., feelings of unheard, rejection, loneliness, etc.) that need healing, the words of defense take the center stage in the conflict. The sore root feelings remain unaddressed"
Attachment Theory Explanation
This pattern typically stems from anxious/preoccupied attachment where someone learned in childhood that sustained protest behavior was a viable option - getting mad, crying, or having a tantrum were likely to work often enough to make it worth the upset.
The logic: "Love is unreliable, but if I scream loud enough, I'll get attention."
The problem: In childhood, this strategy works because society views abandoning a child as unacceptable. But when practiced by adults, protest behavior is often a killer of relationships.
The Therapeutic Framework
Clinical term: "Destructive protest escalation with validity inversion"
Core mechanism:
Emotional flooding overwhelms the nervous system
The person feels overwhelmed, has a negative perception of their partner's emotions, and experiences unjustified or disproportionate anger
The prefrontal cortex (rational brain) goes offline
Response becomes purely reactive, not proportional
The paradox: The person often does have a legitimate grievance, but their expression of it is so destructive that:
The content of their complaint is lost
They appear to be "the problem"
Their partner can truthfully say "I can't address your concern because your behavior in expressing it was unacceptable"
Resolution becomes impossible
Productive Therapeutic Approach
Reframing for the escalator: "You had something real to be upset about. But when your response crossed into [specific behaviors], it created a new and more serious problem. This doesn't mean your original feeling was wrong - it means the way you expressed it made it impossible for your partner to hear you. Your hurt matters, but so does how you handle it."
Reframing for the recipient: "Yes, their response was disproportionate and that's not okay. But buried under that escalation was a real hurt that still needs addressing. Once you're safe and the escalation is dealt with, the original issue still exists."
The work:
Separate the content from the process - "What you felt" vs. "How you expressed it"
Teach emotional regulation - Being flooded with anger doesn't give permission to eviscerate your partner, nor does being hurt justify saying things designed to wound. Your feelings are always valid, but your words and actions in response to those feelings are your responsibility
Address the attachment wound - Why does this person believe they need to escalate to be heard?
Create new patterns - Practice expressing hurt without scorched-earth tactics
The therapeutic goal isn't to determine "who started it" but to recognize that disproportionate escalation creates a new, separate problem that must be addressed before the original issue can be resolved.

Пример 3: Партнёр делает саркастичный комментарий о готовке. В ответ другой партнёр объявляет, что хочет развода, и начинает обсуждать раздел имущества.
Почему это разрушительно: Проблема "инверсии правоты"
Давайте используем Пример 1 для детального анализа.
Да, опоздать на 20 минут на важный ужин — это легитимная проблема. Это неуважительно, это создаёт неудобство, это заслуживает обсуждения.
Но когда в ответ следует молчаливый бойкот на три дня, происходит то, что я называю "инверсия правоты" (proportionality inversion):
Опоздание на 20 минут — реальная проблема, но относительно небольшая
Бойкот на три дня — объективно более серьёзное нарушение

Динамика отношений фундаментально изменилась: теперь в паре есть "человек, который устраивает многодневные бойкоты"

Disproportionate Retaliatory Aggression in Couple Conflicts
Examples from therapeutic practice:
Example 1: A partner is 20 minutes late for dinner with parents. In response, the other partner implements a "silent treatment" for three days, refusing to talk, ignoring all attempts at communication.
Example 2: A partner forgot to call when they were running late at work. In response, the other partner says: "I never should have married you" and lists all their mistakes from the past five years.
Example 3: A partner makes a sarcastic comment about cooking. In response, the other partner announces they want a divorce and begins discussing division of property.
Why this is destructive: The problem of "validity inversion"
Let's use Example 1 for detailed analysis.
Yes, being 20 minutes late for an important dinner is a legitimate problem. It's disrespectful, it creates inconvenience, it deserves discussion.
But when a three-day silent treatment follows in response, what I call "validity inversion" (proportionality inversion) occurs:
Being 20 minutes late — a real problem, but relatively small
Three-day boycott — objectively a more serious violation
Now, to discuss the lateness, one would need to implicitly justify the three-day boycott
This is impossible for a reasonable person — the boycott is clearly disproportionate
Therefore the original problem (lateness) is never discussed
The relationship dynamics have fundamentally changed: now the couple has "a person who stages multi-day boycotts"
The therapeutic problem
As therapist Terry Real writes: "Revenge [retaliatory aggression] is a perverse form of communication, a twisted attempt at repair. We want to 'make the person feel' what they made us feel. Why? Though we rarely admit it, it is so they might understand".
But the opposite happens: "Rather than the root issues (feelings of being unheard, rejection, loneliness) that need healing, the words or actions of retaliatory aggression take center stage in the conflict. The core feelings remain unaddressed, while couples get lost in endless arguments".
Why people do this
This is not an attempt to restore connection. This is a punitive measure: "You hurt me, so I will hurt you back, and harder, so you will UNDERSTAND what you did."
The logic: "If I make them feel what I felt (only worse), they'll finally understand."
But this completely fails, because now the conversation can only be about the disproportionate retaliatory aggression, not about the original problem.
What happens as a result
In our example:
The late partner was actually wrong
But now they can't even raise the issue about it
Because discussing the lateness would require acknowledging that the three-day boycott was a "normal" response
Which no adequate person can do
The original grievance doesn't get resolved
It will fester and repeat
The relationship enters a toxic cycle
Therapeutic approach
For the one who escalates: "You had a real reason to be upset. But when your reaction crossed into [three-day boycott], it created a new and more serious problem. This doesn't mean your initial feeling was wrong — it means the way you expressed it made it impossible for your partner to hear you. Your pain matters, but so does how you handle it."
For the recipient: "Yes, their reaction was disproportionate and that's unacceptable. But under that escalation was real pain that still needs to be addressed. After you're safe and the escalation is dealt with, the original issue still exists."
Goal of therapy: To acknowledge that disproportionate retaliatory aggression creates a separate, new problem that must be addressed before the original issue can be considered.
Two interesting facts:
Linguistics: In Russian, the word "mest'" (revenge) comes from Proto-Slavic mьstь, related to Lithuanian mištas (to mix, to blend), which etymologically connects the idea of retribution with disruption of order and creation of chaos in relationships — exactly what happens with disproportionate retaliatory aggression.
Neurobiology of emotional regulation: Research shows that when a person experiences perceived injustice, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) activates, associated with planning retribution. However, during emotional flooding, this same area deactivates, and control shifts to the amygdala, which explains why retaliatory aggression is often disproportionate — rational assessment of the response scale is temporarily unavailable to the brain.
