I have an idea. I think it may have interesting ramifications. It comes in the form of a meta-scientific axiom, which states that physical quantities must be measurable in some sense, and so they must be well-defined in the language they are described. Non-physical quantities can be useful, and it may employ mathematics that causes things to be infinite or undefined at times. But these things are not true physical quantities. This meta scientific axiom gets at the heart of what is real

This is a fascinating philosophical proposition that touches on deep questions in the philosophy of science and measurement theory. Your axiom seems to combine elements of operationalism (the idea that scientific concepts must be defined in terms of measurement procedures) with a linguistic/mathematical constraint on physical quantities.
Let me explore some implications of this axiom:
It would suggest that any "real" physical quantity must have both:
A practical or theoretical measurement procedure
A mathematically well-defined description free of infinities or undefined values
This could have interesting implications for how we think about quantities in quantum mechanics. The uncertainty principle, for instance, might be seen as defining the fundamental limits of what constitutes a "well-defined" measurement.
It raises interesting questions about idealizations in physics. Take point particles or infinite potential wells - are these useful mathematical tools that represent non-physical quantities, by your axiom?
This reminds me of Eugene Wigner's famous essay on "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences" - but your axiom suggests almost the opposite: that physical reality constrains which mathematics can represent it.
What fascinates me is how your axiom might help distinguish between mathematical artifacts and physical reality. For instance, would you consider singularities in general relativity to be indicators that our mathematical description has strayed from physical quantities into useful but non-physical mathematics?

This is a profound insight. Following your reasoning: if spacetime curvature is a physical quantity, it must be well-defined everywhere it exists. The fact that general relativity predicts singularities (points where curvature becomes infinite and physics breaks down) might indicate that we're treating curvature as more fundamental than it really is.
This connects to several interesting threads:
The notion that general relativity, despite its extraordinary success, may be an effective field theory rather than a fundamental description. Just as we now understand fluid dynamics as emerging from more fundamental molecular behavior, perhaps spacetime curvature emerges from more basic physical quantities that remain well-defined even at extreme scales.
Various approaches to quantum gravity (string theory, loop quantum gravity, causal dynamical triangulations) often have as one of their goals the resolution of classical singularities. Your axiom suggests this isn't just a technical necessity but a fundamental requirement - if these theories still contain true singularities, they may not be describing physical quantities.
The holographic principle and AdS/CFT correspondence might be relevant here - they suggest that what appears as curved spacetime in one description can be recast as quantum fields living on a boundary. Perhaps one description captures physical quantities better than the other?
What do you think about quantum fields in this context? They're also mathematically problematic (requiring renormalization to handle infinities), yet they lead to some of the most precise predictions in physics. Would you consider them physical quantities under your axiom?
