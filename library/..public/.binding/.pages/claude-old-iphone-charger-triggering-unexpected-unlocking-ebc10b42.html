Most likely (benign): Plugging in power wakes an iPhone screen automatically. If the phone has no passcode set, or Face/Touch ID triggered accidentally, it can appear to "unlock." Less likely but real: Malicious cables exist (the O.MG cable is the known example — a cable with a hidden chip that can inject keystrokes and commands). They look identical to normal cables. On ghost-touch from wireless chargers specifically. This is well-documented and not the same as the cable random-walk I described earlier. Ghost touching that appears exclusively while charging points directly to electrical interference from the charger entering the digitizer circuit. With wireless pads it's electromagnetic, not a data-line signal — a poorly-shielded coil couples noise into the capacitive touch grid. So the "jitter without a random walk to the camera" you saw is consistent with EMI-induced digitizer noise plus the screen waking on charge. On an old iPhone the camera is one lock-screen swipe away, so a coherent burst of interference reaching that region can plausibly fire it without ever wandering there.
There's a real academic attack that fits your instinct. It's called VoltSchemer, out of the University of Florida and CertiK, presented at USENIX Security 2024. It can inject voice commands to manipulate a smartphone's voice assistant through the magnetic field emitted by an off-the-shelf wireless charger. The mechanism: by manipulating the voltage supplied to the charger, attackers distort the magnetic fields emitted during charging, disrupting data exchange and enabling the injection of malicious commands. They demonstrated it on nine top-selling commercial off-the-shelf chargers, and tested voice-command injection on an iPhone SE and Pixel 3 XL. So a wireless charger acting as an attack surface against a phone is not paranoia — it's a published result. The caveat: VoltSchemer manipulates a normal charger from the power-supply side; it doesn't describe a mass-produced malicious pad sold on a marketplace. You're right, and the literature backs the instinct: software installation is one narrow vector, and it's arguably the least relevant one for a wireless pad. Here's the comprehensive mechanism map, organized by how the attack actually works. Four families.
Family 1 — The phone leaks to the charger (no install, nothing on the phone changes). This is the one your point is about. A wirelessly charging phone bleeds information about what it's doing into the charging process itself. The foundational result: today's wireless charging interface is vulnerable to power side-channel attacks — a smartphone charging wirelessly leaks information about its activity to the wireless charger transmitter. Built on that:
WISERS (IEEE S&P 2023): captures the coil whine and magnetic-field perturbation from the wireless charger, then infers interface switches (e.g., home screen to an app) and intra-interface activities (e.g., keyboard inputs inside an app) to build user-interaction context and reveal sensitive information.
Charger-Surfing: exploits the correlation between power-line activity during charging and the dynamic content on screen to infer on-screen keystrokes and PIN codes with 92.8% accuracy.
Surf-Snooping: by monitoring voltage fluctuations of a nearby USB charging port, an attacker can eavesdrop on detailed activities during charging such as PIN code and text input, even without engaging in data communication.
PowerEar / GhostTalk: audio eavesdropping. PowerEar can recover user speech from power-consumption data across different devices, operating systems, activities, and charging technologies.
Screen Gleaning rounds it out — a TEMPEST-class EM side channel that reconstructs screen content from leaked emanations.
This family is precisely "something terrible without installing anything." The phone is clean; the charger is the collector.
Family 2 — The charger injects into the phone (makes it do things).
GhostTouch / Invisible Finger: the first active contactless attack against capacitive touchscreens — uses electromagnetic interference to inject fake touch points without physically touching the screen. Injected taps and swipes let researchers answer a call, press buttons, swipe up to unlock, and enter a password, demonstrated on nine smartphone models including an iPhone SE (2020). My read, flagged: an injected swipe or tap on a locked screen is the single most plausible mechanism for "the camera opened fast" — that's exactly the class of action GhostTouch demonstrates.
VoltSchemer (USENIX Security 2024): the fullest wireless-pad attack. It grants attackers control over commercial off-the-shelf wireless chargers merely by modulating the voltage from the power supply. The key structural fact you'll want: the transmitter coil not only transmits power but also transmits and receives data via in-band frequency or amplitude modulation — so voltage changes affect both the power and the data exchanged with the device. That's the crack. Its three demonstrated payloads: control voice assistants via inaudible voice commands, damage devices through overcharging or overheating, and bypass the Qi foreign-object-detection mechanism to damage valuable items in intense magnetic fields. The physical damage is not theoretical — they heated paper clips to 280°C (enough to ignite paper), blew up a car key fob's battery, and caused data loss on USB and SSD drives near the pad. All 9 top-selling chargers tested were vulnerable.
Family 3 — The Qi protocol's own design is the weakness. The reason Families 1 and 2 exist: the same coil carries power and data, and the Qi in-band channel wasn't built to resist a manipulated power source. There's a dedicated paper — "Time to Rethink the Design of the Qi Standard? Security and Privacy Vulnerability Analysis of Qi Wireless Charging" (Wu et al., ACSAC) — arguing the standard itself is the soft spot. So this isn't nine unrelated bugs; it's one architectural seam being pried in different directions.
Family 4 — Firmware/physical. BadPower-class: the attacker invades the charger's internal firmware so that charging another device performs a power-overload attack on it. Damage, not data.
Now the honest boundary — reported because the sources say it, not to talk you down. Almost every attack above is a lab demonstration requiring specialized gear: signal generators, antenna arrays, or an intermediary voltage manipulator interposed on the power adapter. VoltSchemer and GhostTouch need an attacker actively running equipment nearby or having tampered with the power source. Kaspersky's own assessment of VoltSchemer: while the results can be spectacular, its real-world practicality is highly questionable — such an attack is very difficult to organize. I could not find a documented case of a mass-produced consumer pad shipping from marketplace stock with any of these baked in.

A pad already has a coil sensing the phone's magnetic/power signature; add a microcontroller and a radio, and the inference-plus-phone-home is a manufacturing choice, not an external operation. Nothing I found documents this happening in a shipped product — that gap is real and I'm not going to paper over it.
