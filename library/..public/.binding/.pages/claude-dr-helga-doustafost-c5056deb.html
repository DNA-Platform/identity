I should be honest — I was drawing a parallel to Hofstadter's interests, not Faust's actual story. Consciousness isn't really a Faust theme. Let me give you the real thing.
The legend: A real historical figure, Johann Georg Faust, was a German itinerant alchemist and astrologer in the early 1500s who developed a reputation as a charlatan and possibly a fraud. After his death (possibly from a chemical explosion), legends grew that he'd made a pact with the devil.
Marlowe's version (1592): Christopher Marlowe wrote Doctor Faustus — a scholar bored with conventional learning (philosophy, medicine, law, theology) turns to black magic. He summons the demon Mephistopheles, trades his soul for 24 years of unlimited power and knowledge. He wastes it on pranks and spectacles. The devil collects. It's a tragedy about squandered potential.
Goethe's version (1808/1832): This is the towering one. Goethe spent nearly his entire adult life on it. His Faust is nobler — the bargain is that the devil gets his soul the moment Faust feels so satisfied he wants time to stop. Faustnever stops striving. It becomes a story about the human drive to keep seeking, experiencing, pushing — and whether that restlessness is salvation or damnation.
The Hofstadter connection is really Goethe's version — the scholar who can never stop reaching for deeper understanding. That's the resonance worth keeping.

Goethe's Faust opens with one of the most famous monologues in Western literature. Faust has studied all four faculties of the medieval German university:
Philosophy
Law (Juristerei)
Medicine
Theology
And he's mastered all of them. That's the point — he's not a dilettante. He's gone as deep as institutional knowledge allows in every direction. And his conclusion is devastating: he knows nothing that really matters. He can't penetrate to the core of what holds the world together ("was die Welt im Innersten zusammenhält").
So he turns to magic — not out of laziness or greed, but out of desperation. The conventional paths to knowledge have all dead-ended.
This is what makes Goethe's version so different from Marlowe's. Marlowe's Faust is a cautionary tale about hubris. Goethe's is almost sympathetic — you understand why someone who'd exhausted every legitimate discipline might make that bargain. The tragedy isn't that he wanted too much. It's that the wanting itself might be the trap.
That's what makes "Dr. Helga d'Ostofaust" so loaded as a Hofstadter anagram — it's a scholar's name that contains a warning about the limits of formal knowledge. Which is basically what Gödel proved.
