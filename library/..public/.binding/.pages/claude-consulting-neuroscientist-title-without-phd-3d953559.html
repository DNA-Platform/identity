I am published in nature neuroscience but I have a masters in computational neuroscience and not a phd. What might a good title be for this consultancy position be? Do research. Short answer: nothing prohibits it. "Neuroscientist" is not a protected or licensed title anywhere in the US, unlike physician, psychologist, or professional engineer. Licensing is generally not required for neuroscientists doing only research; it matters only when the role involves diagnosing or treating patients.
The constraint is conventional, not legal. The standard usage is that a neuroscientist commonly holds a doctorate, so in the specific audience you care about (PIs, department admin, grant personnel pages) the word carries a doctoral presumption. The cost is asymmetric: it buys you nothing with people who will read your CV anyway, and it costs you if someone notices the gap. Role-descriptive titles don't have that exposure and read as more senior, not less, because they say what you do.
Options that carry the substance:
Computational Neuroscience Consultant — plainest, no credential claim, says exactly the thing
Consulting Computational Scientist — "computational scientist" has no doctoral convention attached to it; this is the closest you get to "consulting neuroscientist" without the freight
Scientific Consultant, Computational Neuroscience — good for formal/contract contexts
Research Consultant (Neural Data Analysis / Encoding Models) — most specific, best when the parenthetical is true
One thing worth raising with the PI: rather than self-describing, ask whether the lab can put you up for a non-employee academic appointment. Most institutions have these categories, and they're conferred by the department rather than claimed. UT Austin, for example, appoints non-employee research affiliates as visiting researchers, scholars, or visiting scientists, and MIT's Visiting Scientist appointment covers people actively participating in research who aren't employees. A department-granted "Visiting Scientist, Department of Neuroscience, Baylor College of Medicine" is unimpeachable in a way no self-description is, and those categories generally hinge on the sponsoring faculty's judgment rather than a degree requirement.
Separately: the Nature Neuroscience paper does more work than any title will. On a bio line, "published inNature Neuroscience; consulting on computational analysis at Baylor College of Medicine" lands harder than any noun you could pick.

That works well. It puts "research" in the role rather than in your credentials, so it describes the work without asserting a rank, and "neuroscience" stays as the domain modifier rather than the noun you're claiming to be.
It also scales: if the collaboration turns into authorship or a formal appointment, the title doesn't need revising, it just gets supplemented.
