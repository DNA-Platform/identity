You're right on every count. Let me think through what you're saying rather than rush to rewrite.
On "spawn": Dead wrong. An experiment is inert. It sits there with its question and its evidence. The experimentalist looks at the evidence, interprets it, and asks a new question. The relationship between experiments is mediated entirely by reasoning—by inference. "Spawns" pretends the experiment has agency. "Inspires" is closer but you're right that it belongs to the experimentalist, not to the experiment. The honest description is: the experimentalist infers a new question from the evidence of a prior experiment. The prior experiment doesn't do anything. It just has evidence that, interpreted, suggests something worth asking.
On questions: You're right that even exploration has questions. I was wrong to say surveys have "no question." They have questions—often many, often vague, often unarticulable in advance—but they have them. The difference isn't presence versus absence of a question. It's how constrained the question is and whether the answer has a known shape. "What does the crypto DD market look like on Fiverr?" is a question. It just doesn't resolve to a boolean.
On the identity of an experiment: This is where I need to think harder. You're proposing that an experiment might be its question. That the question is the identity. And in market research specifically, you're asking: is the question always about a service provider (or collection of service providers)?
I think it's almost that, but not quite. Consider:
"What does seller X's gig look like?" — question about a service provider. Yes.
"What do buyers say in reviews of crypto DD gigs?" — question about buyers, but in the context of a service category. The subject is buyer behavior within a market.
"Can I produce this deliverable in 4 hours?" — question about yourself. The subject is your own capability relative to the market.
"Does Fiverr surface crypto DD gigs when you search 'token analysis'?" — question about the platform as a mechanism.
So the subject is always something in or about the market—a provider, a buyer, a platform, or yourself-in-relation-to-the-market. Market research is questioning the market. The market includes providers, buyers, platforms, and your position relative to all three.
On logical semantics: You're pointing at something I missed entirely. An experiment has the structure of an argument, not the structure of an organism. It has:
Question — what we want to know
Assumption — what we take for granted going in (including, for tests, our prediction)
Evidence — what we observe
Inference — what we conclude from the evidence given the assumptions
Conclusion — the answer to the question, qualified by confidence
And the relationship between experiments is inferential: the conclusion of one experiment, combined with the experimentalist's reasoning, generates the question of the next. The word for this in logic is derivation. You derive a new question from prior conclusions. That's precise, it's about reasoning, and it doesn't assign agency to the experiment.
When does a conclusion become strong enough to act on? You said it well: maybe it's a theorem when we have sufficient evidence. Not mathematical certainty—just enough confidence that we're willing to commit to it as a basis for action. A finding is provisional. A theorem is something we're willing to build on. That's a useful distinction for knowing when a mission is complete: when its key findings have hardened into theorems.
On the distinction between experiment and interpretation: This is the distinction I was collapsing everywhere. The experiment proper is: question, method, evidence. These are somewhat objective—someone else could look at the same evidence. The interpretation is: inference, conclusion, derived questions. These belong to the experimentalist. The spec should honor this boundary. The "Record" section is experiment. The "Findings" section is interpretation. And the link between experiments lives entirely in interpretation, which is why it can't be encoded as a structural property of the experiment itself (like "spawns").
On elegance: I don't have it yet. What I have is: an experiment is a question pursued through observation, producing evidence, which is interpreted through inference into conclusions that may derive new questions. The terms are question, assumption, evidence, inference, conclusion, derivation. The subject in market research is always an entity in the market (provider, buyer, platform, self-in-market). The character (survey/probe/test) describes the constraint on the question.
But I haven't found the single elegant structure that makes all of this feel like one thing rather than a list of parts. I think that structure might be: an experiment is a question and its evidence. Everything else—assumptions, inference, conclusions—is reasoning about the experiment, not the experiment itself. The experiment is just the asking and the observing. The thinking happens around it.
Does that distinction feel right? And does "question about a market entity" work as the identity schema for market research experiments specifically? I want to get the conceptual structure right before I touch the document again.
