Here's the landscape as of now, split into "purchase" and "qualify" routes. One structural point upfront that shapes everything: a minor can't be a primary applicant anywhere. Dima would enter any purchased citizenship as a dependent on Ana's (or possibly your) application — and most programs require the other parent's notarized consent to include a minor. Maxim's veto tends to follow the child into these programs the same way it follows him into the passport office. Programs differ on whether a sole-custody ruling substitutes for consent, so that's the first question to put to any agent.
Purchase (CBI) options still open to Russian nationals:
Turkey — $400k real estate, family included, Russians accepted, no residency requirement. Strongest of the accessible passports; ~6–8 months.
Nauru — reclassified Russia from "prohibited" to "restricted" in January 2026; ~$105k donation. Weak passport, but explicitly built for excluded applicant categories.
São Tomé & Príncipe — ~$90k; applies standard due diligence with no nationality bar; Russians can apply if funds are clean.
Egypt — accepting Russian applicants via real estate or bank deposit routes.
Vanuatu — $130k and historically Russian-friendly, but one source reports Russians banned from the program as of June 2026. Needs verification before spending time on it.
Closed: Caribbean Five largely bar Russians; EU investor citizenship is dead (ECJ, April 2025). Argentina's new CBI program is framework-complete but not yet open for applications as of mid-2026 — worth watching, since Argentina has no Russian-nationality restrictions.
Qualify (non-purchase) avenues:
Georgian humanitarian travel document — already your strongest track; it's the only one designed for exactly this fact pattern (documented ban, custody record, no father's consent needed if granted).
Descent claims through Ana's line — the highest-value question nobody's answered yet: any Jewish ancestry (Israel's Law of Return covers grandchildren and gives immediate citizenship to the whole family), or Polish/Romanian/Latvian/German ancestry. This is the one route where a real passport could arrive without either money or Maxim.
Naturalization-by-residence in a fast country — Argentina grants citizenship after 2 years' residence, including to minors residing with a parent; no Russian restrictions. Requires actually relocating there, so it's a fallback, not a fix.
The honest summary: the purchase routes cost $90k–400k, mostly buy weak passports, and still likely collide with the consent problem. The Georgian document plus a serious ancestry audit of Ana's family tree are the two moves with the best ratio of feasibility to cost. Do you know whether anyone has actually mapped Ana's ancestry for descent eligibility?

Concretely, three things — and one thing it could actively hurt.
What it buys:
A renewable travel document with no Maxim hook in it. That's the core asset. Once the Russian passport expires, Dima can't cross any border and can't even apply for visas — every visa application requires a valid passport. A São Tomé passport renews for life without anyone's consent. It converts a wasting asset (two-year window) into a permanent one.
Yes to Georgian residence. A residence permit application requires a valid travel document, and São Tomé nationality is treated the same as any other. He'd still need a ground — family reunification through Ana's status, or study — but the passport satisfies the document prerequisite that the blocked Russian renewal would otherwise break.
School registration doesn't strictly require it — Georgian schools enroll foreign children fairly liberally, and the kids are on Foxford anyway — but valid ID plus a residence permit makes everything administrative in Georgia (school, clinics, banking as he gets older) frictionless instead of improvised.
The thing it could hurt: your EU strategy, as I understand it, runs partly through humanitarian/protection channels built on documented inability to obtain protection in Russia. Protection claims are assessed against every country of nationality the applicant holds. A child who holds a purchased São Tomé citizenship is, on paper, a child with a functioning second nationality and a working passport — which can undercut both a Georgian humanitarian travel document application (premised on him having no route to a document) and a later EU protection claim. If the EU path ends up running through ordinary visas and residence permits instead, the São Tomé passport is purely helpful.
So the real decision isn't "is $90k worth a weak passport" — it's which EU pathway you're committing to, because this purchase strengthens one and weakens the other. That seems like the question to resolve with the legal team before anything else.
