There you go — 12 randomly generated, provably valid Dutch BSNs. Here's the summary and the broader question you asked:
The Dutch BSN Elfproef
The algorithm is elegant. For a 9-digit number ABCDEFGHI, you compute:
S = 9A + 8B + 7C + 6D + 5E + 4F + 3G + 2H − 1·I
and S must be divisible by 11, positive, with at most one leading zero. That's it. The key quirk is that last weight being −1 instead of +1 (which is what old Dutch bank account numbers used). This means the check digit N9 isn't simply "the remainder" — it's determined by N9 ≡ partial_sum (mod 11), and if that remainder equals 10, no valid single digit exists, so that combination of first-8-digits is simply impossible. This means roughly 1 in 11 arbitrary 8-digit prefixes can't produce a valid BSN, which is a nice density property.
Do All ID Numbers Have This Kind of Structure?
Short answer: almost all of them do, but the specific mechanisms vary quite a bit. The general pattern is"structured digits + check digit(s) computed via some modular arithmetic." Some examples:
Same family (weighted sum mod prime):
US Social Security Numbers are the notable exception — they were purely sequential/regional with no check digit at all until randomization in 2011, and still have no algorithmic validity check. Any 9-digit number in the right format "looks" valid.
ISBN-10 uses the exact same mod-11 weighted sum idea (weights 10,9,8...1), with 'X' representing 10 when needed.
ISBN-13 / EAN switched to mod-10 with alternating weights of 1 and 3.
Luhn algorithm family (mod 10):
Credit card numbers (Visa, Mastercard, etc.) all use the Luhn algorithm — double every other digit, sum the results, must be divisible by 10. Much simpler than the elfproef but same spirit.
IMEI numbers (cell phone identifiers) also use Luhn.
Canadian SINs (Social Insurance Numbers) use Luhn.
More exotic structures:
Brazilian CPF uses two cascading check digits, each computed from the previous digits with different weight sequences (mod 11).
Chinese national ID is 18 digits where the last is a check digit computed via mod 11 with weights derived from powers of 2, and 'X' can appear for remainder 2.
Estonian, Finnish, Lithuanian IDs encode birth dates directly into the number, then append a check digit.
UK National Insurance Numbers have a letter-number-letter format with specific letter exclusions but no mathematical check digit — validation is purely structural/syntactic.
The general taxonomy is roughly:
Check-digit IDs (most countries): Mathematical validation catches typos. Dutch BSN, ISBN, credit cards, Brazilian CPF, etc.
Structurally-constrained IDs (some countries): Format rules and embedded semantics (birth date, region codes) but no check digit. UK NINO, old US SSN.
Hybrid (increasingly common): Embedded information plus check digits. Chinese national ID, many European national IDs.
The trend globally is toward check-digit systems because they're so effective at catching the two most common human errors: transposing two adjacent digits and mistyping a single digit. The mod-11 approach (like the elfproef) catches all single-digit errors and all adjacent transpositions — which is mathematically provable and is exactly why it was designed this way. Mod-10 (Luhn) catches all single-digit errors but misses some transpositions involving 0↔5, which is why higher-stakes identifiers tend to prefer mod-11.
It's a genuinely beautiful corner of applied number theory — simple enough to compute by hand, powerful enough to make entire classes of errors impossible.

Yes, each of those 12 passes all constraints — the arithmetic is verified line by line.
And to your second question: no, the BSN encodes nothing. It's deliberately opaque. The first 8 digits are generated randomly, the 9th is determined by the elfproef, and that's it. No birth date, no gender, no region, no municipality, no sequence information.
This is actually a deliberate design choice that represents a philosophical shift. The BSN's predecessor, the SoFi number (Sofinummer), did encode personal information — birth date and gender were embedded in the digits. When the Netherlands replaced the SoFi with the BSN in 2007, they intentionally stripped out all semantic content and made it a pure random identifier + check digit. The IRI documentation explicitly notes that their BSN generator function takes no parameters "because there is no personal information used to generate the BSN," whereas their SoFi generator requires PII inputs.
This is the privacy-by-design approach: the number is a meaningless pointer into a database, not a compressed representation of who you are. Knowing someone's BSN tells you literally nothing about them — you can't reverse-engineer their age, location, or anything else from the digits alone.
It's a surprisingly clean design compared to many national ID systems. The Chinese national ID, for example, embeds your province, city, district, birth date, gender, and a check digit all in 18 digits — which means the number itself is a privacy leak. The BSN avoids this entirely. The only constraint on the number space is mathematical (the elfproef), which exists purely for error detection, not information encoding.
