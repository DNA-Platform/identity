You're right on the money. Georgia (the country) caps nicotine concentration for e-liquids at 20 mg/ml — which is exactly 2%. That's the same limit the EU uses under its Tobacco Products Directive, and since Georgia has EU-related aspirations, its legal framework has been aligned accordingly.
So yes, they've essentially taken the EU approach: vapes are legal, but with a nicotine ceiling that's much lower than what you'd find in, say, Turkey or the US (where 5%/50mg products are common). Georgia's law doesn't restrict e-cigarette flavors, and the law doesn't regulate nicotine-free e-cigarettes at all — only nicotine-containing products fall under tobacco control law.
A few other things worth knowing for your move: vaping is prohibited in public places and on public transport, online sales of e-cigarettes are banned, and sale to minors under 18 is prohibited. Advertising and promotion of nicotine-containing e-cigarettes is also prohibited.
So the short answer to your question: yes, that's exactly what happened — they accommodated vapes by capping nicotine at the EU standard of 2%, which is why that's what you're seeing on shelves.

Searched the web for “epibatidine nicotinic acetylcholine receptor analgesic mechanism”

Your intuition here is well-supported by the literature, and actually connects two distinct mechanisms that I think together tell a more complete story than either alone.
The analgesic angle: You're right that epibatidine reframed the field. Nicotine's antinociceptive properties were actually first reported as early as 1932, but the area remained relatively unexplored until epibatidine was isolated and shown to be a potent non-opioid analgesic working entirely through nAChRs. The analgesic property is specifically attributed to binding at the α4β2 subtype of nicotinic receptors, and epibatidine is 100-200x more potent than morphine through this mechanism. The key insight you're drawing — that nicotine is a weaker agonist at the same receptor that produces profound analgesia when fully activated — is exactly the right framework. There's even research showing that individuals with chronic pain may be driven to smoke more because the analgesic efficacy of nicotine diminishes over time, which directly supports your hypothesis that persistent low-grade pain relief is part of the dependency cycle.
The breathing connection: This one is really interesting and has solid mechanistic backing. Nicotine stimulates respiration by activating nicotinic ACh receptors in the carotid body chemoreceptors, and when nicotine is administered locally into the ventrolateral medulla, respiration significantly increases through activation of central chemoreceptors. More importantly, nicotinic receptors are involved in maintaining respiratory neuronal activity — meaning they're not just modulating breathing, they're part of the tonic drive to breathe. Low doses of nicotine increase breathing rate by stimulating chemoreceptors in the carotid artery and aorta.
So here's what I think you're onto: if nicotine is tonically facilitating both pain modulation (via α4β2 in descending pain pathways) AND respiratory drive (via carotid body and medullary chemoreceptors), then withdrawal would simultaneously produce a subtle increase in background pain sensitivity AND a subtle decrease in the felt ease of breathing. Neither effect would be dramatic enough to be clinically alarming, but both would be persistent and diffuse — exactly the kind of low-level aversive state that's incredibly hard to articulate but powerfully motivating. The "I can't quite breathe right" feeling you describe maps perfectly onto what would happen if tonic nicotinic facilitation of chemoreceptor sensitivity was suddenly removed — your respiratory drive doesn't fail, but the gain on the system drops, so breathing feels slightly effortful in a way it didn't before.
The combination is arguably worse than either alone, because you'd have persistent mild dysphoria from two completely separate physiological systems that the conscious brain would struggle to attribute to nicotine withdrawal at all. Most people would just say "I feel bad" without being able to identify why.

The analgesia and the respiratory arrest are the same continuum, not two different pharmacological effects that happen to co-occur.
