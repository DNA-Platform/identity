The cicadas you'd encounter around Tbilisi are the stout, bark-colored annual type — not the flashy red-eyed periodical broods people associate with the US. The common one in this region is the ash cicada (Cicada orni): roughly 3–4 cm long, with a broad blunt head, wide-set eyes, and a mottled olive-brown-to-gray body that camouflages beautifully against tree bark. Its signature feature is the wings — clear and glassy, but with a row of distinct dark spots spaced along them, which is the easiest way to tell it apart from other species.
You may also get the larger Lyristes plebejus (one of Europe's biggest, pushing 5 cm) with plainer clear wings, and up in cooler wooded hills the smaller mountain cicada Cicadetta montana.
Mostly you'll hear them before you ever see one — they sing from high in the tree canopy in the hottest part of the day and cling flat to trunks and branches, so they're easy to miss even when a whole tree is buzzing. If you spot a discarded amber-brown exoskeleton split down the back on a tree trunk, that's the nymph's shed skin after the adult climbed out.

Your instinct about a trade-off is genuinely sound — crypsis and mass-synchronized emergence really are two alternative anti-predator strategies, and you can even watch the trade play out within the periodical cicadas: those Magicicada adults essentially abandoned camouflage. Red eyes, loud, flying clumsily in broad daylight — they're the opposite of hidden. They bet everything on swamping predators with sheer numbers instead of on not being found. So the axis you're reaching for (hide vs. overwhelm) is real.
Where the causal story bends is the direction of the question. The prime-number cycle isn't the baseline that Georgian cicadas "opted out" of — it's a freak outlier. Of ~3,000 cicada species worldwide, exactly one genus in eastern North America ever evolved it. So the puzzle isn't "why don't Georgia's cicadas have it," it's "why does that one weird lineage have it at all." And notice: the black-and-green M cicadas you remember from back home are annual, not periodical — same as Georgia's. They're also camouflaged, just against foliage instead of bark. North America's default cicada is a cryptic annual too. The periodical ones are the exception on their own continent.
The prime cycle itself is doing two jobs. Long intervals plus tight synchrony starve predators — no predator can build up a specialized population to track prey that only shows up once in 13 or 17 years, and when they do show up, the glut satiates everything and the survivors still breed. The primeness on top of that minimizes resonance: a 13- or 17-year brood almost never co-emerges with a broodmate of a different cycle length (they'd sync only every 221 years), which limits hybridization, and it also can't be tracked by any predator on a shorter multi-year population cycle. Then Yoshimura's hypothesis roots the whole thing in eastern North America's Pleistocene glaciation — cold lengthening nymphal development, synchrony needed to beat mate-finding problems at low density. It's a deeply history-contingent local accident, not a general response to predation pressure.
Which is why the bird premise doesn't quite carry the weight. Bird predation on cicadas is generalist and satiable — they gorge but can't numerically respond fast enough within a season to be the selective lever, so local bird density isn't really what tunes cicada strategy. And I'd gently poke the premise itself: Georgia is actually one of the great bird corridors on Earth — the Batumi bottleneck funnels literally millions of migrating raptors down the Black Sea coast each autumn. What you're noticing in Saburtalo in July is probably a real local, seasonal thinness, not a country-level scarcity worth building a theory on.
So the honest synthesis: camouflage isn't Georgia's substitute for the prime cycle. Cryptic annual is the global default both continents share; the prime cycle is a rare experiment layered on top of it in one glaciated corner of North America.

You've got the key fact right, and it undoes the clean "annual vs periodical" split I leaned on. Annual cicadas aren't one-year insects. The dog-day cicadas (genus Neotibicen) spend two to five years underground, some species longer — up to about ten. They're called "annual" only because their generations overlap and stay unsynchronized, so some fraction surfaces every summer. The University of Connecticut's cicada group states it flatly: non-periodical cicadas have multiple-year life cycles and are called annual purely because adults appear every year in a given spot. So the real difference between your New York cicadas and Magicicada is not the length of the underground phase — both are multi-year — it's whether the population emerges in lockstep. That's the whole distinction. Everything else is shared.
Your New York ones, by the description — black eyes, dark shell, green "M" — are those annual Neotibicen. Green wing venation and dark eyes is the dog-day signature. Magicicada look nothing like that: red eyes, black body, orange wing veins. So you've almost certainly never seen a periodical cicada; what you remember is the annual kind, which fits your own report that the big choruses come and go irregularly rather than on a clock. (New York does have a 17-year brood in the Hudson Valley, last up in 2013, but that's the red-eyed animal, not yours.)
On the "seven-year" cicada: there isn't one. Magicicada come in 13 and 17 only. The seven-year figure is a folk memory that doesn't correspond to a real periodical species. Interestingly, other lineages did evolve their own periodicities at different intervals — a four-year species in northeast India, an eight-year one in Fiji — just never seven, and never in North America. And the scale question: those seven Magicicada species are a rounding error against the ~3,000 cicada species worldwide, well under a percent. But in raw bodies during an emergence they're staggering — densities up to about 1.5 million per acre, orders of magnitude beyond what any annual species reaches. So "a lot of them in North America" is true in exactly one sense: not many species, not present most years, but astronomically abundant in the years and places they do surface.
Now your real argument — that this is "opt-out," because a species already running a multi-year cycle is a short step from periodicity. You're half right in a way that's worth taking seriously, and half overshooting. The half that's right: the expensive-looking ingredient, the long subterranean development, is already sitting there in nearly every cicada on Earth. Periodicity doesn't have to build that. It only has to add synchronization and lock the interval. So the barrier is genuinely lower than the "annual vs. 17-year" phrasing makes it sound, and I shouldn't have implied otherwise.
Where I'd push back is "not very hard." If it were easy given the raw material, it would have happened repeatedly, because the raw material is everywhere. Instead the full syndrome — tight population-wide synchrony, fixed at a large prime — evolved essentially once, in one genus, on one continent. That n=1 against a universal precursor is the actual puzzle, and it points to synchronization itself being the hard part, not the long cycle. The mechanism thought to lock it is an Allee effect: at low density a cicada that emerges in an off year finds no mates and leaves no offspring, so any deviation from the crowd is punished hard, which ratchets the whole population tighter into one year. That's brutally self-reinforcing once established but very hard to get started, because a rare mutant trying to synchronize is by definition in the minority and gets selected against — the same force that maintains synchrony resists creating it. Yoshimura's addition is that eastern North America's Pleistocene glaciation supplied the unusual cold-and-sparse conditions that could tip a population over that threshold. Take the glacial history away and you likely never cross it, which is why the rest of the world's cicadas, ingredients and all, stayed annual.
Your closing intuition actually names the precursor state precisely. Annual cicadas do have "something like the cycle living at the level of raw numbers" — their year-to-year abundance genuinely swings, boom years and lean years, driven by rainfall and generation overlap. That fluctuating, unsynchronized abundance is exactly the pool that selection would have to tip into lockstep. So it isn't that Georgia's cicadas opted out of a cycle they could easily have; it's that they're sitting in the ordinary, ancestral, mildly-fluctuating state that virtually all cicadas occupy — and the lockstep prime version is the strange thing that happened once, in a glaciated corner, and essentially nowhere else.

The maintenance mechanism first, because that's where the confirmation is cleanest. The synchrony is held in place by predator satiation and competitive exclusion, and off-schedule individuals — "stragglers" — do emerge every generation, at ±1 and ±4 years, and most are few in number and quickly annihilated by predators. That is your "off-cycle ones get eaten before they can seed a lineage," verbatim from the field. And your sharpening-by-external-forcing intuition — the pendulum — has been simulated explicitly. In one predator-satiation model, eliminating off-year stragglers sharpens the emergence peak, and a broad initial hump immediately steepens into a sharp peak, sometimes with a one-year phase shift, exactly the "start it with any wobble and it locks near the driving rhythm" behavior you described. The same simulations show the other limb you asked about: if predation is too light, timekeeping error accumulates and the peak broadens, or the brood crashes. So the answer to "would the cycle spread out if you removed the predators" is — in the models, yes. The physical experiment is essentially unrunnable (13–17 years, subterranean, no way to fence predators off a forest for that long), so it lives in the modeling, and the modeling backs your prediction.
And you're right that there's no cross-cicada chemical coordination. Each nymph runs its own internal clock — the current field-and-genomic picture is a "gate-and-threshold" counter that tracks seasons individually — and the population-level lockstep is imposed by selection killing off whoever emerges off-peak, not by signaling between individuals. Nobody's invoking a magic pheromonal sync. It's selection on the variance, as you said.
Now the origin, which is where your claim is most fully vindicated. There is a formal individual-based model (Tanaka, Yoshimura et al., Scientific Reports 2015, "Evolution of periodicity in periodical cicadas") that is your argument turned into equations. Its setup: a non-periodical ancestor already lays eggs that mature at slightly different times — some emerge a year early, some a year late — and those off-peak adults hit low density and can be lost without producing offspring, cutting adult density ~80% every generation; switching to fixed synchronized emergence eliminates that loss, which is what drives periodicity to fixation. That is precisely "the ones in the burst are the only ones that reproduce, so the burst sharpens." The model also frames the key innovation the way you did: maturation switches from size-dependence to time-dependence, and selection for the prime-numbered cycle only happens after periodicity is already fixed — i.e., getting a fixed non-annual clock at all is the hard step; 5→13→17 is downstream tuning. And on robustness: the 13- and 17-year cycles evolved at least eight separate times in the last ~4 million years, which is what you'd expect if this is variance-sorting on a pre-existing substrate rather than a rare genetic invention.
The one place I'd hand you a refinement rather than a straight yes — and it's a refinement inside your framework, not a correction of it. The origin models put the primary nonlinear squeeze on mate-finding failure, not predation. The trigger is glacial cooling: colder climate lengthens the juvenile stage, which drives adult densities extremely low, which limits mating opportunities and favors synchronized emergence. It's the same class of mechanism you're pointing at — a nonlinear penalty on being off-peak, driven by density — but the field leans on the Allee effect in mating (the "network effect" you named) more than on predators for the origin, and reserves predator satiation more for maintenance. Your mechanism is correct; the glaciation is the field's answer to why the mechanism got to run to completion there and not in Tbilisi's oaks.
