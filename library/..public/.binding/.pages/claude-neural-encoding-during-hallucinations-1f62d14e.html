The hallucination is always in the statistical null space of the stimulus, but not necessarily in the geometric null space of the code. Separating those two gives a clean result.
Setup (linear case). In session 1, R = WX + ε. In session 2, R′ = WX + h + ε, where h is internally generated and Cov(h, X) = 0 across trials. Let P be the projector onto col(W), the stimulus-driven subspace, and let W⁺ be the decoder.
Claim 1: the encoding model is blind to h. The OLS estimate is Ŵ′ = Cov(R′, X)Cov(X)⁻¹ = W, because the h term drops out. Fitting the encoder on either session gives the same W, so you could even recover the neuron correspondence you didn't know about by matching the columns of W. In this sense the hallucination is exactly "null": nothing that correlates with X can see it.
Claim 2: the decoder is not blind to h. X̂ = W⁺R′ = X + W⁺h + noise. The error term W⁺h vanishes if and only if h ⊥ col(W). That splits h into two parts:
Ph (in the stimulus subspace): here R′ = W(X + x_h) + ε with x_h = W⁺h. This is indistinguishable from the animal seeing X + x_h. The hallucination has content in the stimulus coordinates, and you can read that content out as the residual X̂ − X, but only because the experimenter knows X. From R′ alone it is just "a stimulus."
(I − P)h (the geometric null space): it shows up only as extra residual covariance, Σ′ − Σ = Σ_h, restricted to directions the code doesn't use for stimuli. You can detect it but can't label it. It has the same status as the movement subspace in Stringer 2019.
Information-theoretic version. With respect to X, h is noise, so I(X; R′) ≤ I(X; R). But h lives in H(R′ | X), the stimulus-unexplained entropy, which is not the same thing as "the directions the decoder ignores." A hallucination that uses the perceptual code sits in the conditional entropy while occupying the signal geometry.
What it is, in this context: a misrepresentation. It is a component that is signal in the code's own coordinates and noise in the world's coordinates. From third-person data you can know three things: that it occurred (residual covariance), what its content would be in stimulus terms (Ph), and how much is uninterpretable ((I − P)h). That it is perceived is the one piece the data can't supply. It's an account that depends on granting experience, and the formalism gives you the content without the seeing.

You're right that I skipped the definition. I also slipped in an additive decomposition without stating it. Here it is from the ground up.
Primitives
X: world state (stimulus) on trial t.
B: total physical brain state.
R ∈ ℝⁿ: recorded activity.
C: perceptual content, the experience.
v: the veridicality map, sending each world state x to the content a normal perceiver has given x.
Axioms
A1 (Supervenience). C = f(B). No difference in content without a difference in B.
A2 (Partial observation). R = π(B), where π is not injective.
A3 (Stimulus independence). The hallucination-generating process H satisfies I(H; X) = 0.
A4 (Decomposition). R′ = g(X) + h + ε, with h a function of H. This is optional, and the propositions below say where it is needed.
Definition (hallucination). Trial t is hallucinatory iff C_t ≠ v(X_t) and the discrepancy is not caused by X_t. The second clause is what separates hallucination from illusion, and A3 formalizes it.
Proposition 1: the hallucination need not be in R at all. By A1, a content difference requires a B difference. By A2, distinct B can share the same π(B). So a hallucination can leave R unchanged. This confirms your point: supervenience guarantees a physical difference somewhere, not in the recorded neurons.
Proposition 2: the converse fails too. A1 has no reverse direction. Some Δ in R can have no content difference at all, as with unconscious or state-driven activity. So R-activity uncorrelated with X does not indicate hallucination.
Proposition 3: if it is in R, it is in the null space of the stimulus, taken as a space of random variables. Consider L²(trials), the space of trial-indexed variables. Let 𝒮 be the closed span of functions of X. Under A3 and A4, h is independent of X, so E[h | X] = E[h]. The centered h is therefore orthogonal to 𝒮. The hallucination lies in 𝒮^⊥, always. This is your claim, and it holds.
Proposition 4: it need not be in the geometric null space of the code in ℝⁿ. Let S = span of the stimulus-driven directions, which is col(W) in the linear case. Take the counterexample h = W·x_h, with x_h drawn independently of X. Then h ∈ S, not S^⊥, yet Proposition 3 still holds. A hallucinated face that uses face-coding directions is exactly this case.
Why the two come apart. The null spaces live in different spaces. Proposition 3 concerns h as a variable across trials; Proposition 4 concerns h as a direction across neurons. One detail makes them easy to conflate: in the linear case, the neural directions u with Cov(uᵀR, X) = 0 are precisely S^⊥. But a signal uncorrelated with X can still point along S.
Verdict. "If it's anywhere, it's in the null space" is a theorem in L²(trials), given A3 and A4. It is false in neuron space. And even the true version locates only the projection of the supervenience base, never C itself. Without A4, "the hallucinated component" isn't even well-defined, because supervenience alone gives a difference in B, not an additive part of R.

What you're describing is an identifiability theorem. Stated carefully, it comes out slightly stronger than you guessed: in the basic setup, a hallucination is always observationally equivalent either to noise or to ordinary history-dependent encoding. Nothing is left over that could be labeled hallucination.
Primitives (your framework)
𝒳ₜ = (X₁, …, Xₜ): input history.
Zₜ: internal state, including context, arousal, and any generator.
Pₜ = Φ(𝒳ₜ, Zₜ): the representation. It integrates over inputs, is contextual, and carries artifacts. In general Pₜ ≠ Xₜ.
Qₜ = D(Pₜ): the percept, obtained by decoding the representation.
Rₜ = π(Pₜ, ·): the recorded activity, a lossy observation.
Data: only the joint law 𝓛(𝒳, R).
Decomposition (no independence axiom needed). Define m(𝒳) = E[R | 𝒳] and u = R − m(𝒳). By the L² projection theorem, u is uncorrelated with every function of 𝒳. This orthogonality holds by construction, so A3 from before is no longer required.
Definitions
Hallucination: a change in Q not accounted for by 𝒳, i.e. D reads some part of P as content.
Masking noise: variation in P that D does not read as content but that degrades Q's dependence on 𝒳. Your "can't see it because the band is noisy" is this case.
Both definitions refer to D. That is the crux.
Theorem (non-identifiability). Take two models M₁ and M₂ with the same law of 𝒳, the same m, and the same conditional law of u given 𝒳. In M₁, D treats u as content (hallucination). In M₂, D discards u or is corrupted by it (noise). Then 𝓛₁(𝒳, R) = 𝓛₂(𝒳, R).
Proof. The joint law of (𝒳, R) is determined by the law of 𝒳, the function m, and the law of u given 𝒳. D appears in none of these. ∎
Corollary 1 (your band case). Every statistic of (𝒳, R) is identical under the two models. This includes residual covariance, excess power in a frequency band, loss of I(𝒳; R), and decoder error. "Seeing something in that band" and "failing to see because that band is noisy" are the same dataset.
Corollary 2 (the history split). Because Φ integrates past inputs, any percept change that is predictable from 𝒳 is absorbed into m and looks like contextual encoding or adaptation. Any change that is unpredictable from 𝒳 lands in u and looks like noise. The two cases are exhaustive. So in the basic setup, a hallucination is always confounded with encoding or with noise, and the data cannot say which.
The result is nearly definitional, and that is its point. The label "hallucination" is a fact about D, and (𝒳, R) never samples D.
What breaks the equivalence. You need an observable that is a function of Q, such as behavior B = β(Q). Once D enters the law of (𝒳, R, B), the models separate. In signal-detection terms, noise lowers d′, while hallucination produces false alarms carried with hit-like confidence. Schmack et al. (2021, Science) used exactly this move in mice, with confidence measured as time invested in false alarms. Even then you identify D's output, not the experience, so it remains an account.
Prior work. The general form is observational equivalence in identification theory (Koopmans & Reiersøl 1950; Rothenberg 1971). The informal neuroscience version, that decodable information is only information relative to a reader, appears in de-Wit et al. (2016). I don't know of a formal statement specifically pairing hallucination with noise, so this construction may be new.

Here is the model from Definition 3, with dimensions.
Variables
X ∈ ℝᵏ is the stimulus, represented by k features. Its covariance is Σ_X ≻ 0.
Y ∈ ℝᴺ is the recorded activity of N neurons, with N ≥ k.
W ∈ ℝᴺˣᵏ is the encoding matrix, of rank k. Column j is the population's response pattern to stimulus feature j. Its column space 𝒮 = col W ⊆ ℝᴺ is the coding subspace.
E ∈ ℝᴺ is ordinary noise, a function of ξ, with mean 0 and covariance Σ₀ ≻ 0 (N×N).
H ∈ ℝᴺ is internally generated activity, a function of the latent Z, with mean 0 and covariance Σ_H ⪰ 0 (N×N). A concrete linear form is H = BZ with Z ∈ ℝᵐ and B ∈ ℝᴺˣᵐ, which gives Σ_H = B Σ_Z Bᵀ.
X, E, and H are mutually independent.
Generative relation
Y = WX + E + H
Implied moments
E[Y | X] = WX, the tuning.
Cov(Y | X) = Σ₀ + Σ_H, the noise covariance. It does not depend on the stimulus.
Cov(Y, X) = W Σ_X.
Cov(Y) = W Σ_X Wᵀ + Σ₀ + Σ_H, which is signal covariance plus noise covariance.
The full second-moment structure of the data is the block matrix
Cov([X; Y]) = [ Σ_X , Σ_X Wᵀ ; W Σ_X , W Σ_X Wᵀ + Σ₀ + Σ_H ]
What stimulus–response data determine
From this matrix you recover W = Cov(Y, X) Σ_X⁻¹ and the sum Σ₀ + Σ_H. The data never separate Σ₀ from Σ_H. That is the linear content of Lemma 3 and the reason Theorem 1 bites. Getting Σ₀ on its own takes something extra, such as a control session in which Σ_H = 0.
Readout (Definition 4)
Given an assumed noise covariance Σ̃, the readout reports:
the estimate X̂ = AY, where A = C̃ Wᵀ Σ̃⁻¹ is k×N;
the confidence C̃ = (Wᵀ Σ̃⁻¹ W)⁻¹, which is k×k.
Since AW = Iₖ, the error is X̂ − X = A(E + H).
The two readouts differ only in their noise model:
Masking noise uses Σ̃ = Σ₀ + Σ_H. It knows H is noise.
Hallucination uses Σ̃ = Σ₀. It treats H as signal.
Both act on the same Y, so the data (X, Y) are identical under either.

In an open system, both E and H are driven by latents such as movement, arousal, and input from other areas. "Noise" is just the latents you haven't named. Does distinguishing noise from the latent serve you? Inside the brain, no. The one distinction that does serve you is measurement noise: variability added by the recording, which the brain's readout never sees. That gives a two-line model:
R = WX + H. This is the representation; H is all internal non-stimulus activity, with covariance Σ₀ in control and Σ₀ + Σ_Δ in test.
Y = R + M. This is the recording, with measurement noise M of covariance Σ_M.
The readout acts on R, but the data see Y, so Cov(Y | X) = Σ₀ + Σ_Δ + Σ_M.

Σ_Δ is identified if the recording is stable. When Σ_M is the same in both sessions, it cancels in the test-minus-control difference.
The condition needs Σ₀ on its own. WᵀΣ₀⁻¹Σ_Δ ≠ 0 whitens by the internal baseline alone. Evaluating it requires separating Σ_M from Σ₀, for example by assuming measurement noise is private (diagonal) while internal activity is shared.

The earlier E and H are both pieces of W_Z Z. The image is special only because it is the input we ask the readout about.
What the factorization gives you. Everything about hallucination now lives in the geometry between col W₁ and col W_Z.
Proposition. Define 𝒜 = { a ∈ ℝ^{k₁} : W₁a ∈ col W_Z }.
(a) The images consistent with a response y, allowing any values of the other inputs, form exactly the affine set x₁ + 𝒜.
(b) The image is determined by the response if and only if col W₁ ∩ col W_Z = {0}.
Proof. W₁x₁ + W_Z z = W₁x₁′ + W_Z z′ holds exactly when W₁(x₁′ − x₁) = W_Z(z − z′), that is, when x₁′ − x₁ ∈ 𝒜. Since W₁ is injective, 𝒜 = {0} exactly when the two column spaces meet only at 0. ∎
This suggests two definitions of hallucination.
Structural hallucination. Other inputs drive activity along 𝒱 = col W₁ ∩ col W_Z. There, W_Z z = W₁x_h for some x_h ∈ 𝒜: the other inputs write a genuine image pattern into the image's code. The representation itself cannot tell it apart from seeing x_h, so any readout must resolve the ambiguity with priors. Even an optimal readout will sometimes resolve it toward x_h. This definition is intrinsic to the encoding matrices and involves no readout and no noise.
