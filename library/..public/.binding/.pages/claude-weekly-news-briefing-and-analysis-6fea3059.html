On the sharding mechanism, you're right that this is underappreciated. Ethereum's roadmap settled on danksharding — sharding the data layer for rollups while keeping execution on a single L1. TON took the opposite path: the Infinite Sharding Paradigm is bottom-up. Conceptually each account is treated as its own shardchain; those virtual account-chains aggregate into shardchains, which aggregate into workchains (up to 2³²), which all sync through a single masterchain. Shards split when load crosses a threshold and merge when it drops. Routing between shards uses a 15-dimensional hypercube where each shardchain has up to 240 neighbors — meaning you can support millions of shardchains with at most 15 hops and log₁₆(N) routing per message. This is a genuinely different scaling philosophy than anyone else in the L1 space. Ethereum L2s scale by adding external systems that post back to mainnet. TON scales by expanding the L1 itself under load, natively.
The honest caveat: Instant Hypercube Routing (the fast path with Merkle proofs that lets a message from shard A be processed in the next block of shard B regardless of distance) was still being brought online as of early 2025 per the TON GitHub issues. The slow path works today. The Catchain 2.0 upgrade (April 10, 2026) dropped block times to sub-400ms, which is the consensus-layer improvement that actually makes the fast routing matter at consumer latency.
