This is a fascinating thread you're pulling on. Let me lay out the history and then we can discuss the connections to TON.
The jrandom Story
jrandom was an anonymous developer who joined the Invisible IRC Project (IIP) in early 2003. By late summer 2003, he had taken control of the project, renamed it the Invisible Internet Project (I2P), and published a philosophy document outlining its goals. He explicitly set a goal of protection from organizations with "unlimited financial and political resources."
jrandom had strong opinions about the direction of the project. He was strongly committed to open source and free software. He maintained the project through rapid development cycles until late 2006 when he shifted focus to Syndie, a high-latency anonymous messaging system.
Then the mystery: In mid-November 2007, the I2P team received a cryptic message from jrandom that he would have to take a sabbatical from both Syndie and I2P development for a year or more. Unfortunately, they never heard from jrandom again. The project realized that even though it claimed to be totally decentralized, it actually depended on a number of centralized resources, above all, on jrandom.
Garlic Routing
"Garlic routing" was coined by Michael J. Freedman in Roger Dingledine's Free Haven Master's thesis Section 8.1.1 (June 2000), as derived from Onion Routing. Dingledine went on to co-found Tor.
Michael Freedman defined "garlic routing" as an extension to onion routing, in which multiple messages are bundled together. He called each message a "bulb". All the messages, each with its own delivery instructions, are exposed at the endpoint. This allows the efficient bundling of an onion routing "reply block" with the original message.
The key distinctions from Tor:
Garlic routing uses unidirectional tunnels, while mainstream alternatives like Tor and Mixmaster use bidirectional tunnels.
Multiple messages can be bundled in a single garlic "clove," making traffic analysis significantly harder
The TON Connection
TON's architecture shows deep familiarity with this lineage. TON Proxy is the network's anonymizer that will be used to hide the identity and create a decentralized virtual private network (VPN) to secure online privacy. The ultimate goal is "to guarantee to any service including Telegram immunity to censorship."
Looking at TON's recent payment network implementation, privacy is achieved through a "Garlic"-style encrypted task chain, where each task is individually encrypted with a shared key for the specific recipient. Only the intended node can decrypt and read its task. Dummy (fake) tasks are added to the chain to obscure the real routing structure.
How Would Nikolai Have Learned This?
The nLab page on Nikolai Durov is revealing about his trajectory: Nikolai Durov is an experienced computer programmer. He was a member of a St Petersburg State University student team winning a student world tournament in programming. He earned gold medals at the International Mathematical Olympiad in the 1990s and was world champion in competitive programming by the 2000s.
Aged 24, Nikolai Durov defended a dissertation in mathematics before heading off to the Max Planck Institute of Mathematics in Bonn, Germany.
The timing is interesting. Nikolai would have been active in competitive programming circles and cryptography research during the exact period (2003-2007) when jrandom was building I2P. The Russian competitive programming community was small and elite. Nikolai's mathematical focus on commutative algebraic monads as a generalization of local objects in a generalized algebraic geometry shows a mind oriented toward exactly the kind of abstract structural thinking that I2P's architecture required.
