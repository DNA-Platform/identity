The cleanest way to weigh this is to separate two questions that get conflated: which way is the answer likely to go, and how much do we actually know. The arguments for P ≠ NP are strong on the first and weak on the second, and that gap is the whole story.
The case for P ≠ NP
The empirical argument is the heavyweight. Thousands of NP-complete problems — SAT, TSP, clique, integer programming — are all polynomially interreducible, so a fast algorithm for any one collapses the entire class. Decades of effort, with enormous economic and intellectual incentive, have produced nothing. That's absence of evidence, but it's a very loud absence given how much pressure has been applied to so many equivalent targets.
Behind that sits the intuition that search is fundamentally harder than verification. Checking a satisfying assignment is trivial; finding one seems to require navigating an exponential space with no structure to exploit. Aaronson's framing captures the stakes: P = NP would mean that finding a proof is essentially as easy as checking one, i.e., that mathematical creativity is mechanizable across the board. That feels wrong to most people who've done creative work, and "feels wrong to experts" is a real Bayesian input even if it isn't a proof — Gasarch's polls put the believer fraction above 80%.
There's also genuine (if weak) mathematical traction pointing the right direction: we can prove lower bounds in restricted models. Razborov's exponential monotone-circuit bound for clique, parity's exclusion from AC⁰, the time hierarchy theorem giving P ≠ EXPTIME. None of these touches general circuits, but they're consistent with hardness rather than against it.
The skeptical case
Note that almost no serious person argues P = NP is likely. The skeptical position is about epistemics, and it's sharper than it first appears.
First, our intuitions about hardness have a track record of being wrong. Linear programming was "obviously" hard until the ellipsoid method; primality testing fell to AKS in 2002. Problems do collapse, sometimes suddenly, and we're bad at predicting which.
Second — the serious one — we have essentially no proof techniques that work, and we can prove this. The three barriers stack: relativization (Baker–Gill–Solovay), natural proofs (Razborov–Rudich), and algebrization (Aaronson–Wigderson) each rule out a broad class of arguments. The natural-proofs barrier is the most unsettling, because it shows that any proof which is "constructive and large" in the obvious combinatorial sense would itself break the pseudorandom generators whose existence we believe in. In other words: the very combinatorial intuitions that make us confident P ≠ NP are formally incapable of establishing it. The truth, whatever it is, lives outside the reach of the reasoning that makes us sure of it. Williams' NEXP ⊄ ACC⁰ (2011) was celebrated precisely because it slipped past the barriers — and it's still astronomically weaker than what a real proof needs.
A few often-cited pro arguments are also softer than advertised. "Cryptography works, so P ≠ NP" overstates things: crypto needs average-case hardness, which is strictly stronger than the worst-case P ≠ NP question. And industrial SAT solvers dispatch enormous instances daily, which is a reminder that worst-case complexity may be capturing something less practically fundamental than the framing suggests.
Where the evidence leans
Toward P ≠ NP — I'd bet there confidently — but it's worth being precise about what kind of confidence this is. The lean is carried almost entirely by inductive and empirical considerations (no algorithm despite massive search, the felt asymmetry of search vs. verification, expert priors) plus a little restricted-model traction. It is not carried by proof-theoretic progress, and the barriers show our standard intuitions are provably insufficient to close it. So the asymmetry is this: the arguments for the answer are inductive and decent; the arguments for our ignorance are meta-level and ironclad.
My honest summary: P ≠ NP is the right bet, probably by a wide margin, but the confidence rests on induction over a track record, not on mathematical understanding of why it's true. The skeptics are essentially correct about the epistemics even while being wrong about the likely answer — we are nowhere near a proof, and the one thing we've rigorously established is that the reasoning making us confident can't get us there.
