You're making a precise and important distinction that actually matters for how SRT positions itself relative to Chalmers.
Chalmers' actual formulation is categorical, not disciplinary. The "hard" in hard problem doesn't mean "science is bad at this" or "science hits a wall here." It means the problem belongs to a different class of problem than the easy problems — and you're right that calling them easy is almost ironic, since they're essentially the completion of the entire neuroscience program. The easy problems are hard in every practical sense. They're just in the same philosophical category as other functional explanations: given enough mechanism, we can in principle answer them.
The hard problem is asking something structurally different: not how a physical system produces behavior, but why there is anything it is like to be that system at all. That question remains after you've answered every functional question perfectly.
So "science cannot account for consciousness" is actually a derived interpretation — a gloss — not the formulation. It drifts the discussion toward methodological limitations of current science rather than what Chalmers was actually pointing at: a categorial gap between functional explanation and phenomenal explanation.
This matters for SRT because SRT's answer isn't "here's the extra ingredient science needs." SRT's answer is structural — the FPSP and TPOP are ontologically distinct, and the phenomenon in question (a perspective change via transduction) is by definition only accessible from within the FPSP. That's not a limitation of neuroscience, it's a feature of what a perspective is. SRT dissolves the hard problem rather than supplementing science to cross the gap Chalmers described.

That's a real and significant observation. The word "hard" gets absorbed into ordinary language where it just means difficult — as in "this is a hard math problem" — and the entire technical payload gets lost. People invoke the hard problem as a gesture toward mystery rather than as a precise claim about the limits of a class of explanation.
The irony is almost self-illustrating. Chalmers was extremely careful to define "hard" contrastively — hard relative to the easy problems, where "easy" means susceptible to functional/computational explanation in principle. The hard problem is hard precisely because it isn't in that category at all. It's a categorical distinction, not a difficulty gradient. But the moment people start using the phrase in casual discourse, the contrast collapses and "hard" just means "we haven't solved it yet."
Which means most people who invoke the hard problem are actually treating it as an easy problem — one that science will eventually crack with better tools or more data. They're using Chalmers' language while implicitly rejecting his core claim.
And this has a practical consequence: it means most proposed "solutions" to the hard problem aren't engaging with it either. They're solving something in the vicinity — explaining correlates, mechanisms, functional properties — and then declaring victory. Chalmers actually names this pattern explicitly in the paper, pointing out how papers begin with the mystery of experience and end with a functional theory that doesn't touch it.
So the conceptualization gap isn't incidental. It's almost load-bearing for how the field operates — the phrase provides the frisson of engaging with something profound while the actual claim gets quietly set aside.
