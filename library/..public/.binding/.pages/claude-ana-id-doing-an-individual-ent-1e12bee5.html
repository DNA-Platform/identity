დ = d ი = i ე = e ნ = n ე = e ი = i
So დიენეი reads "di-e-ne-i" → dee-eh-neh-ee.
That's not the word "DNA" — it's the English letters D-N-A spelled out by their English names, transcribed into Georgian:
D → "dee" → დი
N → "en" → ენ (here written ენე, catching the trailing vowel)
A → "ay" → ეი
So someone said the brand out loud as "Dee-En-Ay" and a clerk wrote down what they heard in Georgian script. დი-ენ-ეი. It's the pronunciation of the acronym, not a translation of the words.

Good thing to flag, because it's deliberate, not an accident.
Under Georgia's small-business (1%) regime, the law lists certain activities that are barred from the 1% rate. Consulting is the big one. The rule comes from the same Resolution N415 the document cites — its Annex N4 names the prohibited activities, and consulting/advisory services are on that list. If your declared activity is consulting, you don't get 1%; that income gets taxed at the standard rate instead (effectively the ~20% personal income bracket).
So the phrase "professional, scientific and technical activities (except consulting)" is doing a specific job: it's the standard activity classification ExpatHub selected for her minus the slice of it that would disqualify her from the 1% rate. The classification "professional, scientific and technical activities" is a broad bucket that ordinarily includes consulting — so they explicitly carved consulting out to keep her cleanly inside the eligible 1% zone. The application even has her affirming she doesn't perform any prohibited activity; the "except consulting" wording is what makes that affirmation true and consistent.
Why it matters in practice: the category is about how income is labeled, and the labeling determines the tax rate. For cartography, illustration, and design work, the eligible category fits naturally. The thing to be aware of going forward is that if some of Ana's actual work were ever billed or described as "consulting" or "advisory" — to a client, on an invoice, on Fiverr — that specific income could fall outside the 1% protection and be taxable at the higher rate. The registration is set up correctly; the discipline is just keeping the descriptions of the work aligned with the eligible category rather than drifting into "consulting" language.
I'd treat the exact boundary — what counts as consulting vs. eligible technical work in a borderline case — as a question for ExpatHub or an accountant rather than something to infer, since that line is where the 1% status actually lives or dies. But the short answer to your question: consulting is excluded because Georgian law excludes it from the 1% regime, and the wording protects her eligibility.

12:58 UTC, 3 June 2026
This is exactly the kind of question where guessing would be worse than useless, so let me check what the actual rule turns on rather than reason from intuition.
Searched the web for “Georgia small business status 1% consulting prohibited activity definition Resolution 415 what counts as consulting”
Direct answer: no, it's not the client's label that controls — it's the substance of the work, as the Revenue Service's audit department would characterize it after the fact. That's the crucial and slightly uncomfortable part.
Here's how it actually works, and there's a real gap between the two stages:
At registration, it's self-declared and barely checked. When you state your activity at the service centre, they rely on your words and issue the certificate without requesting service contracts or verifying what you actually do — say "IT service" and you get it; say "consultation service" and they tell you you don't qualify. So the label at registration is just what you assert.
At audit, substance is what governs. If a tax inspection is later initiated, inspectors from the audit department (a different department from the one that issued the certificate) analyze the work — and can conclude that your activity is, for example, "providing consultations regarding IT" rather than "IT service." That's the risk: the certificate doesn't protect you if the underlying work is, in substance, consulting. The audit looks through the label.
So to your question framed precisely: it is not the hiring company's choice of word, and it's not safely the word you put on registration either. Both of those can be overridden by what the tax authority decides the work was — judged from contracts, invoices, deliverables, and how the service was actually rendered.
What "consulting/advisory" means in this regime, from the law itself: the prohibited list in Resolution 415 names medical, architectural, legal or notarial, auditing, and consulting (including tax) activities, and the broader reading includes advisory services generally. The conceptual line that recurs across sources isdoing the work yourself and delivering a product/service (eligible) versus advising a client on what they should do (consulting, ineligible). For Ana, drawing a map or producing an illustration is delivering a work product — clearly on the eligible side. Telling a client how to design their own maps, or advising on their cartographic strategy, would drift toward advisory.
Two practical implications worth holding onto, though I'd put the fine line in front of ExpatHub or an accountant rather than treat my read as authoritative:
The protection lives in how the work is described and documented — invoices, Fiverr gig titles, contract language. Keeping those phrased as deliverables ("map illustration," "design," "artwork") rather than "consulting" or "advice" is the discipline that keeps her cleanly inside the 1% regime, because those documents are exactly what an auditor would read.
And there's a related trap one source raised that isn't your question but sits right next to it: "disguised employment" — a freelancer whose arrangement actually looks like employment can also be denied the 1% rate. Single long-term client, fixed monthly amount, working under their direction — that pattern can get recharacterized too. Not a concern if she's invoicing varied clients for discrete pieces of work, but worth knowing the audit lens looks at the real shape of the relationship, not the contract's title.
The boundary in any genuinely borderline case is a question for the accountant — that's where 1% status actually lives or dies, and it's not something I'd want you acting on from my read alone.
