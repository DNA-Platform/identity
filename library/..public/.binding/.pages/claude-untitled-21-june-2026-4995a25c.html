Both questions have real, substantial answers — and the striking thing is that several quite different research traditions have converged on the same structural move without always recognizing each other. Let me take them in turn and then draw the thread together, because the convergence is the most interesting part.
I. The semantics of the subjective/objective distinction
The question "what does it mean, semantically, for content to be subjective?" was first posed sharply by Frege, and his answer is a genuinely semantic criterion rather than a metaphysical one. He partitions content into three: the Bedeutung (reference), the Sinn (sense), and the Vorstellung (idea/presentation). The crucial contrast is between sense and idea. An idea is "wholly subjective"; the sense lies between the subjective idea and the object itself — no longer subjective, yet not the object. His operational test is ownership and shareability: senses and thoughts exist in a "third realm," not dependent on any one person's psychology — a thought has a truth-value whether or not anyone grasps it, and senses are interpersonal. An idea, by contrast, requires an owner and cannot be had by two minds. So for Frege the semantic mark of the subjective is non-shareability / owned-ness, and the mark of the objective is ownerless graspability plus truth-evaluability. His telescope analogy makes it precise: the retinal image (idea) is private and standpoint-bound; the optical image in the instrument (sense) is "one-sided and dependent upon the standpoint of observation, but still objective, inasmuch as it can be used by several observers." That last phrase already contains the whole later debate in embryo: objectivity as invariance across observers.
The modern formal machinery for this starts with Kaplan's character/content distinction (context-sensitivity built into meaning) and then takes a "perspectival turn" precisely over expressions that resist it. The driving phenomenon is faultless disagreement — Max Kölbel's term — and the canonical cases are predicates of personal taste. Lasersohn argues that the truth values of sentences with predicates like fun or tasty must be relativized to individuals: if you say roller coasters are fun and I deny it, I negate the very content you assert and directly contradict you, yet both utterances can be true relative to their separate contexts. His formal device is to introduce an individual ("judge") index alongside the world and time indices, with context supplying its value in the derivation of truth values from content rather than in the derivation of content from character. This is the semantics of subjectivity made explicit: a subjective predicate is one whose extension depends on a judge parameter. There's then a live, well-mapped dispute about where to put that parameter — Stephenson and others note that the relativist (judge as a parameter of the circumstance of evaluation) and the contextualist (judge as an implicit argument in the content) accounts are close to notational variants for taste, though they diverge on disagreement and retraction.
The most worked-out version is MacFarlane's assessment-sensitivity. His innovation is to relativize truth not just to a context of use but to a context of assessment — any situation from which an assertion might be assessed, the same kind of entity as a context of use. Formally, his theory replaces the ordinary monadic (assessment-insensitive) truth predicate with a dyadic, assessment-sensitive one, and a "postsemantics" then defines truth-at-a-context. The payoff is exactly your question: an expression is subjective, in MacFarlane's sense, when its truth is assessment-sensitive, and what makes this relativism rather than mere indexicality is, in his words, not the kind of parameter one relativizes propositional truth to, but what one does with it. This is, to date, the sharpest semantic regimentation of "subjective."
On the objectivity side, the cleanest formal proposal is Nozick's last book, Invariances. Its central chapter offers a theory of the objective/subjective contrast in terms of invariance under transformations: what makes a fact objective is that it is invariant under certain transformations. He identifies three weaker marks — accessibility from different angles, possibility of intersubjective agreement, independence of belief/desire — and argues a fourth is more fundamental: invariance under transformations, with spacetime as the paradigm (the Lorentz-invariant interval is objective precisely where the purely temporal or purely spatial separations are not). This deliberately mirrors the physicist's notion — objectivity as what survives change of coordinates, symmetry, conserved quantities — and it pairs naturally with Bernard Williams's "absolute conception" and Nagel's "view from nowhere" as the limiting ideal of perspective-independence. Note how exactly this dovetails with Frege's telescope: objective = invariant under change of standpoint.
II. Formal systems for subjective expression itself
Your second question — an ontology designed to capture what can be expressed subjectively, the structure of subjective expression — also has several serious candidates.
The canonical one is Lewis's centered-worlds / de se framework. Lewis argued that the content of a thought you'd express with "I am hungry" is a set of centered worlds, where a centered world is a possible world centered on an individual at a time; to believe it is to locate oneself in the set of centered worlds whose center is hungry. Belief becomes self-location, and the self enters as an irreducible coordinate of content — this is an ontology of subjective content. Its motivation, Perry's "essential indexical," is that de se content can be characterized as sets of centered worlds, or as self-ascribed properties, and it extends naturally: a growing number of philosophers and linguists hold that thoughts about subjective matters like taste also have centered content — to believe licorice is tasty is to locate oneself among the centers to which licorice tastes good. For communication of such content there's the multicentered worlds refinement (Ninan, Torre): content as sets of multicentered worlds — worlds centered on a sequence of individuals — developed in a Stalnakerian model of communication with a matching semantics for personal pronouns and taste predicates. (The dissenting view worth knowing is Cappelen & Dever's The Inessential Indexical, which argues the whole de se edifice is "just another instance of Frege's puzzle" and that perspective does no special semantic work.)
There are also logics built directly on the first person. Prior wrote a system literally called "Egocentric Logic," taking his cue from Castañeda's work on indicators and quasi-indicators — Castañeda's "he*" / irreducible "I" being the formal apparatus for first-person reference that can't be reduced to names or descriptions. Erich Rast and others have built explicit indexical predicate logics (FOLC) that reify contexts of utterance into the object language with designated parameters for the current context, treating indexicals as fundamentally egocentric. Two-dimensional semantics (Chalmers, Stalnaker) belongs here too: the primary/epistemic intension is essentially the centered, subjective dimension of content, the secondary intension the objective one.
A genuinely different semantic theory of subjective discourse is expressivism (Blackburn, Gibbard). Rather than treating "X is good/fun" as describing a judge-relative fact, it holds that such sentences express non-cognitive states — attitudes, or in Gibbard's norm-expressivism, acceptance of norms/plans — and the whole project is then to give a compositional logic for those states (the Frege–Geach problem). That is, quite directly, an account of "the structure of what can be expressed subjectively," built on the logic of attitudes rather than of facts.
From phenomenology, Husserl's noema/noesis is an explicit structural ontology of subjective experience. Noesis and noema are the subjective and objective poles of any intentional act; by examining the structures of the noesis we learn how objects must be constituted to be objects of experience at all — the subjective and objective are co-constituted in every act of consciousness. The Smith–McIntyre tradition reads the noema as a generalization of Fregean sense to all intentional acts, and there are real formalizations: Krysztofiak's "Noemata and their formalization" (Synthese 1995), and Marbach's "Towards a formalism for expressing structures of consciousness." These are the most direct attempts at exactly the ontology you describe — a formal system for the structure of subjective intentional content as such.
Two more, on opposite ends. On the computational end, Jøsang's subjective logic is a fully worked calculus in which a subjective opinion carries an explicit owner (the "subject") and applies to a target frame, the opinion itself being a composite of belief, disbelief, uncertainty mass, and a base rate. The caveat is that its "subjectivity" is epistemic (uncertainty, trust, ownership of a probability judgment), not phenomenal — but notice it makes theowner of the opinion a first-class formal object, which is Frege's ownership criterion turned into engineering. On the phenomenal end, quality-space theory (Austen Clark, David Rosenthal) is the attempt to formalize the harder, qualitative subjectivity — sensory qualities represented structurally as positions in a relational quality space, defined by similarity/discrimination relations rather than by any intrinsic felt character.
And then the one I suspect will interest you most given the category-theoretic work: sheaf-theoretic contextuality (Abramsky & Brandenburger). Using sheaf theory, they show that contextuality — and non-locality as a special case — corresponds exactly to obstructions to the existence of global sections. The dictionary writes itself for your question: local charts/sections are each locally meaningful and may restrict compatibly on overlaps (gluing into one coherent global section), or they may fail to agree on shared overlaps so that no coherent gluing exists. Concretely, the gluing condition is pairwise agreement on overlaps; when local sections fail to glue, the failure is a Čech 1-cocycle whose cohomology class is the obstruction — non-vanishing means no global section is compatible with the local data. Read perspectivally: each subjective viewpoint is a local section; an objective fact is a global section that glues all viewpoints consistently; and irreducible subjectivity/contextuality is precisely a cohomological obstruction to that gluing — something locally expressible from each standpoint that provably cannot be assembled into a single standpoint-independent description. There's even recent work (2026) porting this transport/obstruction machinery to AI agents and scientific theory-shift, which suggests the framework is being taken seriously as a general model of perspective, not just a quantum curiosity.
III. The thread, and the honest gap
Step back and one schema runs through almost all of it. To model subjectivity, you add a coordinate to the semantic apparatus — Frege's owner, Lasersohn's judge index, MacFarlane's assessment context, Lewis's center/self, Jøsang's opinion-subject, Abramsky's measurement context. Objectivity is then characterized as independence from, invariance under, or glueability across that coordinate — Nozick's invariance under transformation, Frege's "usable by several observers," the existence of a global section. Subjective = section over a base of perspectives; objective = the section that is constant, or that glues into a single global one. That is a real unification, and it's the kind of result your reference-theoretic work is positioned to say something about, because it casts the subjective/objective distinction as fundamentally about what a structure looks like under transport between reference frames.
The honest limitation: nearly everything above formalizes indexical, perspectival, or evaluative subjectivity — self-location, taste, viewpoint, opinion-ownership. The qualitative subjectivity — the felt structure of experience, what is expressible only from inside a phenomenal point of view, the thing Nagel's bat is about — is where the formal project genuinely thins out. Quality-space theory and the Husserlian formalizations gesture at it but are partial and contested, and there's a respectable view (the explanatory-gap intuition) that it's not formalizable in the same way at all. So if your underlying interest is "a formal system for whatever can be expressed subjectively," the field gives you powerful, mature tools for the perspectival component and a sparse, frontier landscape for the phenomenal one — and the sheaf/obstruction picture is, to my mind, the most promising bridge between them, because "expressible locally but not globally" is at least a precise formal shape that qualitative subjectivity might turn out to have.
