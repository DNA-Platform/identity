First, the framing fix, because it changes the whole picture: this isn't a wallet-to-wallet compatibility problem. The two wallets can talk to each other on-chain perfectly well. What blocked her is acompliance gate inside one of them.
The message came from Telegram's @wallet, which is a custodial service — not a DeFi wallet. That distinction is the heart of it. A DeFi/self-custody wallet (Tonkeeper, etc.) is just keys; it sends wherever you point it, no permission needed. A custodial wallet holds your funds for you, which means a company is legally on the hook for where those funds go — so it screens every withdrawal against sanctions databases before releasing money. <cite index="31-1">Screening tools like Elliptic and Chainalysis run in real time at deposits, withdrawals, and ongoing monitoring — catching not just direct list matches but exposure by association.</cite> @wallet has had to lean hard into this; it already <cite index="35-1">suspended services in the UK to restructure and register as a crypto provider under FCA anti-money-laundering rules.</cite>
So when she tried to withdraw to UQCJ...cEFz, @wallet screened that destination and its analytics flagged it as linked to a sanctioned entity — and "linked" is doing the work. It almost never means her wallet is on the OFAC list. It means the destination address's transaction history connects, within a few hops, to something that is. <cite index="33-1">A wallet can constitute or contain blocked property regardless of whether it's individually identified on the SDN list.</cite>
Why would her own DeFi wallet pick up that taint? The most likely answer, given she's a Russian user, is the funding trail. If that DeFi wallet was ever funded from — or received funds tracing back to — a Russian exchange or P2P service, the screening inherits that exposure. This is a documented, widespread pattern: <cite index="32-1">many Russia-linked exchanges still route significant volume to OFAC-sanctioned targets — one service sends roughly 5% of outgoing funds straight to the sanctioned exchange Garantex.</cite> Funds that pass anywhere near that get a risk score, and the receiving address carries it forward. It can also simply be a false positive from over-aggressive address clustering — that happens too.
The "do not attempt again — all future attempts will be rejected" line means @wallet has now blacklisted that specific destination address in its system. Re-sending won't get a different result.
What this actually means for her, honestly:
The narrow issue is one flagged address. The broader issue is structural and worth her knowing: as a Russian user moving crypto through compliant services, she's likely to keep hitting this wall, and the ground is tightening fast. <cite index="34-1">From May 24, 2026, transactions with Russian-registered crypto providers became illegal for anyone under EU jurisdiction, and Russia is simultaneously moving to ban personal wallets and force custody into depositories.</cite> Even Telegram itself is caught in this: <cite index="36-1">$500M of its financing was frozen in Russia's sanctioned settlement depository.</cite> So this isn't @wallet being arbitrary — it's the whole compliant rail narrowing around Russian-sourced funds.
Legitimate things she can do, rather than fight the screen:
Check whether it's a false positive — she can paste her DeFi wallet address into a free risk-screening lookup, or contact @wallet support and ask them to review the flag. If the taint is real (the wallet was funded through a flagged Russian route), no compliant custodial service will release funds to it, and that's not something to try to route around — defeating sanctions screening is exactly the conduct these rules exist to stop, and for a Russian national the legal exposure is not trivial.
The cleaner path for buying on Fragment is usually to avoid the custodial hop entirely: fund a Tonkeeper (self-custody) wallet with TON from a source that doesn't carry the taint, and pay Fragment directly from there. But "from a clean source" is the whole catch — if the underlying funds themselves trace back to a sanctioned rail, every compliant off-ramp will keep catching them, no matter which wallet they pass through.
I'd want to be careful not to overstate any of this as legal advice — I'm not a lawyer, and her situation as a Russian citizen sits right on a line that's moving month to month. If real money is tied up here, this is genuinely worth a consult with someone who does crypto-sanctions compliance, not just a workaround.

Yes — and you've just put your finger on the actual difference between the two kinds.
A free username from Telegram settings is attached, but it's a revocable label, not a possession. Telegram lets you use it; Telegram can also take it back (inactivity, policy, trademark complaint), and you can't sell it. You can detach it from one account and stick it on another of your own, but you can't give or sell it to another person. It isn't an asset — it's permission to use a handle.
A Fragment collectible username is the opposite: it's an NFT sitting in the TON wallet. Ownership lives in the wallet, not in any account. So the model is two separate things —
Ownership = the NFT in the wallet (permanent, yours, Telegram can't reclaim it). Usage = you assign that owned handle to a Telegram account to actually display it as @whatever.
Because ownership is the wallet, transferring to someone else is a wallet operation, and yes, Fragment is where you do it. You unassign it from the account, then on Fragment send the username NFT to the buyer's TON wallet (or list it for sale and the sale transfers it). The handle detaches from your account and reattaches to theirs once they assign it on their end. That's the whole reason these things have resale value — the free kind can't change hands, the owned kind can.
So to directly answer: the free one is attached-but-not-givable; the Fragment one is owned-in-a-wallet and fully transferable, and Fragment (or any TON NFT marketplace) is the venue for handing it to someone else.
