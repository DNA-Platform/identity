That's a ladybird (ladybug) pupa — so your "larval maybe" instinct is in the right family, just one stage further along. It's not the larva and not the adult; it's the transitional stage in between.
Here's what gives it away: ladybird larvae, when they're ready to transform, glue their rear end to a leaf surface and hunch up into that domed, immobile capsule. The dark fringy bit at the top of the creature is the shed, crumpled larval skin it's still anchored by. The orange body with irregular black blotches is classic pupal coloring.
Species-wise, the orange-with-black-spots pattern points most likely to Harmonia axyridis — the harlequin or Asian lady beetle — which is extremely common and a voracious aphid eater. I can't confirm the exact species from one photo with certainty, but the genus/family read (ladybird pupa) is solid.
The good news for your apple tree: this is a beneficial insect. Both the larvae and adults eat aphids and other soft-bodied pests, so having them pupating on your leaves is a sign the tree's ecosystem is doing its own pest control. Leave it be and in a few days an adult beetle will emerge.

What is a colorado bug? Similar?
Claude

No, quite different — and the confusion is worth clearing up because they're ecological opposites.
The Colorado bug almost certainly means the Colorado potato beetle (Leptinotarsa decemlineata), called колорадский жук in Russian — which is probably where you've heard the name, since it's one of the most notorious garden pests across the former Soviet space and Europe.
Here's the contrast that matters:
The ladybird in your photo is a predator and a friend. It eats aphids. You want it around.
The Colorado potato beetle is a pest and an enemy. The adult is rounded like a ladybird and similar in size, but it's yellow-tan with ten distinct black lengthwise stripes running down its wing cases — stripes, not the random spots a ladybird has. Its larvae are fat, humpbacked, brick-red or orange grubs with rows of black dots along the sides, and they devour potato, tomato, eggplant, and pepper foliage, stripping plants bare.
So the two can look superficially alike at a glance — small, domed, orange-and-black — but the rule of thumb is: spots = usually a ladybird (good); lengthwise stripes = Colorado beetle (bad). And the Colorado beetle goes after nightshades, not apple trees, so it isn't what's on your leaf.
