Origin: BitMEX, 2016
Arthur Hayes invented the perpetual swap at BitMEX — a crypto-native instrument with no expiry, kept anchored to spot via an 8-hour funding rate paid between longs and shorts. Before this, crypto traders had to roll dated futures. The perp solved the rollover friction problem but introduced the funding rate as an ongoing cost — which is actually the mechanism doing the heavy lifting in price discovery.
Does isolated margin actually solve unbounded loss on shorts?
Partially — and the distinction matters.
It solves the definitional unbounded loss problem: your max loss is capped at the margin allocated to the position. If you're short 10x with $100 isolated, you lose at most $100 regardless of how far price rips. Traditional short selling has no such cap — you can owe more than you put in.
But three residual risks remain:
Gap risk. If price gaps through your liquidation level faster than the engine can close, you go into negative equity. The exchange insurance fund absorbs this — until it doesn't, triggering socialized loss via ADL (auto-deleveraging), which forcibly closes profitable counterparty positions.
Funding rate erosion. During sustained bull momentum, shorts pay longs. Margin bleeds even if liquidation never triggers. This is a slow-kill mechanism invisible to the unbounded-loss framing.
100% margin loss vs. proportional loss. Isolation converts unbounded loss into total margin loss. That's bounded, but it's still -100% on allocated capital, which for retail is psychologically and practically equivalent to ruin.
So: isolated margin bounds the loss in absolute terms but doesn't reduce the probability of total wipeout — it just defines the ceiling precisely.

You're right on the bounded loss question — and it's precise.
Isolated margin bounds what you put in. But gap risk creates negative equity, meaning the position owes more than the margin. That's unbounded in structure.
What actually closes the loop is the insurance fund. The exchange absorbs negative equity so it never reaches you. That's the real mechanism solving unbounded loss — not isolated margin itself. Isolated margin defines the exposure unit; the insurance fund is what makes the user's loss genuinely capped at zero.
So for TON/Crypto Wallet: yes, users are protected from negative equity by the insurance fund. You can't be called for more than you deposited. If the fund runs dry, ADL kicks in — but that's an exchange-level failure, not a user liability.
Your actual point — solving the traditional securities problem
In equities, short selling means borrowing shares. Price goes parabolic, your broker issues a margin call, and in extremis you owe more than your account. There is no structural cap.
Perps with isolated margin + insurance fund genuinely solve this. You define your risk upfront, the mechanism enforces it, and the exchange eats the gap. That's a real structural improvement over traditional short mechanics.

Who funds the insurance fund:
Liquidation surplus. When the engine closes a position at liquidation price before it reaches zero (which happens in orderly markets), the residual margin goes into the fund. It's essentially a skim off every liquidation that didn't go all the way negative. In volatile bull markets the fund grows large. In cascades it drains fast.
The failure mode you're describing: socialized losses / clawback
When the insurance fund is exhausted, the exchange can't absorb negative equity anymore. So it reaches into profitable counterparty positions and force-closes them — ADL. You're long, making money, and your position gets involuntarily closed to cover someone else's deficit.
BitMEX called this clawback explicitly before ADL became the industry standard term. The tail risk doesn't disappear — it mutualizes across the network of profitable traders.
The catastrophic version is FTX — their insurance fund was essentially Alameda. When Alameda collapsed, the entire structure unwound simultaneously. The "provider of the position" was the exchange, and the exchange was insolvent.
The deep structural problem:
You've identified exactly what perps actually do: they don't eliminate unbounded loss, they re-route it up the stack — from individual → insurance fund → network participants → exchange solvency. Each layer holds until it doesn't.
Traditional CCPs in TradFi have the same architecture, just with regulatory capital requirements backstopping each layer. Crypto perps replicated the structure without the regulated capital base underneath it.
