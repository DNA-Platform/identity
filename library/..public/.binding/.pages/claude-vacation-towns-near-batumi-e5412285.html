The license — yes, you're fine. A valid US license works for driving in Georgia (the country) because it's in Latin script. To drive a rental car in Georgia, your regular driver's license is enough as long as it has Latin characters — you don't need an international driving permit. It's recognized for one year from the date of your last entry into the country, after which you'd need to exchange it or get a Georgian one. Two practical notes: carry your passport (police can ask for it to verify the license), and confirm directly with your rental company — some apply their own stricter rules (minimum age, deposit, occasionally asking for an IDP regardless of the law).
Differences from US driving:
Structural (easy to adjust to):
Same side — they drive on the right, left-hand-drive cars, like the US.
Metric: speed in km/h, distances in km. Cameras enforce in km/h.
More roundabouts than you're used to; yield to traffic already in them.
No right turn on red — unlike most US states, a red light means stop, full stop, unless there's a green arrow.
Rental cars are often manual — confirm automatic when you book if you want one.
Behavioral (the real adjustment):
Driving is noticeably more assertive than the US. Expect frequent lane changes, tailgating, and overtaking on blind curves — minibuses (marshrutkas) especially. Drive defensively and don't mirror it.
Turn signals are used inconsistently — people merge and turn without indicating, so don't rely on others signaling.
The horn is communication, not aggression — a quick beep often means "I'm passing" or "heads up," not anger.
Pedestrians: drivers frequently don't stop at crossings, and jaywalking is common, so watch closely.
Speed cameras are increasingly common near towns and on highways, and they're accurate.
Roads:
Major highways like Tbilisi–Batumi are in good condition, well-marked, with signage in Georgian and English. In the highlands, roads can be unpaved, narrow, steep, or seasonally closed, and a 4×4 becomes closer to a necessity.
On rural roads, watch for livestock — cows on the road are a genuine, regular thing.
One more worth knowing: Georgia has very low tolerance for drinking and driving, so treat it as effectively zero. For navigation, Google Maps and Yandex both work well locally. If you're staying on the Tbilisi–Batumi corridor and the coast, it's all good highway and very manageable.

Got it — you're asking how the road signs and signals themselves differ from what you know in the US, not the route. Let me lay that out.
The short version: Georgia uses the European (Vienna Convention) sign system, which is different in look from US signs, though the logic is learnable in a day.
The specific things you asked:
Red and green lights — same meaning. Red = stop, green = go, yellow/amber = prepare to stop. No surprise there. The one real difference is no right turn on red (which I mentioned earlier) — a red light means stop and wait, unlike most US states.
Stop signs — yes, octagonal and red, same shape you know. Georgia uses the international standard, so the octagon is universal. It often reads "STOP" in Latin letters (sometimes alongside Georgian). So that instinct is safe.
Where the bigger differences are:
Most signs are symbol-based, not word-based. The US uses a lot of text signs (SPEED LIMIT 55, YIELD, WRONG WAY, DO NOT ENTER). Georgia/Europe uses pictograms inside shapes, with few or no words. You read the shape and symbol, not text.
Sign shapes carry meaning:
Red-bordered circles = prohibitions/limits (e.g., a number in a red circle is the speed limit; a red circle with nothing = no entry for vehicles).
Red-bordered triangles = warnings (curve ahead, pedestrian crossing, livestock — point-up triangle, unlike the US diamond-shaped yellow warning signs).
Blue circles = mandatory (must go this direction).
Blue rectangles = information (parking, services).
Speed limits are a number in a red circle, in km/h, not a black-and-white "SPEED LIMIT" rectangle in mph.
Yield is a downward-pointing white triangle with red border — no "YIELD" text.
No entry / one-way uses the red circle with a white horizontal bar (the classic European "no entry"), versus the US "DO NOT ENTER" / "WRONG WAY" text signs.
Priority roads — Europe has a yellow diamond sign meaning "you have priority" and signs telling you when you lose it. There's no direct US equivalent, so that one's genuinely new.
Two behavioral ones that go with the signs: roundabouts are common (yield to traffic already in the circle), and place-name/direction signs on main routes are usually in both Georgian and Latin script, but rural ones can be Georgian-only.
So: stop signs and traffic lights — trust your US instincts. Speed limits, warnings, and prohibitions — retrain your eye to read European shapes and symbols instead of text. Want me to pull up the actual Georgian sign chart with images so you can eyeball them before you drive?
