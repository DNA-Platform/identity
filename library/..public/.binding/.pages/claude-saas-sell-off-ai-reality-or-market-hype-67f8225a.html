What's Happening
On February 3 and 4, 2026, global markets saw a $285B software sell-off that traders are calling the "SaaSpocalypse." The sell-off accelerated Tuesday after Anthropic released a new automation tool for legal work, sending shares of legal software and publishing firms tumbling—Thomson Reuters plunged 16%, RELX fell 13%, and LegalZoom plummeted 20%.
The selloff has pushed more than $17.7 billion of US tech company loans into distressed territory over the past four weeks, expanding the total tech distressed debt pile to approximately $46.9 billion.
Reality vs. Hype: Both, But...
Here's the nuanced answer—it's actually both, but in different proportions:
The AI Reality component:
The fundamental concern is real: if an AI agent can handle the workload of five people, a business only needs to buy one license instead of five. This is a direct threat to the "per-seat" pricing model that almost every software company uses.
AI isn't eating the product—it's eating the budget. When AI can do the work of multiple humans, you need fewer humans, and when you need fewer humans, you need fewer seats.
The Hype/Panic component:
Public SaaS growth rates have declined every single quarter since the 2021 peak. The AI crash narrative just gave the market permission to finally re-rate what the numbers have been screaming for three years.
Stephens Research argues that fears of AI disintermediating SaaS are overstated—incumbent platforms benefit from proprietary data, core workflows, and long operating histories that large language models don't replicate.
There's a disconnect between what's being feared and ground reality: "Agentic AI adoption is going really slowly," and SaaS companies are embracing it, but enterprise customers remain extremely risk-averse to making wholesale changes.
The honest take: "The draconian view is that software will be the next print media or department stores. That the pendulum has swung so far to the sell-everything side suggests there will be super-attractive opportunities that come out of this."
The sell-off reflects genuine long-term structural concerns, but the timing and intensity are driven by narrative contagion and delayed repricing of a sector that was already decelerating. The market is pricing in a 5-10 year disruption scenario today, which is classic hype behavior—even if the underlying thesis has merit.

This is the right question. And the answer is: no, AI is not scaling as expected, and we're already in the early stages of that realization.
Here's what the data shows:
The scaling wall is real and documented: It is a well-kept secret in the AI industry: for over a year now, frontier models appear to have reached their ceiling. The scaling laws that powered the exponential progress of LLMs have started to show diminishing returns. Inside labs, the consensus is growing that simply adding more data and compute will not create the "all-knowing digital gods" once promised.
GPT-5 was the canary in the coal mine: The CEO had previously announced that GPT-5 would be a significant step towards AGI. However, the model's launch was met with widespread disappointment. Users shared instances of the new model making basic errors, such as incorrectly labeling a map of the United States.
It feels unclear that GPT-5 represented any genuine strides towards human-level intelligence—it can't even correctly count the number of r's in the word "raspberry." Sam Altman himself told reporters he believes we're in a "phase where investors as a whole are overexcited about AI."
The math is brutal: According to Cal Newport, AI companies have spent $560 billion in the last 18 months, while earning only $35 billion in revenue. The math simply doesn't add up.
A recent MIT report on AI in business found that 95 percent of all generative-AI deployments in business settings generated "zero return."
The historical parallel: A professor of computer science at the University of California, Berkeley, warned that the current situation resembles the bubble burst of the 1980s, when technological innovations failed to deliver on promises and could not provide investment returns. "The bubble burst, the system didn't make money, and we couldn't find enough high-value applications."
So to your point: The SaaS selloff you're asking about is part of the AI correction, but it's oddly inverted. The market is simultaneously:
Punishing SaaS companies for not being disrupted by AI fast enough
While the AI companies themselves can't make the economics work
This is the 3D printer dynamic exactly. The capability to disrupt exists in imagination, the money is being spent on that basis, but the actual scaling/adoption/returns curve isn't following the projections. Burry's instinct seems sound—the question is timing. We may be watching the correction unfold right now, just messily.

18:40 UTC, 4 February 2026
You're touching on something most coverage completely misses. Let me take each one:
Context ≈ "Intelligence" conflation
This is huge. A model that can hold 128k tokens feels smarter because it remembers your whole conversation, can reference that document you uploaded, doesn't lose the thread. But that's memory, not reasoning. It's the difference between someone who can hold a lot in working memory vs. someone who can actually think through novel problems.
The "reasoning" models (o1, o3) tried to address this by spending more compute at inference time—letting the model "think longer." But that's still not fundamental intelligence gains, it's just... thinking longer. And it's expensive.
Researchers absolutely know this distinction. Whether they communicate it clearly up the chain is another matter. The incentive structure rewards conflating "better benchmark scores" with "closer to AGI."
The n² problem
This is the brutal physics of transformers. Self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length. Double your context window, quadruple your compute costs. There are workarounds—sparse attention, sliding windows, linear attention approximations—but they all trade something off.
So when a company says "we went from 8k to 128k context!" the cost implications are enormous. And the returns are... you can paste in a longer document. That's useful! But it's not intelligence scaling, it's memory scaling with punishing economics.
Investor comprehension
This is where it all falls apart. Most investors understand:
Bigger model = smarter (sort of true, with diminishing returns)
More data = smarter (true until you saturate)
Therefore: spend more = AGI eventually
What they don't understand:
The scaling laws show logarithmic returns—you need exponential compute increases for linear capability gains
Some problems may be computationally irreducible regardless of scale
"Pattern matching with vast context" and "genuine understanding" might be categorically different things
The benchmarks being crushed don't measure what matters
Your observation about GPT-5 being "templated" and "rude in a weird way" is actually diagnostic. It suggests heavy RLHF optimization for metrics that don't correlate with what users actually want. Goodhart's Law in action—when you optimize for the measure, it ceases to be a good measure.
The uncomfortable synthesis:
The people who understand compute don't understand cognition. The people who understand cognition don't understand markets. The people who understand markets don't understand compute. And the researchers who understand all three have careers dependent on not saying the quiet part loud.
So yeah—Burry might be right for exactly the reasons you're identifying. The correction might not be "AI doesn't work" but rather "AI works, but the scaling story that justifies the valuations is thermodynamically and cognitively bounded in ways investors haven't priced in."
