This is a question where the honest answer starts with a disambiguation, because half the difficulty is that "subjective" names several different things, and the literatures that take the semantics seriously are largely the project of prising them apart. Let me lay out who has addressed the meaning of the distinction (Part 1), then who has tried to build an ontology of subjective content itself (Part 2), and then where the two projects strain against each other — which is the genuinely hard and unresolved part.
Part 1 — The semantics of "subjective" vs. "objective"
The cleanest meta-level analysis is John Searle's, who argues the distinction is systematically ambiguous between an epistemic and an ontological sense. A claim is epistemically objective if its truth can be settled independently of anybody's attitudes or feelings, and epistemically subjective if it cannot — "Van Gogh died in France" versus "Van Gogh was a better painter than Gauguin." Cutting across that, an entity is ontologically subjective if its existence depends on being experienced by a subject (a pain), and ontologically objective if it does not (a mountain). His sharpest semantic point is that these come apart: you can make an epistemically objective claim about an ontologically subjective thing, which is why a science of pain is possible — and, he argues, relativism's basic error is conflating the ontological subjectivity of statement-making with the epistemic subjectivity of what gets stated. This is the place to start because it's explicitly about what the words mean rather than about consciousness.
Underneath that sits the Fregean foundation, which is where the semantics of subjectivity first gets pinned to a theory of content. In "Der Gedanke" (The Thought), Frege separates the objective realm of thoughts/senses — shareable, truth-apt, belonging to no one's mind, his "third realm" — from the subjective Vorstellung, the private idea or image that is owned by a single consciousness and cannot be handed to another. The whole architecture of sense and reference is, among other things, a claim about which features of meaning are objective (the thought a sentence expresses) and which are merely subjective (the accompanying mental imagery). Frege also flagged the residual puzzle that the first-person "I" seems to involve a special, primitive mode of presentation each person has only of themselves — the wrinkle that the entire later de se literature inherits.
A third strand treats objectivity as a gradable property of representations, which is squarely semantic/representational rather than metaphysical. This is Thomas Nagel ("The View from Nowhere") and Bernard Williams ("the absolute conception"). For Nagel, we move toward objectivity by a stepwise abstraction away from any particular point of view — replacing indexicals like "I," "here," and "now" with neutral descriptions, correcting for the contingencies of one's standpoint, and adopting theories that detach the world-picture from immediate appearance. The fully detached "view from nowhere" is a regulative limit he thinks no subject can literally occupy. Williams's twist is the criterion of self-explanation: the absolute conception represents the world independently of any perspective while non-vacuously explaining how the various perspectival views of it are possible. On this picture, a representation is more objective the more it brackets perspective and the more it can account for its own perspectival appearances — a semantic property of conceptions, gradable rather than binary.
Then there is the strand that most literally answers "what does it mean for a statement to be subjective": the contemporary truth-conditional semantics of predicates of personal taste (PPT) — "tasty," "fun," "disgusting." The organizing puzzle is faultless disagreement: if I say the crème caramel is delicious and you deny it, we seem to genuinely disagree, yet neither of us is at fault. Three families of semantic theory carve this up:
The contextualist line treats taste predicates as carrying a hidden judge argument supplied by context, so "tasty" means roughly "tasty-for-x." The relativist line — Peter Lasersohn's 2005 "Context Dependence, Disagreement, and Predicates of Personal Taste," Max Kölbel on faultless disagreement, and John MacFarlane's "Assessment Sensitivity: Relative Truth and Its Applications" (2014) — adds a judge or assessor parameter to the circumstances of evaluation, so the very same proposition can be true relative to one assessor and false relative to another. Lewis's earlier "Index, Context, and Content" supplies the formal machinery here, and MacFarlane's assessment-sensitivity is the most developed version: truth is relativized not just to a context of use but to a context of assessment. The expressivist line says the semantic job of "this is tasty" is to express a gustatory attitude rather than to describe a fact; there are now hybrid "lightweight expressivist" treatments that bolt an expressive component onto a relativist recursive semantics, partly to handle the acquaintance requirement (sincerely calling something tasty normally requires having tasted it). Worth flagging, because Doug values the deflationary moves: Stojanovic, Kneer, and others argue that contextualism and relativism are, from a purely truth-conditional standpoint, close to notational variants — they assign the same truth values across parameter settings, so the dispute becomes about disagreement, retraction, and pragmatics rather than about compositional semantics proper.
This connects directly to metaethics, where the same three options appear as cognitivism vs. relativism vs.non-cognitivism/expressivism (Allan Gibbard, Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism). And here the semantics-of-the-subjective hits its hardest, most famous problem: the Frege–Geach (embedding) problem. If "lying is wrong" merely expresses disapproval rather than stating a fact, what does the antecedent contribute in "if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong," where nothing is being asserted or disapproved? Expressivism owes a compositional semantics for how subjective/expressive contents embed under negation, conditionals, and propositional attitudes — and the bulk of Gibbard's and Blackburn's technical work exists to pay that debt. This is the single best place to see that giving subjective discourse a semantics (not just a metaphysics) is genuinely difficult.
Finally, two formal handles worth naming. David Kaplan's "Demonstratives" gives the character/content distinction — character is a function from context to content — which is the formal backbone under "subjective" in its indexical/perspectival sense and the bridge to Part 2. And Crispin Wright's "Truth and Objectivity" (1992) offers something rare: a pluralist framework for grading how objective a whole discourse is, via criteria like cognitive command (whether disagreement must involve some cognitive shortcoming), width of cosmological role, and superassertibility. Alongside the response-dependence literature (Mark Johnston, Wright, Philip Pettit, and McDowell's "sensibility theory"), which gives a crisp semantic criterion — a concept is subjective/response-dependent if its extension is fixed by reference to subjects' responses under specified conditions — this is the closest thing to a general semantics of what makes a region of discourse count as objective.
Part 2 — An ontology of subjective content itself
Here the field fragments by kind of subjective content, and each kind has its own formalism. There is no single accepted "ontology of the subjective"; there are several, each strong for one slice.
Self-locating / first-personal (de se) content. This is the most successful formal framework for an irreducibly subjective slice. Building on Quine's "centered worlds" and John Perry's "The Problem of the Essential Indexical" (1979), David Lewis ("Attitudes De Dicto and De Se," 1979) proposed that the objects of belief and desire are not propositions (sets of worlds) but properties — ways an individual might be — and that to believe is to self-ascribe a property. Formally this generalizes the possible-worlds model: contents become sets of centered worlds, each a possible world plus a designated person-and-time, where the world represents an objective situation compatible with the subject's view and the center represents who and when the subject takes herself to be. The metaphor is a possible world as a map and a centered world as a map with a "you are here" arrow. This is, quite literally, an ontology built to hold whatever is expressible from a first-person standpoint but lost in a perspective-free description. The standing critique is Cappelen and Dever's "The Inessential Indexical," which argues the alleged irreducible de se is just another instance of Frege's puzzle and carries no special philosophical weight — a live dispute about whether subjective content needs its own ontology at all.
Phenomenal/qualitative content — the quality-space tradition. This is the lineage that tries to capture the structure of experience. Its root is the old color solid and Quine's notion of a "quality space"; its mature philosophical statement is Austen Clark's "Sensory Qualities" (1993), where a quality space is an abstract structure that locates each sensory quality by the ways it can discriminably differ from others, reconstructed from similarity/discriminability judgments via multidimensional scaling — "like reconstructing the map of a country from a list of the distances between its cities." Peter Gärdenfors's "Conceptual Spaces: The Geometry of Thought" (2000) generalizes this: a conceptual space is built from quality dimensions with geometric structure, and natural concepts correspond to convex regions — color modeled by hue, saturation, brightness, pitch as a curved one-dimensional space, and so on. Paul Churchland's state-space/vector account of qualia is the neurocomputational cousin. The boldest version isIntegrated Information Theory: Balduzzi and Tononi's "Qualia: The Geometry of Integrated Information" (2009) posits a qualia space Q with an axis for each possible state of a system, within which the quality of an experience is a "shape" — a quale — specified by the informational relationships the system generates, while Φ measures the quantity. In IIT 3.0 this becomes the identity claim that an experience just is a maximally irreducible conceptual structure, a constellation of concepts in qualia space. Whether that identity is earned is exactly what its critics contest, but as a formal ontology of phenomenal content it is the most ambitious on offer.
That ambition has since broadened into an explicit research program — the "structural turn" in consciousness science, associated with Johannes Kleiner and the Association for Mathematical Consciousness Science. The aim is a general definition of what a "mathematical structure of conscious experience" is, meant to unify the scattered quality-space, qualia-space, and phenomenal-space approaches into a common formal language. Its method is structure-preserving maps — isomorphisms or homomorphisms between phenomenal structure and physical/neural structure — andHolger Lyre's "neurophenomenal structuralism" pushes the strong line that specific phenomenal experiences are strictly relational affairs, with a homomorphic mapping of self-organized neural structures onto phenomenal Q-structures. There is even category-theoretic and sheaf-theoretic work aiming at a formal account of phenomenal unity. This is the closest thing alive today to a dedicated formal framework for "the structure of subjective content."
Intentional content — the phenomenological lineage. Worth naming separately because Husserl actually coined the term formal ontology, and his noesis/noema/horizon apparatus is an account of the structure of intentional (hence subjective) content. The noesis is the intentional process; the noema is its ideal content, "the object as intended." The analytically-formalized reading — Føllesdal, and David Woodruff Smith and Ronald McIntyre's "Husserl and Intentionality" — treats the noema as a generalized Fregean sense, giving a "semantic," roughly possible-worlds-flavored formal model of intentionality in which objects are "constituted" in consciousness via noema and horizon. A different tradition from the quality-space one, aimed at the structure of directedness rather than of sensory feel.
Normative/attitudinal content. For evaluative subjectivity there is a genuine formalism in Gibbard's "Thinking How to Live": fact-prac worlds (a possible world paired with a complete contingency plan, or "hyperplan"), where a normative judgment is modeled set-theoretically as ruling out fact-prac worlds — an apparatus purpose-built to give the structure of a kind of subjective (planning) content while staying expressivist about its function.
Credal content. And for the precise case of subjective probability, the de Finetti/Ramsey tradition of subjective Bayesianism is a fully worked-out formal ontology: credences as coherent degrees of belief, pinned down operationally by betting behavior and backed by representation theorems. One narrow kind of subjective content, completely formalized.
Where the two projects pull apart — the hard part
Three tensions are worth sitting with, because they're where the substance is and where nothing is settled.
First, the deepest obstacle to any general structural ontology of the subjective is the Newman problem (Russell's old objection, now central to the structuralist turn). Pure structure — relations specified only up to isomorphism — is too cheap: it pins down the organization of experience while leaving the intrinsic qualitative character underdetermined. So a quality space or a Φ-shape can capture how experiences stand to one another (this red nearer that orange than this blue) without capturing what redness is like at all. Which means the formal frameworks are strongest exactly where subjectivity is relational — similarity orderings, self-location, planning, credence — and weakest where it is supposed to beintrinsic and qualitative. Nagel's point reappears inside the formal project: the residue that resists the view from nowhere is the same residue that resists structuralization.
Second, there's an unresolved clash between the two parts. The relativist and expressivist semantics in Part 1 treat subjective discourse as something other than ordinary world-carving propositional content — it expresses attitudes, or is true only relative to an assessor. But the centered-worlds and quality-space ontologies in Part 2 do the opposite: they re-propositionalize the subjective, modeling it as sets of centered worlds, or points and shapes in a space, i.e. as perfectly respectable truth-evaluable structure. So a real question sits underneath both literatures: is subjective content best understood as a refined kind of truth-apt content (Part 2's bet) or as content that isn't truth-evaluable in the standard way at all (the expressivist's bet)? These programs have largely not been made to confront each other directly, and they can't both be the whole story.
Third — and this is why I'd resist any tidy "yes, framework X does it" — no one has a unified ontology covering everything expressible subjectively. What exists is a set of domain-specific formalisms (de se properties, quality spaces, noemata, fact-prac worlds, credence functions), each capturing one band of the spectrum, with no agreed account of how they compose into a single structure of subjectivity, or whether they even should. The most promising candidate for a general such framework is the mathematical-consciousness-science / structuralist program, precisely because it's trying to state the unifying notion of "a structure of experience" — but it's also the program that runs hardest into the Newman problem, which is to say it's strongest at the ambition and weakest at the residue that made the subjective seem distinctive in the first place.
