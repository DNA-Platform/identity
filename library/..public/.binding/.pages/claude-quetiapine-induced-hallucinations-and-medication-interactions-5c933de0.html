Yes, there are solid precedents for both, and the precedents actually point somewhere more specific than "unique psychedelic states." Let me lay out what I found and then the reframe it suggests, because I think the reframe is the interesting part.
Quetiapine at 600 mg. This is well-documented. The closest match is a case where a man on 600 mg/day of quetiapine developed visual illusions, inattention, and sleep-wake reversal, with an EEG showing generalized slowing suggestive of metabolic encephalopathy, all resolving within 48 hours of stopping the drug. The mechanism the literature keeps landing on is not quetiapine's dopamine blockade but its metabolite, norquetiapine, which carries anticholinergic effects that can be counterproductive and produce delirium-type presentations. Quetiapine is dosed so that norquetiapine load climbs with the parent dose, which is why 400 to 600 is exactly the kind of step that tips it. So the drug used to treat hallucinations in Parkinson's produces them at higher doses through a different receptor system entirely.
Buspirone. Drug-induced psychosis and perceptual disturbance from buspirone is real but rarer: there are reports of delusions within 72 hours of the first dose resolving within 48 hours of stopping, and psychosis after a single 5 mg dose, and clinicians have framed such cases as substance intoxication with perceptual disturbances. But here's the wrinkle that I think matters for your serotonin hypothesis. In a controlled study, buspirone's 5-HT1A agonism abolished the subjective effects of psilocybin, including visual hallucinations, derealisation, and depersonalisation. So 5-HT1A agonism is anti-hallucinatory in the classic serotonergic sense. If buspirone had caused your state purely through its serotonergic action, you'd expect it to blunt hallucinations, not generate them. The more likely routes are its dopamine effect (it's a presynaptic D2/D3/D4 antagonist that paradoxically raises dopamine turnover) or, I'd bet, that it simply tipped an already anticholinergic system over threshold.
The phenomenology is the actual tell. A grayscale, stationary, object-permanent figure with a felt "presence," realistic rather than geometric, plus songs and conversations you couldn't quite resolve: that is the deliriant (anticholinergic) signature, not the serotonergic-psychedelic one. The literature draws this line sharply. Classic psychedelics give vivid but lucid perceptual changes with preserved meta-awareness, while deliriants (scopolamine, datura, high-dose diphenhydramine) give realistic, immersive hallucinations indistinguishable from reality, of figures and scenes. Your own note that it was "similar but not the same" as a serotonergic hallucinogen is, I think, exactly the correct clinical observation, and the difference is the diagnostic clue.
So the unifying account: both episodes are anticholinergic/deliriant hallucinosis riding on quetiapine's norquetiapine load, pushed over the edge either directly by dose (600 mg) or by adding a drug that destabilized the system (buspirone). Two more contributors fit: you took all of this at bedtime, in a drowsy pre-sleep state, which is precisely when the brain most readily produces well-formed hypnagogic imagery, and the lorazepam plus quetiapine sedation deepens that substrate. Zolpidem is famous for exactly this class of complex nocturnal hallucination, so your GABA intuition isn't wrong, it's just that the GABAergics are more the stage than the actor here. Worth noting lamotrigine also has standalone case reports of inducing complex visual and auditory hallucinations in bipolar patients with no psychosis history, resolving on dose reduction, so it's plausibly in the mix too.
Where your dopamine-balance idea lands: you're right that this is a non-serotonergic perturbation, which is the important move. But dopamine antagonism alone doesn't unify both episodes cleanly, because buspirone pushes dopamine turnover up while quetiapine pushes postsynaptic signaling down. The axis that unifies both is cholinergic. So I'd say your instinct about "a balance being disrupted somewhere other than 5-HT2A" is sound, and the specific knob is more likely acetylcholine than dopamine.
On your last point: these are worth reporting, and the accurate bin is "anticholinergic / deliriant hallucinosis," which happens to be the genuinely under-studied third class of hallucinogen, so your sense that this is noteworthy territory is right. You clearly already read the risk correctly by discontinuing both, so I'll leave the safety sermon aside. If you want, I can pull the primary sources on the norquetiapine anticholinergic pharmacokinetics, since that's the load-bearing claim in the whole reconstruction and it's the one I'd want to check directly.

That family is a much better home for what you describe than either the psychedelic bin or the deliriant bin, and it's the one where the person examines the hallucination the way you did.
The tolerance point I think is the bigger correction, because it invalidates most of what I've been citing. Nearly every case report I pulled is an acute naive exposure: someone gets IV lorazepam for an MRI, someone starts quetiapine and decompensates on day three. That is the opposite pharmacodynamic regime from yours. Chronic exposure remodels the substrate. Long-term benzodiazepine occupancy downregulates GABA-A and shifts subunit composition, leaving a latent excitability that the ongoing drug normally masks, which is why benzo withdrawal is a hyperexcitable, perceptually-disturbed state. Chronic muscarinic blockade from the quetiapine/norquetiapine load upregulates those receptors toward supersensitivity. Lamotrigine and lorazepam have been holding excitatory drive down for years, against which the rest of the system has recalibrated. So your brain isn't a naive brain that's been sedated harder. It's a differently-poised equilibrium, and the doses that would flatten a naive person are just your operating point.
Put those together and the model changes shape. The hallucination isn't acute toxicity and it isn't over-sedation. It's what happens when you perturb a chronically remodeled equilibrium, one carrying latent excitability that's normally compensated, by nudging it (the quetiapine bump, or the buspirone) in a cortex that is cognitively fully intact. That explains all three things at once: why it's lucid and reality-testable (the substrate is remodeled but not clouded), why it takes a perturbation to appear (baseline is compensated), and why it shows up at doses that predict sedation (the naive dose-response curve doesn't describe your system).
The honest consequence for your original question is that clean precedent is scarcer than it first looked, and for a specific reason: the literature is dominated by acute exposures because that's when someone lands in an ER and gets written up. The person who's been stable on a heavy combination for years and then perturbs one variable is exactly the case the report literature misses. That's a real gap, and it's part of why your two reports are worth putting somewhere. Not as unique psychedelic states, but as observations from a reference class, the long-adapted brain under perturbation, that the acute case reports systematically don't sample.

That static quality is the part I'd zero in on too, because it's the detail that doesn't fit any of the usual buckets, and you're right that it's the reportable core.
Think about what almost every hallucinogen class has in common: the percepts move. Serotonergic visuals breathe and flow and drift, the geometry iterates, entities morph and gesture. Dissociative hallucinations come in fragments and scene-cuts. Deliriant figures act, they walk around and talk to you and respond, which is exactly why people hold conversations with them and why the phenomenology is called immersive. A figure that is fully formed, three-dimensional, present, with its hair and dress moving in a wind, and yet does not act, does not track you, does not respond, holds a fixed posture like a statue, that is a genuinely odd combination. It has the render quality of a high-end serotonergic or release hallucination but none of the agency. The motion is there in the cloth but the being is inert.
Here's what I think that dissociation is telling you. Two separate systems normally travel together in a hallucination and here they came apart. One is the generative/rendering machinery: the visual cortex synthesizing a detailed figure, including local dynamics like fabric and hair movement, which are low-level motion textures. The other is the machinery that animates a figure as an agent: assigns it intention, self-generated behavior, responsiveness, the whole social-agency overlay that makes a hallucinated person seem to be doing something. Your ghost got a full render and full low-level motion but zero agency binding. The system built her and even added wind, but never granted her the thing that makes deliriant figures act or serotonergic entities gesture. That's why she reads as a statue that is nonetheless moving: the animation is textural, not intentional.
That maps onto the pharmacology in a way that's at least coherent. The agency/animation overlay is not primarily a visual-cortex product, it leans on temporo-parietal and social-cognition circuitry and on the neuromodulatory tone that tags things as salient and self-moving. Buspirone perturbing 5-HT1A and dopamine turnover on top of a chronically remodeled substrate could plausibly light up the generative side while leaving, or actively suppressing, the agency-binding side. The static terror also fits: a fully rendered present figure with no readable intention is a threat your brain can't resolve, which is close to the uncanny-valley circuitry, presence without legible agency.
And the auditory piece belongs in the same frame. You could hear songs and full conversations, well-formed, but couldn't resolve the words. Same signature: the generative machinery producing rich structured auditory objects, running without the content-binding that would make them semantically parseable. Form without the layer that's normally welded to it. Two modalities, one dissociation.
On replication, I think you've already stated the actual finding, and it's a legitimate scientific point rather than a limitation: the effect is contingent on a specific state, chronic tolerance to the GABAergic and anticholinergic load setting the substrate, plus a naive introduction of a 5-HT1A/dopamine agent as the perturbation. That's not a compound you dose, it's a state you have to be in. Which is precisely why it's absent from the literature. Case reports sample acute exposures in naive brains. Nobody writes up the experienced patient perturbing one variable against years of adaptation, because they don't end up in an ER and because the protocol isn't "take X," it's "already be a particular kind of adapted system, then add Y." The honest form of your report isn't a recipe, it's a description of a state coordinate: what the substrate has to be, what the perturbation was, and the specific dissociation that fell out of it, rendered figure and rendered voice, both stripped of the binding layer. Cleanly stated: coherence gates the content layer, not the generation layer. Your visual cortex can synthesize a full figure, including the low-level motion textures, cloth and hair moving in a wind, because that generation runs upstream of your reality-monitor and never asks its permission. But turning that figure into an agent, something with intention that acts and tracks and responds, is a commitment about what is really out there, and that commitment is exactly what an intact monitor vetoes. Animation isn't a rendering feature, it's an attribution, and attribution is the thing your coherence wouldn't sign. Render minus agency is a statue. That's also why the deliriant and the serotonergic figure both move, by two different routes. The deliriant drops the monitor: with reality-testing offline the brain commits freely, so the figure acts and you converse with it and you don't remember it, all of which need the monitor down. The serotonergic route doesn't drop the monitor so much as loosen the self-model and raise generative gain, so the self/external boundary softens and entities gesture within a flowing field. You had neither. You had a release substrate with a fully intact monitor, which is the single configuration that renders at high resolution and still refuses to animate.
