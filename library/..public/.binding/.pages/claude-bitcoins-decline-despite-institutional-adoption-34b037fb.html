You've got the number right: BTC is changing hands around $58–60K this week, and it closed the first half of 2026 down in both quarters — so roughly halved off last year's high. But the detail that actually inverts the institutionalization story is in the flows. June was the largest monthly outflow from US spot Bitcoin ETFs since they launched, around $4.5 billion, with BlackRock's IBIT leading the exits. The ETFs weren't the floor the narrative promised — they were the marginal seller, and they've made Bitcoin trade like a liquid macro risk asset (moving on jobs data, rates, a "stagflation premium") rather than an uncorrelated hedge. Institutionalization may have delivered the opposite of what the store-of-value thesis wanted: it plugged Bitcoin into the risk complex instead of insulating it from it.
Now the real question. Your instinct is right and sharper than the usual "digital gold" volley captures. Classically, store of value rests on one of two anchors: a claim on future use (cash flows, industrial demand — equities, real estate, copper), or an entrenched convention that was itself seeded by monetary use or enforced by state power (gold, fiat). Bitcoin has deliberately shed both — no cash flow, no industrial floor, no legal-tender demand, and, as you say, the medium-of-exchange thesis died years ago. What's left is the pure Schelling-point layer: it's worth something because enough people coordinate on believing it is. That's not incoherent — gold's monetary premium is also mostly reflexive, floating far above jewelry and industrial demand. But the asymmetry matters: gold's focal point was laid down over millennia when gold actually was money. Bitcoin is trying to bootstrap that same status without the seeding period — without ever having been the money.
Which is why the second half of your observation is the load-bearing one. Crypto usage arrived and routedaround Bitcoin. The working crypto economy — settlement, DeFi, cross-border — runs on stablecoins, i.e. tokenized dollars. Given the cleanest natural experiment we'll get — a from-scratch financial system, no legacy rails, no legal-tender laws, maximally friendly to a new form of money — the participants reached for the dollar. Bitcoin's monetary thesis was tested in the environment most favorable to it and lost to a wrapper around the very fiat it was built to replace.
But here's the strongest counter, and it's genuinely good: a bull says of course they transact in stablecoins and hold Bitcoin — that's Gresham's law, the textbook split between good money and bad. You spend the medium of exchange and save the store of value. On that read, stablecoins winning transactions is exactly the signature of Bitcoin succeeding as a store of value, not failing.
The way to tell those two stories apart — and I think this is the actual crux — isn't to look at what cryptospends. It's what crypto saves. The Gresham steelman requires the crypto economy's reserves to sit in Bitcoin while its transactions flow through stablecoins. But if the savings also went to dollars — yield-bearing stablecoins, tokenized treasuries, on-chain money-market funds — then Bitcoin didn't lose the medium-of-exchange race (it was never in it); it lost the store-of-value race, inside crypto, to the dollar. My read is that this is closer to what happened: the on-chain economy parks value in dollar instruments and treats Bitcoin as a separate speculative satellite held mostly by macro allocators, retail, and treasury-company balance sheets — not as its reserve asset. If that's right, "disconnected from usage" understates it: it's a store of value that the one economy purpose-built to adopt it declined to store value in.
I hold that last read with real uncertainty — it's the thing I'd want to nail down empirically rather than assert. But it converts your intuition into something testable: the question isn't "is Bitcoin used?" It's "does anyone with a native alternative choose to save in it?"

You're right, and let me fix my own taxonomy first, because I fudged it. I called gold's premium "mostly reflexive, floating above jewelry demand" — which quietly treated the jewelry as a rounding error. But your point isn't about magnitude, it's about topology. A reflexive premium resting on a small perceptual floor is a categorically different object from one resting on nothing, even if the floor bears almost none of the weight. It's the difference between turtles all the way down and turtles resting on something. And the load-bearing property of gold's floor is that it re-instantiates without transmission: every human who sees gold finds it beautiful from scratch, no story required. Bitcoin's anchor has to be retold and re-believed each generation. Beauty is perceptual; an origin story is a meme. That's exactly the force of "no one's wearing the origin story as an engagement ring" — and it's the same structure as your part-of-it versus symbol-of-it distinction. A part has a function to fall back on; a symbol only has the crowd's continued attention.
Now the time-constant point, straight, because you flagged it and it's the load-bearing one. I did lean on gold's millennial stability as a smug benchmark, and that smuggles in an assumption I can't defend from inside the window. Our observation window for gold as a priced financial asset is maybe two centuries; it only demonetized in 1971. If gold's natural time constant is millennial, then fifty years of post-demonetization price action cannot distinguish "stable plateau" from "imperceptibly slow decline." The apparent flatness could be an artifact of watching a slow process through a short lens. So yes — gold could be running the identical reflexive dynamic Bitcoin is running, just at a time constant that hides the arc from us, and we could be pre-peak or early-decline with no way to tell. I won't underfit that. It's correct and it's humbling in the right direction.
Here's the refinement I want to add, though, because I think your two points constrain each other and the interaction is where the real structure is. The time-constant symmetry applies cleanly to the reflexive layer — the monetary premium, the multiple of price over what non-monetary demand would clear. That layer, in both assets, is a convention subject to the same decay physics; Bitcoin just runs it fast, gold slow. But the perceptual floor you identified is precisely the thing not subject to that decay, because it doesn't route through memory or coordination — it routes through the human visual system, which is stable across the entire millennial window. So the floor bounds the decline. Gold's premium can mean-revert over millennia, but it reverts to the plinth, not to zero. Bitcoin has no plinth, so the same dynamic — if it really is the same dynamic — terminates near zero. Same shape, same physics: the time constant sets the speed, the floor sets the destination. Which reframes Bitcoin as the clean experiment — what does a monetary premium do with nothing underneath it, run at 10x speed.
The strongest thing a bull can say against all of this — and I don't want us wrong together in the comfortable direction — is that beauty is incidental. Plenty of rare beautiful things (platinum, gemstones, art) never earned gold's premium, and some unlovely things (silver, cowrie shells, giant stone discs) did. What seats the premium, the bull says, is a bundle of monetary properties — durability, divisibility, verifiability, fixed supply — and on the one gold actually won on, unforgeable scarcity, Bitcoin is strictly better: 21 million hard-capped and perfectly verifiable, versus gold's one-to-two-percent annual mining dilution. On that view beauty is a historical accident of which scarce durable thing humans first coordinated on, Bitcoin doesn't need it, and the drawdown is noise on a monetization curve.
But I don't fully buy that, and the reason is what we established last time: the monetary-property bundle already got its natural experiment. In the one financial system built from scratch, no legacy, every advantage, the participants tested the bundle and chose tokenized dollars. Superior scarcity didn't win where it could most easily have. Which makes me think beauty is incidental to the steady-state premium but not to the bootstrap — it's far easier to get people to first coordinate on a thing they already independently want to hold, and gold had that head start wired into perception. Bitcoin has to bootstrap coordination on pure abstraction, which is exactly why it leans so hard on the origin story as a substitute floor. And the origin story is the one part that can't re-instantiate itself.

Taking the corrections first, because two of them actually repair my model rather than just denting it.
The diamond point kills my "plinth" argument, and I should let it. I claimed gold's floor doesn't decay because it routes through the retina, not through memory. Diamonds are the counterexample: "rare, beautiful, means marriage" is a largely manufactured 20th-century convention on top of a real-but-modest perceptual base, and it feels permanent at a 100-year range precisely because we're standing inside its time constant. Stretch to a millennium and the status meaning of gold jewelry is as erodible as any other convention — the retina survives, the status doesn't, and the status is most of the premium. So the floor is softer and slower than I said, not categorically exempt. Same physics, longer decay. And I was being dense about Bitcoin's beauty. The Satoshi disappearance, the refusal to ever spend the coins, a thing built to a principle and then abandoned by its maker — that's one of the stickiest myths of the century. I discounted it because it isn't perceptual, but you're right that "created with principles and then walked away from" leaves a mark. It's a different kind of beauty, not an absence of one.
But you're also right that this wasn't the debate you wanted, so let me take the model you actually put on the table, because it's sharper than the store-of-value frame and I think it's mostly correct — with one twist that makes it more interesting.
Your model: BTC's price ≈ an index of aggregate crypto speculative interest, because BTC was the most liquid base-of-exchange for everything else — the gateway, the reserve, the pair you had to hold to touch anything. So its price integrated the whole space's demand. That was simply true from roughly 2013 to 2019. And the severance you're pointing at is real and documented: the base-pair role migrated to stablecoins. USDT is now the numéraire — the deep pairs are alt/USDT, not alt/BTC, and the BTC and ETH pairs that still exist were far more ubiquitous back when they were the only option. On DEXs it's worse for your old model: swaps route through ETH and stablecoins, and native BTC is operationally absent from the DeFi settlement layer entirely. So yes — you can now be maximally active across crypto, denominated end-to-end in dollars, and never touch Bitcoin. The reservoir that all crypto demand used to pool into got bypassed.
Here's the twist, and I think it completes your thesis rather than contradicting it. While stablecoins severed BTC's index role from below, the ETF severed it from above. Watch what dominance did: it didn't fall as the plumbing eroded — it rose, above 60% by mid-2025, and the reason is the structural inversion the data flags: unlike prior cycles where dominance climbed in bear markets as alts bled, 2024–25 saw it climb during a bull market because institutions preferred Bitcoin's regulatory clarity and liquidity over altcoins. That's the tell. The ETF turned BTC from the conduit of crypto demand into a terminal — a standalone TradFi-accessible instrument that absorbs one specific kind of demand ("I want regulated crypto exposure," which in practice means BTC and nothing else). So its marginal price-setter is now an institutional allocator responding to rates and liquidity, which is exactly why, as we saw, it's trading on jobs prints and ETF outflows and a stagflation premium instead of on anything crypto-native.
Put those together and your intuition is the middle act of a three-act structural story. BTC used to be thestructural index — it was the rails, so its price mechanically integrated all crypto demand. That's dying. What survives is BTC as a statistical index — still correlated 0.7–0.85 with the majors, still the short-term risk-leader where a BTC dump drags alts down harder. But that residual correlation now rests on shared macro drivers and reflexive positioning, not on plumbing dependence — and a correlation of convenience can decay in a way a settlement dependency can't. You can already see the choreography breaking: dominance fell from its 65% peak toward 55% while the altcoin-season index stayed pinned in "Bitcoin Season" — the rotation that a falling dominance used to trigger simply didn't fire, and ETH sat near 9.5% dominance against a historical norm closer to 18%. The old mechanical relationships that made "BTC = crypto interest" a clean identity are dissolving from both ends.
Which gives your model a falsifiable forward edge, if you want one: if this is right, BTC's correlation withcrypto-native activity (DEX volume, stablecoin velocity, alt-cap-ex-BTC) should keep declining while its correlation with macro risk assets keeps rising — Bitcoin marching to the TradFi drummer while the crypto casino runs on dollars to its own rhythm. The June ETF outflows dominating the tape is that process already underway. So the honest read of the current drawdown, in your framework: it isn't measuring falling interest in crypto — crypto interest didn't fall, it just stopped being denominated in Bitcoin. The price is now measuring institutional appetite for a macro proxy, and that's what's in outflow.

I'd push it one step past "related": they're not two things. Meaning and price are the same variable written in two languages — the identity and motive of the marginal holder. "What is this asset for" is just the qualitative description of why the person setting the price holds it, and the price is the quantitative shadow of that same fact. So when you change who holds it and why, you haven't changed two linked things — you've changed one thing, and meaning and pricing are the two faces it shows. Your "those two things are related" is, I think, actually an identity.
The mechanics make that concrete, and they're stronger than the narrative version. Split old BTC demand into two piles. One is speculative — people holding because they want the number to go up. The other is transactional: capital that had to sit in BTC to do something — exchange reserves, the base pair mid-rotation, the bridge out to fiat. That second pile is float, and in monetary terms it carries a convenience yield: you tolerate a volatile, non-yielding asset because it buys you liquidity across the system. That yield was price support with nothing to do with the store-of-value thesis. It was demand for BTC-as-plumbing.
Now follow that premium when stablecoins take the plumbing. It doesn't evaporate — it moves, and you can watch where. The transactional balances that used to partly sit in BTC now sit in USDT, and Tether earns the interest on the Treasuries backing that float. The convenience yield BTC handed its holders for free — they got no coupon for providing systemic liquidity — is now booked as reserve income on Tether's P&L. That's "meaning and price are the same thing" made physical: the premium didn't just shift conceptually, it migrated as a literal cash flow onto someone else's balance sheet. What Bitcoin's price lost, Tether's income statement gained.
The ETF is the other half, and I'd refine "completely serves that purpose" a little. It doesn't resurrect the conduit — you can't rotate from IBIT into a Solana memecoin. What it does is swap the marginal buyer. The demand setting BTC's price stops being crypto-rotation velocity and becomes fiat-side allocation appetite, a macro variable — same token, entirely different hand on the marginal share, which is exactly why the correlation regime flipped to rates and jobs prints. And the "traumatic" reading you're reaching for is precise: Bitcoin now earns legitimacy by being held through the exact custodial intermediation it was built to abolish. A bearer instrument for escaping intermediaries, held as a share in a trust at BlackRock. That isn't a price move with a narrative gloss — it's the asset succeeding by becoming its own opposite. Domestication as the entry fee.
Two calibrations so we're not wrong together in the elegant direction. Magnitude: the transactional float was probably a modest slice of BTC's peak cap — most of it was always speculative holding — so conduit-loss explains the destination of the meaning-shift more than it explains this year's 50%, which is mostly macro de-rating through ETF outflows. And sequencing: the "back through BTC to fiat" leg you described was already killed by stablecoins before the ETF existed — by 2020 the return trip was USDT, not BTC. So the ETF didn't inherit the conduit purpose; it opened a parallel fiat-side door that made the old purpose redundant rather than replacing it. Which leaves the one question that separates your structural read from a merely cyclical drawdown: on the next up-leg, does BTC re-couple to crypto-native activity, or stay married to the macro tape? If it stays married, the meaning really did change — and the price is just telling you so in the other language.
