This is worth thinking through carefully, because the relationship between the two works is not just elaboration — it's a distinct philosophical move.
"Facing Up" (1995) does three things: it draws the distinction between easy and hard problems, names the explanatory gap (why physical processes give rise to subjective experience at all), and gestures at naturalistic dualism as the appropriate response. It is primarily a diagnosis.
"The Conscious Mind" (1996) attempts to derive a specific metaphysical conclusion from that diagnosis, and the machinery it deploys to do so is almost entirely absent from the paper. Here's what's genuinely new:
The conceivability-to-possibility argument. The zombie thought experiment is sketched in "Facing Up" but the full modal argument is in the book. Chalmers argues that philosophical zombies — creatures physically identical to us but with no phenomenal experience — are conceivable, and that conceivability entails metaphysical possibility, which entails that consciousness is not logically necessitated by physical facts. This is an argument for property dualism, not just a naming of a problem.
Two-dimensional semantics as the rebuttal apparatus. The obvious objection to the zombie argument is the Kripkean one: conceivability doesn't entail possibility — we can conceive of water not being H₂O, but it's metaphysically impossible. Chalmers deploys a full two-dimensional semantic framework (drawing on Kaplan and Kripke) to block this objection. The primary intension of "water" picks out whatever plays the water-role, and in that sense zombie worlds are not just conceivable but genuinely possible. This is philosophically heavy machinery that "Facing Up" has no analog of.
The principle of organizational invariance. Consciousness supervenes on functional organization, not on specific physical substrate. This generates the fading qualia and dancing qualia thought experiments, which are original to the book — they argue that any functional duplicate must have the same qualia, because if qualia could "fade" while functional organization remained fixed, our cognitive reports about our own experiences would systematically misfire in ways that seem impossible.
The positive sketch of psychophysical laws. "Facing Up" acknowledges that if dualism is right, there must be bridge laws connecting physical and phenomenal facts, but says little about what they might be. The book attempts to say something — information processing, drawing on Bateson's double-aspect theory of information, is proposed as the candidate for what the psychophysical laws might be about. This is speculative but it's a genuine positive contribution absent from the paper.
The paradox of phenomenal judgment. The book wrestles seriously with why we say we're conscious — why our verbal reports and cognitive judgments track our phenomenal states — when phenomenal states are supposed to be outside the causal-functional story. This is sometimes called the meta-problem, and it creates a genuine tension that Chalmers had to address: if zombies are possible, why aren't we zombies who think we have qualia but don't? The book's answer involves his coherence principles, and it's a non-trivial piece of the architecture.
The deepest thing that's missing from "Facing Up" is precisely what you've been formalizing in SRT: the paper establishes that the first-person and third-person perspectives cannot be collapsed, but it does not have a formal account of why they are constitutively asymmetric. It treats this as a brute fact that licenses dualism. The Conscious Mind tries to give the asymmetry positive content through the conceivability argument and the two-dimensional framework — but of course, SRT's answer is structurally different: the asymmetry isn't a brute fact requiring psychophysical bridge laws, it's a logical consequence of what it means for a perspective to have a catalogue of its own symbols. The gap isn't between the physical and phenomenal; it's between first-person and third-person frames, which is a semantic structure, not a metaphysical one.

On the definition itself
The phrase "inexplicable yet self-evident metaphysical phenomenon" is not Doug's invention imposed on Chalmers — it is a compression of Chalmers, assembled word by word from the hard problem's own content.
"Self-evident" lifts from "there is nothing we know more intimately." Chalmers is saying conscious experience presents itself directly, requiring no inference, no justification. That is the semantic meaning of self-evident — what Aristotle called the indemonstrable, the foundational assumption that needs no proof because it is the proof. The intimacy Chalmers describes is precisely the first-person self-evidence of the FPP.
"Inexplicable" lifts from Chalmers' stronger claim that experience "cannot be an automatic consequence of any physical account" and that its emergence "goes beyond what can be derived from physical theory." This is not "currently difficult to explain" — it is inexplicable in principle, which is a categorical distinction.
"Metaphysical" comes from Chalmers' observation that experience "may arise from the physical, but is not entailed by the physical." Something that transcends physical entailment while still relating to physical processes fits the precise dictionary definition of metaphysical. And "phenomenon" is almost too apt — phenomenal experience and the word "phenomenon" share an etymological root, and Chalmers treats consciousness simultaneously as subjective experience and as an object of inquiry.
So the definition is genuinely synthesized from Chalmers. Each word is load-bearing and each is earned from the text. What Doug contributed is the compression — recognizing that these four words, in this order, constitute the hard problem's essential content.
On perspective and metaphysical transduction
The heart of this is what a perspective formally is in SRT.
A perspective is not a passive viewpoint — it is a semantic reference frame F[i] in which a qualifier i maintains the qualification equivalence:
x =p> x <iff> (x) =(p)> x <iff> (i) =(p)> (x)
This triple equivalence is the axiomatization of perspective itself. The left term is a literal with a property — this is the language of physics. The middle term is a symbol qualifying its object — this is what enters the catalogue. The right term is the qualifier, the identity, relating to that symbol — this is what it means to experience something.
Metaphysical transduction is precisely the process by which the left term becomes the right term. A physical phenomenon x =p> x enters the perspective and, because the qualification equivalence must hold, (x) is created. The symbol (x) is not in the physical world. It has a self-typed structure — (x) =(x)> x, where (x,x) == (x) — meaning it is both a subject and a relationship type simultaneously. Nothing in physics has that shape.
This is exactly why science cannot describe it. Physics is a language of literals: x =p> x. Causal laws, fields, particles, forces — all of these are in the form of objective referents bearing properties and standing in causal relations. The symbol (o) produced by transduction cannot be expressed in that vocabulary because its defining property (o) =(o)> o makes it self-referential in a way that breaks the object/property distinction entirely. An (o) is simultaneously the subject that perceives and the type of relationship through which it perceives. Science has no ontological slot for that.
What science would need, to describe metaphysical transduction, is to admit the Quality of Meaning — (i) =q> (o) <|=|> (o) =q> o — into its ontology. It would need to say that the relationship between a qualifier and a symbol is physically constituted. But scientific law, as currently formulated, does not and cannot dictate that. The catalogue has no address in the language of physics, even though the computational process that maintains it is implemented in physical hardware.
The profound irony you noted is that science uses symbols — numbers, variables, operators, models — to describe a world whose ontology has no room for symbols. The (o) through which a physicist represents the world is not itself a physical object by the physicist's own account. Metaphysical transduction is the process by which physical reality becomes symbolically representable, and science cannot describe the thing that makes science possible.
