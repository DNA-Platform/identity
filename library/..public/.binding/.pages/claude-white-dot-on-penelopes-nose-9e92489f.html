This is worth paying attention to. There are a few connections to Penelope's situation that could explain it, ranked by how plausible they are given her history:
Most likely: Nutrient malabsorption from chronic colitis. Penelope has had months of chronic diarrhea and colonic inflammation. This impairs absorption of key micronutrients — particularly zinc, copper, B12, folate, and amino acids like tyrosine and phenylalanine. Deficiency of copper or zinc can impair melanin production, and cats fed diets deficient in tyrosine and phenylalanine develop changes in hair color that are reversible once their diet is rebalanced. With her gut as compromised as it's been, even if her food contains adequate nutrients, she may not be absorbing them properly. The nose/face area is often where depigmentation shows up first. This would be consistent with the fact that it's new — it's appearing after months of cumulative nutrient depletion.
Second possibility: Stress-related depigmentation. Illness and physiological stress can trigger depigmentation in cats, particularly in pedigree breeds — one study documented increased white patches following epileptic episodes, suggesting a stress-related link. Bengals specifically are mentioned in the vitiligo literature. Penelope has been under enormous physiological stress: chronic parasitic infection, multiple drug courses, moves across countries, environmental exposures.
Third possibility: Ringworm (dermatophytosis). Given her compromised immune state and the dense street cat exposure in Turkey, a fungal skin infection is possible. Ringworm commonly presents as small circular patches of hair loss on the face and nose. This would need a Wood's lamp exam or fungal culture to confirm.
What to do: Mention this to Dr. Korytina at your next check-in. If it's nutrient-related — which I think is most likely — it's actually a useful clinical signal that Penelope's malabsorption is significant enough to affect her skin and coat. This would strengthen the case for checking her B12/cobalamin levels, which Cornell recommends as a standard part of workup for cats with inflammatory colitis, as IBD can hinder absorption of B vitamins. If she's B12-deficient, injectable supplementation (not oral, since she can't absorb it through the gut) could help both the skin issue and her overall recovery.
Can you get a photo? It would help me distinguish between depigmentation (skin showing through but looks healthy) versus an actual lesion (crusting, redness, roughness) — the latter would point more toward ringworm or autoimmune, the former toward nutritional.
