Research findings: Cross-border residency split between parent and minor child
What I found in Georgia (the country)
No published Georgian case precedent surfaced in English-language sources matching the exact scenario — a mother retaining her valid TRP while her minor child's renewal is denied because the child's underlying documents (passport from origin country) couldn't be obtained or renewed. Georgian administrative court decisions on individual TRP refusals are not systematically published in English, so absence in my search is not the same as absence in fact. The Georgian Public Defender (Ombudsman) reports and TDI publish migration-related case summaries, but I didn't find one matching this fact pattern.
The legal architecture that creates the risk is well-documented, and it's structural, not discretionary:
Each TRP application is individual — family members submit separately even when reuniting under a sponsor's permit (ExpatHub).
Under Article 6 of the Procedures (Order No. 520), every applicant — including a minor — must submit "a copy of the alien's travel document" (passport). Georgia gives almost no flexibility here; the passport is the one document that cannot be substituted (matsne.gov.ge).
Article 15 of the Procedures: a TRP is "automatically terminated upon expiry." Termination of one family member's permit doesn't cascade to the others. The mother's TRP remains valid until its own expiry; the child's lapses on its own timeline.
Article 18 of the Law on Legal Status of Aliens lists denial grounds. Inability to produce a valid passport isn't framed as a denial ground — it's an application-completeness failure, which functionally produces the same outcome: no permit.
So the mechanism in Georgia is: the mother's permit is unaffected by the child's renewal failing, and a child without a renewable origin-country passport has no path to renewal regardless of the parent's status. The Article 3(5) workaround Doug already knows about (one parent's consent is sufficient for a TRP for a minor) does not solve the passport problem — it solves a different problem (consent of the other parent for the residence application itself).
Comparable scenarios in ECHR jurisprudence
I didn't find an ECHR case on the precise pattern (parent's permit valid, child's renewal denied due to origin-country document failure). The closest jurisprudence is on the related but distinct question of family unity when one member is denied status:
Jeunesse v. Netherlands (GC, 2014) — Surinamese mother denied a residence permit; ECHR found Article 8 violation because authorities failed to weigh impact on her children. Reverse direction from your scenario, but establishes that immigration authorities must assess the family-unity impact of splitting status, not just the individual applicant (HUDOC).
Berrehab v. Netherlands (1988) — parent-child family life is not severed by divorce or geographic separation.
Tuquabo-Tekle v. Netherlands and Sen v. Netherlands — establish that where children cannot reasonably establish family life elsewhere, the state's margin of appreciation narrows.
Slivenko v. Latvia (GC, 2003) — the Grand Chamber explicitly noted that the family was expelled as a unit, which is what made the expulsion compatible with Article 8. The implication: splitting a family by denying one member's status raises a stronger Article 8 claim.
Important limitation: Georgia is a Council of Europe member and ECHR-bound, but the ECHR does not give a right to family reunification in a chosen country, and the bar for finding a violation is "exceptional circumstances." A document-failure denial would likely be defended by Georgia as routine administrative process, not a rights violation. I couldn't find an ECHR case where document-renewal failure (vs. immigration policy choice) was the trigger.
The honest gap
The specific scenario you're describing — a child's residency lost because the origin country (not the host country) won't renew the underlying travel document — sits in a jurisdictional dead zone:
The host country (Georgia) treats it as the applicant's failure to produce a required document.
The origin country (e.g., Russia, requiring both parents' consent for a minor's passport renewal) treats it as a domestic family-law matter.
ECHR mechanisms generally challenge the host country's policy, but the host country has done nothing irrational — it's applied a generic document requirement.
Statelessness frameworks (1954 and 1961 UN Conventions) are the closest legal instrument that addresses this gap, but a child with an unrenewed-but-not-renounced citizenship is typically not deemed de jure stateless. UNHCR has documented de facto statelessness in exactly this category (children unable to access nationality documents because of family conflict or state non-cooperation), but enforceable protections are limited.

Got it. The real question is about the child's TRP, not Ana's. Here's the direct answer:
By default rule, yes — Georgia would deny the child's TRP renewal without a valid passport. The Law on the Legal Status of Aliens (Article 6) and Order No. 520 (Article 4) treat the travel document as a non-substitutable requirement. Ana's valid status doesn't shield him; her renewal goes through, his doesn't, and he becomes an overstayer with no exit option. That's the trap.
But Georgia has two formal escape hatches built into the law that exist for precisely this kind of dead end:
1. Statelessness Determination Procedure (SDP), Articles 22–24. Once granted, this gives the child:
A renewable 3-year residence permit (Article 15(e))
A Georgian-issued travel document — a "travel passport for stateless persons" issued by the PSDA, which Georgia recognizes as a valid travel document for entry/exit
During the procedure (up to 9 months), he gets a temporary ID and cannot be removed
No fee, no lawful-stay requirement to apply, applies even if he's already overstaying
The catch: the 1954 Convention definition Georgia adopted is "not considered as a national by any State under the operation of its law" — that's de jure statelessness. A child who is still legally Russian but can't get a passport is de facto stateless, not de jure. Whether Georgia's PSDA stretches the SDP to cover the de facto case — where the country of nationality refuses to issue documents because of a parental dispute — is the actual unknown. UNHCR's partner NGO in Georgia for SDP appeals is the Innovations and Reforms Center (Tbilisi, +995 32 255 20 01) — they would know if any such case has been adjudicated. ENS (European Network on Statelessness) and Rights Georgia maintain Georgia's country profile and would also know.
2. Special residence permit by ministerial initiative, Article 15(j). A member of the Georgian government can submit a written initiative for a TRP to be issued. This is a discretionary humanitarian path — not denied for failure to produce a passport — but it requires a sponsor inside the government and is not a right.
Cross-jurisdiction analogue worth knowing about: Hoti v. Croatia (ECHR, 2018, application no. 63311/14). Mr. Hoti was born in former Yugoslavia, could not establish nationality with any successor state, and Croatia repeatedly denied him regularization. The ECHR found a violation of Article 8 because Croatia failed to provide an "effective and accessible procedure" to regularize his stay given the impossibility of obtaining documents elsewhere. The principle: when a person is genuinely unable to obtain documents from any other state through no fault of their own, the host state has a positive obligation to provide a regularization route. Georgia, as an ECHR signatory, is bound by this. It's the precedent that would be invoked on appeal if the SDP route were denied.

Short answer: Real "buy citizenship" doesn't quite exist in Georgia, and what does exist would not automatically cover the kids — that's the part that matters for your situation.
Here's how it actually works:
Property purchase ($100K) gets only a residence permit, not citizenship. A short-term TRP (renewable, 1-year terms initially), valid for the buyer plus spouse and minor children. After 10 years of continuous residence on this basis, the family becomes eligible to apply for permanent residency, then naturalization with the language/history exam. So the property route doesn't shortcut the passport problem at all — the children still need valid travel documents to maintain residency along the way.
$300K investment also gets residency, not citizenship. A 5-year investment residence permit for the principal plus spouse and minor children, which can convert to permanent residency after 5 years if turnover thresholds are met ($50K year 1, $100K year 2, $120K years 3–5). Still requires 10 years total residence to apply for citizenship, still requires language and history.
The actual citizenship-by-investment route is Article 17(2) "exceptional citizenship" — discretionary, granted by Presidential decree. The Special Contribution Citizenship Program launched in 2025 has three tiers: €3M business investment with 10 jobs created, €2M refundable fund contribution held 5 years, or €300K non-refundable government donation (currently discounted to €250K for early applicants). This route waives the residency, language, and history requirements, and explicitly permits dual citizenship (the only legal route in Georgia that does, aside from Presidential grant).
On your actual question — does it cover the family? This is where it gets unfavorable for your scenario. Article 17 of the Organic Law on Georgian Citizenship grants citizenship "by way of exception to a citizen of another country" — it's framed as an individual grant, not a family grant. The various law-firm marketing pages talk about "family inclusion" but don't cite a legal mechanism, and the actual statute doesn't have a dependent-inclusion clause the way investor residency programs do.
In practice, what I've seen described is that a successful Article 17 applicant's spouse and minor children would still need to apply separately — typically through naturalization with the spouse-of-citizen 5-year track, or through the children-of-citizen permanent residency route. Some advisors claim dependents can be added to the Article 17 application, but I haven't found this in the statute itself, and it would be at Presidential discretion case by case. The structural problem: an Article 17 grant to Ana doesn't automatically make her son a Georgian citizen, and her son still needs valid documents (presumably his Russian passport) to apply for derivative status — which puts you back at the original trap.
There is one nuance that could matter: under Article 17 of the older citizenship law (the "child upon change of citizenship of either parent" provision), if a parent acquires Georgian citizenship and the other parent remains a foreign citizen, the child may become Georgian "at the parents' joint request." Joint request is the operative phrase — meaning the Russian father's consent could again be the bottleneck. There may be a workaround if Ana has sole custody under Russian law, but that's a fact-specific legal question.
Bottom line for your two-year planning horizon:
The €250K Special Contribution route would give Ana a Georgian passport in 3–6 months without residency requirements, but it doesn't solve the children's problem on its own. The children still need either (a) their own valid Russian passports for derivative TRP, or (b) Georgian citizenship via parental request — which under the older statute appears to require both parents' consent, or (c) the SDP/statelessness route I described before.
If the budget is there, the most defensible play is probably: Ana gets exceptional citizenship → simultaneously pursue Georgian citizenship for the children via Article 17 or the parental-acquisition route, with a Georgian family-law attorney addressing the joint-consent problem in advance. Probably not, and here are the four reasons stacked against it, in descending order of severity:
1. The program may not be fully legitimate. Investment Migration Insider — the trade publication of record for citizenship-by-investment — flagged in February 2025 that they "received conflicting reports about the program's legality, as some sources question its authenticity." The program operates through a private operator (Georgia Growth Fund LLC) under an MoU with the Ministry of Economy, and uses Article 17(2) of the existing citizenship law as its statutory hook rather than dedicated legislation. It's not been tested at scale. Paying €250K into a program that gets unwound or rejected by a future president is a real risk.
2. Family inclusion is not clearly authorized for the 2025 program. Article 17(2) of the Organic Law grants citizenship "to a citizen of another country who has made a contribution of exceptional merit." It speaks of the applicant, not their family. The official program materials (residencepermits.ge, the Beijing presentation) describe procedures for individual applicants — "passport, birth certificate, background check, investment plan" — without a dependent-inclusion framework. A 2018 one-off program tied to the Chess Olympiad explicitly covered family up to age 26, but that was a different initiative. Some law firms imply family coverage for the current program, but I cannot find statutory authority for it. This would need to be confirmed in writing with the Public Service Development Agency before transferring funds.
3. Even if the program covers family, you still need the child's documents to apply. The application requires "passport, birth certificate (apostilled), background check." A child whose Russian passport has expired and who can't get a new one cannot complete the application. The €250K doesn't bypass that requirement — it bypasses residency, language, and history, not identity documents. So if the passport problem materializes before you apply, the program door closes for the child specifically.
4. The child is the harder applicant, not the easier one. The framing of "exceptional contribution" doesn't fit a minor — children don't make economic contributions. Realistically, Ana would be the named applicant (since she'd be the one whose investment justifies the grant), and the question becomes whether the children are added as derivatives. That puts you back in the territory of Article 22 of the Citizenship Law (parental change of citizenship) which references the parents' joint request — meaning the Russian father's consent could re-enter the picture.
The honest framing: if you're going to spend €250K, the actually-defensible play is to apply now — well before the passport expires — and have a Georgian citizenship lawyer structure the application with explicit dependent inclusion negotiated up front, in writing, before funds transfer. Make Ana the principal, name the children as dependents, get the Citizenship Commission's pre-clearance opinion on whether dependents are accepted under this program, and use a legal structure where the donation is contingent on approval of the entire family unit (the 2018 Chess Olympiad program reportedly did this — funds held in escrow until decision).
You'd want a lawyer who has actually closed an Article 17(2) case under the 2025 program — not just one who advertises it. That's probably 2–3 firms in Tbilisi at this stage. ExpatHub doesn't specialize in this; you'd want someone like Legal.ge, BLC Law Office, or Mgaloblishvili Kipiani Dzidziguri (MKD) — though I haven't independently verified which of those have actually closed cases.
