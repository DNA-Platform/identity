Now I have a solid picture. Let me compile the ranked list based on everything from the survey document and my additional research. I'm ranking by what will be most useful for you to study when building a site that presents a business at the illustration+interactivity intersection — prioritizing sites that either (a) present that integrated offering well, (b) have exceptional design quality in how they explain their business, or (c) sit one step away but model something you'll need to do.
Here's the list, ranked:
1. 0 — Data visualization and cartography studio, San Francisco. This is the gold standard for presenting art+technology as a unified practice. Their ethos — "we don't build first and design after... beauty isn't something that comes after utility" — is philosophically closest to what you're building. Their Watercolor basemap was acquired by the Smithsonian. Study how they frame the studio, explain their process, and position beauty as engineering, not decoration.
2. 0 — UK illustrated map specialist. Exceptional site design with clear service articulation across tourism, editorial, infographics, and wayfinding. She explains what illustration does for clients rather than just showing pretty pictures. Her services pages (especially tourism illustration) are a masterclass in presenting creative work as business problem-solving. The site itself is warm and well-structured.
3. 0 — "Data-Driven Cartography" studio. Small team that bridges design thinking with interactive web cartography. They publish the open-source Cartography Guide, created ColorBrewer, and built the Napa Valley Winery Map. Their site demonstrates how to present both intellectual depth and practical capability — tools, guides, client work, and open-source contributions all living together. Relevant because they occupy a thoughtful middle ground between art and engineering.
4. 0 — One of only four integrated illustration+interactivity providers you found. Sole operator (Senan) serving ski resorts, waterparks, and hospitals. His site is the closest existing business model to what you might build. Study both what works (testimonials, service breadth, InterMaps platform presentation) and what doesn't (the site has some design clutter). He shows how one person can credibly offer both illustration and a proprietary interactive platform.
5. 0 — John Potter, South Florida. Dominates the boat show illustrated map market with 15+ years of continuous client relationships. His site is portfolio-forward — you can see every version of every boat show map going back to 2010. Study how he presents domain depth and long-term client trust. The blog posts per project are effective: he explains the design thinking behind each map, not just the deliverable. Not the most polished design, but the business explanation through accumulated work is powerful.
6. 0 — The legendary ski trail map painter. His site is essentially a Shopify store selling prints and the Kickstarter-funded book, but the positioning is worth studying — he turned a sole-practitioner illustration career into a brand with cultural weight. The site's simplicity is itself a statement: the work speaks. Relevant for how to present mastery and heritage.
7. 0 — Boutique illustrated city maps and guides, London. "Fiction, reality, and the otherworldly." This is a product brand built entirely around illustrated maps as objects of desire. Beautiful, minimal e-commerce design. Study how they turn maps into a lifestyle brand with editorial voice. One step away from your domain but the brand presentation quality is high.
8. 0 — Vineyard/winery niche integrated maps with interactive app. Rachel LeRoy's site shows how a domain-specific integrated offering is marketed. The print+app dual delivery model is relevant to your thinking about bridging static and interactive. The site effectively communicates both the illustration side and the technology side through the winery lens. Design is clean if not extraordinary.
9. 0 — propMAPS, the pure interactivity layer for ski resorts. They explicitly accept someone else's map artwork and add the interactive overlay with real-time conditions. Their site is well-organized and demonstrates how the technology side presents itself — conditions reporting, digital signage, embeddable maps. Study this as the "other half" of the split you identified. Their blog post reviewing ski map artists is also revealing for market dynamics.
10. 0 — Enterprise interactive map platform (700+ higher education clients). Polished SaaS marketing site. Study their positioning, testimonial strategy, and how they segment by industry (higher ed, hospitality, venues, DMOs). This is what the technology side looks like at scale. The design is corporate-clean — instructive for what to aspire to in professionalism, but also what to differentiate from aesthetically.
11. 0 — Gary Milliken's VistaMap. All-vector ski maps where "the paint never dries." His site is sparse and dated, but the content is worth studying — his process page explains the vector methodology, and the portfolio shows 35+ ski resorts and 70+ parks/zoos. Relevant because his vector-native approach is the closest precedent to what AI-generated SVG maps might look like. The contrast between his excellent work and his basic website is itself a business observation.
12. 0 — Pentagram's data visualization portfolio (Giorgia Lupi's practice). One step away from maps specifically, but the website design quality for presenting data-driven creative work is world-class. Each project gets a story page with context, process, and impact. Study their project page format as a template for how to present integrated technical+artistic work.
13. 0 — "Medium for Maps." No-code interactive map builder with custom illustrated base layers. SaaS model starting at $100. Their site is clean, modern, and effectively communicates the self-serve proposition. Relevant as both a competitor (they sell accessible interactivity) and as a design reference for presenting map technology simply.
14. 0 — Specialist in hand-drawn agritourism maps (corn mazes, pumpkin patches, orchards). Study as an example of a solo illustrator who owns a narrow domain completely. The site shows how niche specificity creates credibility even with modest web design.
15. 0 — Axis Maps' typographic map prints. They built city maps composed entirely of text — every street, park, and water body rendered as its own name. Study this as an example of how a technical cartography studio monetizes beautiful artifacts alongside consulting work. The aesthetic is striking and the execution leverages the same skills used in their client work.
16. 0 — Resort maps, 3D renderings, virtual tours. Bridges illustration (leaning 3D render rather than hand-drawn) and interactivity. Serves hotels, resorts, golf courses. Less distinctive than the top entries but worth a quick look as another data point on how integrated providers present themselves.
17. 0 — Maria Rabinky, campus map illustrator who partners with CampusTours for interactivity. Relevant specifically because the partnership model (illustrator + separate interactive platform) is the current norm that an integrated business would disrupt.
18. 0 — Travel Graphics International. 40+ years in the hotel/tourism illustrated map business with ad-supported revenue. Worth visiting to see the oldest integrated model in the space — their approach predates the internet and evolved with it. The site may feel dated, but the business model documentation is instructive.
A few notes on what to watch for as you browse:
The illustration-side sites (Niehues, Milliken, Potter, Anna Simmons) generally let portfolio images carry the weight. The technology-side sites (Concept3D, propMAPS, Mapme) rely on feature lists, testimonials, and use-case segmentation. Stamen and Axis Maps are the rare exceptions that present both technical capability and aesthetic sensibility as equal partners — which is exactly the positioning problem you'll need to solve. The gap your survey identified (illustration and interactivity living in separate industries) is visible even in how these businesses present themselves online.
