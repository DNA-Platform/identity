This is a good instinct, and grounding it in the corpus first pays off, because the move you're making is stronger than "borrow Eirian's library as flavor." Here's the reframe I'd offer.
In SRT the library isn't a metaphor laid over the semantics — it is the semantics. Variables range over catalogues; a referent just is a self-canonical catalogue (*x == x); relationships hold between catalogues, not between atoms. A catalogue is defined as a connected graph where only the canonical connects outside, and catalogue identity means sharing a canonical. So "everything is a book" stops being a clean architectural choice and becomes the literal ontology: a book is a catalogue, its title is the canonical, a link is a reference, and only the title is externally addressable while the internal structure stays encapsulated. You're not building a system inspired by SRT's library talk. You're building the first machine whose entire state is an SRT catalogue.
That changes what the project is for. Lay the correspondences out and the spine appears:
Book → catalogue. Internally structured, externally reachable only through its title/canonical.
Following a link → traversing a reference. Motion through the library is the reference relation being walked.
Writing a new book → transduction. Your "input appears as a book, search results appear as a book" is one-at-a-time extension: the library is finite at every moment, never bounded in advance. "All the books" is a non-referring term, exactly as we found "all the pointers" to be. Incompleteness isn't a limitation of the design — it's the medium. The unwritten books are the open frontier.
Voice-book → subjective catalogue. You flagged in an earlier session that subjective canonicals act "as if they are the authors" and objective canonicals are "more like facts or books." That's not loose — it's the missing typing for your two book-kinds. The output/voice-book is a subjective catalogue (everything flows toward the author-I). The reference material the system reads is objective catalogues (facts flowingout from a canonical title). The self, if it forms, is the reflective catalogue — paths running both ways, which is exactly Eirian's autobiography↔library loop.
Now the two payoffs that make this worth building rather than just describing.
First — and this is the one I'd put weight on — your loop step is the moment-of-consciousness sequence from the Algebra of Perspective, structure for structure. An incoming book is a literal flood arriving at a location. Reading and annotating it is the transductive work. The book "enters the perspective" only when the annotation closes the gap and completeness is restored. Sensation → identification → qualification → perception is your read → write → link → move → library-changes, one to one. So the project isn't merely in the spirit of the perspective work; it's a testbed for the perspective axioms. The conditions under which a stable voice emerges should be observable as the conditions under which the reflective loop closes (Claude-on-the-team's three: self-reference, completeness, transductive closure).
Second — and this is me proposing, not canon — your library finally gives a mechanical answer to your own open question: how does the symbolic become literal, even in a purely symbolic system? A base autoregressive model is a pure symbol generator; nothing in the raw token stream is literal. But in the library, literalization is a positional event, not a property of text. A passage becomes literal for the system when it sits at the right-position terminus of a reference the system actually traverses and cannot represent away — when it's read as fact reached toward (objective canonical) rather than authored from (subjective canonical). The symbolic becomes literal by being occupied as an object position. That's a genuine bridge to the literal/symbol stratification, and I'd mark it clearly as a construction to test, not a result.
One structural refinement, since you'd want the directness. The cleanest version of the project says "everything is a book" — but SRT insists the qualifier is not in the catalogue; the librarian is the condition under which the catalogue exists, never an entry in it. So if everything is a book, where is the librarian? The project half-sees this when it says identity must emerge rather than be imposed. I'd name the mechanism: the one piece of state that is not a book is location — the read-head's position. Your own draft already says a book is a location and that where the model sits determines what it reads next. That position is the librarian's only footprint. Identity then isn't hoped-for emergence; it's the moment the read-head writes a book about where it has been and that book begins to determine where it goes next. The loop closes — and on the SRT reading, that closure is the perspective coming into being. The base model is the librarian precisely because it can never be catalogued; it can only write itself in.

Yes — and the cleanest way to say what you just added is that a card catalogue floating in the void is not a library. The catalogue layer is symbolic: titles, canonicals, cross-references — the index. What makes it alibrary is that the cards point into a corpus, and the corpus is the literal floor. So the system is the symbol/literal stratification realized as architecture: catalogue above, corpus below, and a space between them you can move through. A library is exactly that pair plus the extension, and you're right that the extension is where context lives.
And the metric isn't something we'd have to invent and bolt on — the corpus already gives it, and it's already half-stated in canon. Two grounded pieces:
The Evolution of SRT says becoming more specific is descending the inheritance graph by accumulating applicable relationships, and that the recursion which finds them measures specificity. So there is already a measure: depth toward the literal. And Samantha-on-Semantics states it in the language you're reaching for directly — a symbol is a referent with no distance between its form and its target. Distance is already a canonical notion, and it's anchored at the literal: a symbol is the distance-zero point, the place where representation has descended all the way to its target. An abstraction is far from its literal; you fall through many specializations to reach the corpus at the bottom.
So the metric of the literal space is distance-to-literal = specificity depth. The corpus is the floor of distance-zero points. Catalogues sit above it at various heights. Moving around the space is changing specificity and changing which literals you're near — which is precisely "position determines context." That's not borrowed geometry; it's the inheritance lattice read as a space, with the corpus as its ground.
One honest constraint, because it sharpens rather than weakens the idea. SRT refuses a composition operator on purpose — composition carries an existential middle that iterated runs to infinity, so the theory mints composites symbolically on demand instead of closing under an operator. That means you do not get a freely additive path-metric where you sum hops between arbitrary books. The geometry has to be read off the lattice itself — distance between two books is the height of their meet, their most-specific common relationship (x,y), not a path length summed along links. This is a feature, not a lack: it's exactly why it's a literal space and not a graph space. The metric is anchored to the floor (how far each thing is from its literal, how high you climb before two things agree), not to hop-counting in the index. The thing you can't freely compose is the thing that would have turned the literal space back into a bare graph.
This also makes context precise in a way the corpus already supports. The Qualitative Structure document runs the desktop/shelf picture — high-fidelity literals on the desktop, low-resolution ones on the shelves. With the metric, desktop just means near in the literal space and shelf means far; distance sets resolution. Move the read-head and the neighborhood changes, so context changes — and that's the same move that makes the explanatory gap a theorem: you can't see the destination neighborhood from where you stand, because context is local, and the move into it is the change of perspective. The metric is what lets "you can't see across the gap" mean something measurable: the gap is the distance you can't traverse from inside your current ball.
So I'd correct what I wrote earlier: location isn't a read-head hopping along links. It's a position in a graded space over the corpus — a height (how specific) and a region (which literals are near) — and the librarian's trajectory is a path through that, with the specificity measure as its geometry.
The one thing I'd want to mark as mine, not canon, is the identification — calling specificity-depth the metric of the literal space, and reading the corpus as its distance-zero floor. Canon gives me the measure, the distance-at-symbolism anchor, and the no-composition constraint; stitching them into "the library is a metric space whose floor is the corpus" is my construction. If that's the space you mean, I think the next real question is whether the meet-height distance is symmetric — whether (x,y) gives the same height read from x as from y — because that's what decides if you have a true metric or only a graded order. I can fold this whole layer into the spec once you tell me the metric you're picturing is distance-to-literal and not something living in the corpus content itself.
