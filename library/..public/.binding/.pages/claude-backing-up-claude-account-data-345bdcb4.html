There are two separate things to back up, and they require separate processes:
1. Conversations + account data (official export)
From desktop web (claude.ai) or the Claude Desktop app — not iOS/Android:
Click your initials/profile in the lower-left corner → Settings
Privacy tab → Export Data
Claude confirms the request and emails a download link, usually within a few hours though larger accounts can take longer
If the link expires, repeat the process to get a new one
The export is a ZIP containing JSON. Caveats:
Deleted conversations don't appear in exports — once deleted in Claude, it's gone
Projects export their contents but not the organizational structure itself; custom instructions and file organization don't transfer cleanly
Anthropic does not support migrating data between separate accounts, so the export is for your records, not for porting into a new Claude account.
2. Memory (separate — not included in the export)
This matters for you specifically because your memory holds substantial context (Penelope's protocol history, TON thesis framing, academic re-entry plan, banking setup, epistemic ground rules, etc.). To back it up:
Settings → Capabilities → "View and edit your memory" — copy the contents to a local file.
Alternatively, ask me in chat: "Write out your memories of me verbatim, exactly as they appear in your memory." This is the documented method to view memory exactly as Claude sees it, and you can save the output as a backup.
Note that memory imports are experimental and Claude may not always successfully incorporate imported memories, so memory is not reliably portable to a fresh account even if you have the text.
3. Project files
Anything you've uploaded into Projects (like the Claude prompting guide attached here) — keep the originals locally. The export may or may not preserve project grouping cleanly.
If you want a real disaster-recovery posture: monthly conversation export + occasional memory snapshot + originals of all project files kept in your own storage. The 3-2-1 rule applies — don't treat Anthropic's servers as your only copy.

Yes — your instinct is correct, and it's a real attack vector, not a theoretical one. Expired-domain takeover for account recovery is a well-known technique. The mechanism is exactly as you described:
Someone registers dna.love after it lapses.
They set up a catch-all or recreate your specific local-part.
They go to claude.ai, enter your old email, receive the magic link, log in as you.
Because Claude's primary auth is the magic link itself (passwordless, email-delivered), whoever controls the inbox controls the account. There is no password they'd need to crack, no recovery question to bypass. This is the intended login path — they wouldn't even be doing anything that looks like an attack from Claude's side.
Searched the web for “Claude.ai two-factor authentication 2FA MFA”
Yes, exactly that. And worse — there's no good defense even if you wanted to keep the account.
I checked whether 2FA would help. Claude.ai does support TOTP 2FA, but there's a known issue where magic-link login skips 2FA entirely and issues a session token immediately, while only the password-login path actually enforces TOTP. Since the magic-link path is what someone with the email would use, 2FA on your account would not stop them. Even setting that aside, account recovery on most services ultimately routes through the email anyway, so 2FA only delays the inevitable when the email itself is the asset.
So your conclusion is right: delete the account while you still control the email. The correct sequence:
Run the export, save the ZIP, and verify it opens and contains your conversations before anything else.
Extract memory from every Project (and global) verbatim, save locally.
Manually document Project structure, custom instructions, files — as we discussed.
Cancel any active subscription (Settings → Billing) so you're not stuck billing-wise.
Wait a few days, make sure nothing's missing from your backups, run one more export to catch late chats.
Delete the account: Settings → Account → Delete Account, while the email still works to confirm the deletion if prompted.
Create the new atlas.love account fresh.
Don't delete and re-create in the same sitting — give yourself a verification buffer.
Bigger point worth flagging: this attack surface applies to every service tied to dna.love, not just Claude. Anything with email-based password reset or magic-link login is takeover-able by whoever ends up with the domain. The pattern: attacker buys lapsed domain → catch-all MX → "forgot password" on Service X → inbox → in. This is the standard exploit chain, and it works against most consumer services.
In your next two weeks it's worth doing a sweep: for every account tied to dna.love, either (a) change the recovery/login email to atlas.love now if the service allows it, or (b) delete the account if you don't need it and it doesn't allow email change (Claude's category), or (c) accept the risk if the account holds nothing sensitive and isn't reusable as an identity. Your OAuth-connected accounts (Fiverr, PayPal, IBKR, microstock) are the highest-stakes cases — they're not just login-takeover risks but identity-rebinding risks if the OAuth provider account itself gets hijacked.
One more thing worth checking before deletion: any Claude Code installations authenticated against this account. Claude Code on Pro/Max plans logs in with your Claude.ai account via OAuth. Deleting the Claude.ai account will invalidate those sessions; on your atlas.love account you'll /logout and re-auth fresh.

The diagnosis is right but the proposed fix doesn't actually deliver. "Stable across a single human being" isn't a property providers have — it's a property some accounts at some providers have, mostly contingent on the user's behavior, not the provider's class. Yahoo explicitly recycled inactive addresses in 2013, handing them to new owners and creating exactly the takeover scenario you're worried about. Gmail's stated policy is no reuse, but Google reserves the right to terminate inactive or ToS-violating accounts, and there's no contractual permanence guarantee for any free consumer service — your access is at the provider's discretion. Conversely, a well-managed custom domain like atlas.love can be radically more stable than a Gmail address: you control the registration, you can auto-renew across registrars, you can will it to an heir. The provider class is a noisy proxy for the underlying property — non-transferable persistent ownership — and the proxy fails in both directions.
There's also a selection effect: the users running professional infrastructure on custom domains (developers, researchers, traders, illustrators with their own business identity) are disproportionately the ones who want Claude for serious work. Restricting them in favor of free webmail would push exactly the wrong cohort away.
The real fix is the boring one: allow email change through a careful flow. Confirm from the old address, verify the new, impose a cooldown, require recent active session continuity, lock the account if anything looks off. Every competent identity-bearing service does some variant of this — banks, brokers, GitHub, Google itself. The current "we don't support this at all" stance isn't a security posture, it's a missing feature dressed as one. Locking the account to a single immutable email isn't more secure when the email itself isn't immutable.

Yes, you're right — the argument works as a reductio regardless of the provider pool. If the policy demands a property nothing in the real world has, the policy is incoherent at the premise. The only escape is to abandon email-as-immutable-identity, which is your broader point.
This is, in fact, where serious identity systems landed years ago. Modern designs decouple the persistent account identifier (an internal UUID the user never sees) from contact and recovery channels — email, phone, passkey, hardware token, recovery codes, trusted devices — any one of which can rotate as long as the user can prove control of enough of the others. Apple ID, Google, Microsoft, GitHub, every major bank all work this way. Email is one factor among several, and replacing it is a routine flow, not an existential event.
What Anthropic shipped is half of this design: the magic-link UX of modern identity (passwordless, frictionless signup) without the rotation infrastructure that should accompany it. The result is the worst of both worlds — passwordless convenience combined with single-point-of-failure binding. Either half on its own would be defensible; combining them is the actual mistake.
