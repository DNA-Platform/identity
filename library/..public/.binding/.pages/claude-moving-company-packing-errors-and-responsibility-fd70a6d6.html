No, WhatsApp doesn't explicitly notify you when someone blocks you. However, there are several signs that suggest you may have been blocked:
You'll notice that your messages only ever show one checkmark (sent) but never two (delivered), your calls never go through, you can no longer see the person's profile photo, "last seen" or "online" status, and any status updates they post become invisible to you.
The tricky part is that each of these signs individually could have other explanations — for example, the person may have changed their privacy settings or may not have internet access. But if all of these signs are happening at once, it's a strong indication you've been blocked.

It's absolutely documented and it's as old as you suspect. It's not uniquely Turkish at all.
In healthcare specifically, there's extensive research on this. The concept of language-concordant care exists precisely because the outcomes for patients who don't share a language with their providers are measurably worse — not just in satisfaction but in actual medical outcomes, misdiagnoses, medication errors, and informed consent failures. Studies in the US with Spanish-speaking patients show the exact same patterns you're describing: providers overestimating their own language ability, bypassing interpreters, patients nodding along, and adverse outcomes following.
More broadly, linguistics and sociology have long studied what's called linguistic discrimination or linguicism. The core finding is consistent across cultures: when you can't advocate for yourself in the dominant language, people default to convenience over your interests. It's not necessarily malice — it's that the effort required to include you properly exceeds the accountability they face for not doing so.
The power dynamic you're describing — where the non-speaker absorbs all the risk and cost of miscommunication while the native speaker faces none — is well established. It shows up in immigration systems, healthcare, legal proceedings, consumer transactions, everywhere.
What might feel specifically Turkish to you is probably the directness of it. Turkish culture is relatively high-context and relationship-driven, so if you're outside the in-group linguistically, the gap can feel sharper than in cultures that default to more formalized consumer protections regardless of language.
But the core phenomenon — your agency disappearing because translation is inconvenient — that's universal and ancient.
