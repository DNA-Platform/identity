Nestor Makhno (1888–1934) was a Ukrainian anarchist revolutionary who led the Revolutionary Insurrectionary Army of Ukraine, commonly known as the Makhnovshchina or Black Army, during the Russian Civil War (1917–1921).
He was born to a poor peasant family in Huliaipole, in what is now southeastern Ukraine. Makhno became politically radicalized as a teenager and joined an anarchist group, which led to his arrest and imprisonment under the Tsarist regime. He was released after the February Revolution of 1917.
Makhno organized a peasant insurgent movement that at its peak controlled a large territory in southeastern Ukraine with a population of several million. His forces fought against multiple enemies: the Austro-Hungarian and German occupiers, the Ukrainian nationalists, the White Army, and ultimately the Bolsheviks—despite periods of tactical alliance with the Red Army against common foes.
The Makhnovist movement attempted to implement anarchist-communist principles, including collective farming, workers' self-management, and opposition to centralized state authority. After the Bolsheviks consolidated power and turned against him in 1921, Makhno was forced into exile. He spent his final years in Paris, working as a carpenter and struggling with poverty and illness until his death from tuberculosis in 1934.
He remains a significant but contested figure—celebrated in anarchist circles and increasingly in Ukrainian historical memory as a symbol of resistance.

Quite the opposite—Makhno was a committed anarcho-communist who rejected the very concept of the state. His vision was fundamentally anti-statist.
His ideology centered on several core principles:
Free soviets: He supported local councils (soviets) but only as voluntary, bottom-up assemblies—not as instruments of a centralized government. He opposed the Bolshevik model where soviets became subordinate to party control.
Voluntary confederation: Instead of a state, communities would freely associate and coordinate through congresses of peasants, workers, and insurgents. Participation was voluntary, not coerced.
Collective ownership: Land and means of production would be held in common by those who worked them. He organized agricultural communes in the territory he controlled, though peasants could also farm individually if they didn't exploit others' labor.
Opposition to all authority: He rejected not just the Tsarist state but also the Bolshevik state, nationalist movements seeking to build a Ukrainian state, and any form of imposed hierarchy—political parties, police, standing armies controlled by governments.
This is precisely why the Bolsheviks eventually crushed him. Lenin and Trotsky could tolerate Makhno as a temporary military ally against the Whites, but his movement represented an existential ideological threat—proof that revolution could organize itself without a vanguard party or state apparatus. That example couldn't be allowed to persist.

You're thinking of "stateless" — a stateless society.
The parallels to crypto-anarchist ideals are actually striking:
Decentralization: Makhno wanted power distributed across voluntary local assemblies with no central authority. Crypto enthusiasts envision blockchain networks where no single entity controls the system—transactions validated by distributed consensus rather than banks or governments.
Disintermediation: Makhno rejected intermediaries like the state, party officials, or landlords standing between people and their labor/resources. Crypto seeks to eliminate middlemen—banks, payment processors, platforms that extract rent from transactions.
Voluntary association: In Makhno's vision, you'd participate in communes and confederations by choice, not compulsion. DAOs (decentralized autonomous organizations) and crypto communities theoretically operate on similar opt-in principles.
Trustless systems: Makhno's communes relied on direct accountability within small communities. Blockchain creates "trustless" systems where you don't need to trust institutions—the code and cryptographic verification replace institutional authority.
Resistance to seizure: Makhno fought against states confiscating peasant grain and labor. Crypto appeals to those who want assets that governments can't easily freeze or confiscate.
The irony is that both visions struggle with similar problems: how do you handle disputes, prevent accumulation of power by bad actors, and coordinate at scale without some form of governance emerging? Makhno's experiment was crushed militarily before those tensions fully played out.
