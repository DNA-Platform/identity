Your history from past chats: a lateral calcaneal osteotomy for flatfoot around January. The screws backed out and were removed around mid-July. The wound then got infected, was debrided, and you were non-weight-bearing for a while before walking again about 3 weeks after the removal. In August we laid out three timelines. The osteotomy was already united. The screw tract would consolidate about 6–12 weeks after removal, so roughly now. The soft tissue and nerve changes run 12–18 months from the first surgery.
What healthy overload usually feels like at this stage: a diffuse, dull ache in the heel or arch after a long day. It's worse at first steps after rest or in the morning, then loosens as you walk. It eases overnight or with a day of lighter activity, and it rises and falls with how much you walked the day before. That pattern is soft tissue adapting, not damage. The heel fat pad and plantar fascia spent months in a boot and shoes, and they now carry a new load path after the correction.
Why barefoot specifically: shoes absorb most of the heel-strike impact. Your fat pad and plantar tissues are deconditioned and are being asked to take that impact directly, right when your walking volume went up. Pain only when barefoot, not in shoes, fits that picture well. It points to a cushioning and capacity problem more than a bone problem.
What would suggest you've crossed into stressing the bone:
Pain at one spot you can point to with a fingertip, rather than diffuse
Pain when you squeeze the heel from both sides (inside and outside) with your hand
Pain that builds the longer you walk instead of warming up and easing
Ache at rest or at night
Given your infection history: deep pain with warmth, swelling, or redness over the old incision, especially if it's getting worse. That warrants an X-ray and a call to the surgeon rather than waiting.
If it's the first pattern, the usual approach is to keep shoes on, including at home, for a few weeks. Hold your walking volume steady or trim it a little rather than adding more. Reintroduce barefoot time in short stretches. Where is the pain: under the heel, the inner side, or near the lateral scar? That would narrow it down.

Yes, very likely it gets better. Plantar heel pain at this stage usually fades over weeks to a few months as the tissue reconditions. A small minority of people have lingering heel sensitivity after calcaneal surgery, so it isn't guaranteed. But nothing you're describing points that way.
Your read on the arch supports is right. They move load off the heel pad and onto the arch, so walking feels better. The cost is that the heel pad and plantar fascia get even less of the loading they need to toughen up. When you go barefoot, they're suddenly taking full impact with less capacity than before. It's like resting a sore muscle and finding it feels worse the first time you use it again. The fix isn't to give up the supports. It's to add barefoot time deliberately in small doses: a few minutes at a time on a softer floor, building up over a couple of weeks, while keeping the supports for longer walks.
A setback of this size after the removal is plausible, and it's more than the removal alone. Three things stacked up:
The removal itself. Large screws through the calcaneus leave sizable tracts, and they're only now finishing consolidation.
The infection. It added the non-weight-bearing period, which deconditioned the heel pad again just as it was adapting.
Where the screws went in. Screws for this osteotomy usually enter from the back-bottom of the heel, so the entry sites and tracts can sit close to where you strike the ground. Your surgeon would know exactly where yours were.
So "under the heel" fits both soft-tissue reconditioning and the tract area being tender. Either way, it should fade over the next couple of months.
Two things would change the picture and justify an X-ray:
Pain at one spot you can press with a fingertip, or pain when you squeeze the heel from both sides
Pain that's getting worse week to week, or aching at rest or at night
If instead it's a broad soreness that's worst on first steps and stable or slowly improving, that's the reconditioning pattern.

A heel scar is in a high-pressure spot, and scar tissue stays sensitive to load and pressure for months while it remodels and softens, typically over 6 to 12 months. So the realistic expectation is gradual improvement over the next few months. It won't be linear. It will tend to flare after days with more walking or barefoot time and settle again. Things that tend to help a sensitive heel scar:
Gentle scar massage once it's fully closed, a few minutes a day with lotion.

A cushioned heel cup if barefoot is unavoidable, for example in the shower.

Most likely this is compensation, not your original problem coming back. The timing points that way: it started right after you began keeping weight off your heel. To do that, people shift load forward and change how they walk, often gripping with the toes or walking on the forefoot or outer edge. That puts unusual stress on the top of the midfoot and the tendons running over it, and a new ache there within days or weeks is a classic result.
Recurrence is less likely for a structural reason. The osteotomy permanently moved your heel bone, and the bone is now fully healed in its new position. So the correction can't drift back the way an untreated flat foot gradually collapses. Some soft-tissue sag is possible, but not a return to the old geometry.
Signs it's compensation:
It started with the change in how you walk.
It's sore along the top of the foot rather than deep in one joint.
It's worse after long periods of altered walking.
It eases when you walk normally in your arch supports.
Signs it may be the old joint pain:
It sits in exactly the same spot and feels the same as before surgery.
It's a deep ache at one joint, sometimes with swelling.
It's worse on uneven ground or stairs.
You can also check the arch yourself:
Stand barefoot and compare the arches of both feet.
Try a single-leg heel raise on the operated foot. The heel should swing inward as you rise.
If the arch has visibly flattened, or the heel doesn't swing inward, that's worth a standing X-ray and a surgeon visit.
The practical step is probably the opposite of what you've been doing. Rather than avoiding the heel, walk normally in your supports and let the heel take its share of load in moderate amounts. If the top-of-foot pain fades within a week or two of that, it was compensation.
Is it on the inner side (toward the big toe) or the outer side of the top of the foot? And is it the same spot as the pain before surgery?

The outer top of the foot fits compensation even better, and there are two reasons it would show up now:
Keeping weight off the heel usually means rolling onto the outer edge of the foot.
Arch supports push load toward the outer side by design.
You've increased both recently, and together they load the outer column of the foot (the cuboid and the base of the fifth metatarsal), which isn't used to it.
There's one overlap to sort out. Pre-surgery flatfoot pain on the outer side is often impingement at the sinus tarsi, a soft hollow just in front of and below the outer ankle bone. The original osteotomy is designed to relieve exactly that. Pressing on a few spots can help tell them apart:
Sinus tarsi (the soft hollow in front of the ankle bone): deep pain there that feels like your old pain would suggest the joint.
Further forward along the outer edge (midfoot, toward the bump at the base of the little toe's bone): that's lateral-column overload from how you're walking.
Burning, tingling, or zinging when you tap near the scar: that's the sural nerve, which was already sensitive. Scar tissue around it can flare when you put new pressure or tension on the area.
Your sense that the old pain was worse is meaningful. Pain from a collapsing foot tends to build steadily with activity. Compensation pain is milder and tracks how you've been walking.
The practical test is the same as before: walk as evenly as you can, letting the heel take its share, and see whether this fades over a week or two. If it keeps building, or turns out to be in the sinus tarsi spot and feels like your old pain, that's the point for a standing X-ray and a visit to your surgeon.
