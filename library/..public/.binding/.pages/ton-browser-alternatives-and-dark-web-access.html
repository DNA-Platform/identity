Short answer: no. There is no Tor-Browser-equivalent for TON — no polished, dedicated client that packages the darknet layer the way Tor Browser packages onion routing. What exists is a set of access shims onto TON Proxy:
MyTonWallet's browser extension — supports TON Proxy directly, connecting to TON Sites without third-party services. This is the most "reputable" option, and notably it's Zinchuk's product — the same engine underneath Gram Wallet.
Public HTTPS gateways — ton.run or tonp.io let you browse TON Sites with no setup, but route your traffic through a third party and aren't recommended for regular use. These are the antithesis of the anonymity design — a clearnet peephole.
Manual proxy config — pointing Chrome/Firefox at a TON entry proxy, which is the raw rldp-http-proxy mechanism from Nikolai's spec.
The architectural irony is that the anonymizer layer you'd need for the true dark-web-2.0 reading — TON Proxy operating in garlic-routing mode, the sec. 3.1.6 material — is specified but not what any of these tools actually do. The current proxy is an HTTP-to-ADNL bridge where the server IP is published to the DHT and simply not exposed at the HTTP layer — location-hiding for servers in a weak sense, but not multi-hop anonymity for clients. The eep-service architecture exists on paper; the shipped tooling is closer to an alternative DNS root than an I2P.
And you're right about the discovery layer: there's no dark.fail equivalent because there's essentially no dark ecosystem to index. TON Sites in practice host wallets, casinos, and Foundation pages — the directory function, such as it is, lives in Telegram channels, which is itself consistent with the pattern: Telegram is the front-end for everything, and the anonymizer layer stays dormant plumbing until someone — presumably the same someone — decides to activate it.

This corrects what I told you yesterday — Tonnet Browser is much closer to the Tor-Browser analogue than anything I surfaced then. Worth registering what it actually is:
It activates the dormant layer. It's a native desktop browser for TON that browses .ton and .t.me sites directly via P2P with 3-hop garlic routing, built-in TON Storage, 15-vector anti-fingerprinting, and first-party isolation — and per your screenshot, v2.0 migrated to a native ADNL tunnel protocol with tunnel nodes discovered via DHT, explicitly crediting Tor as inspiration and using adnl-tunnel as the garlic routing relay for anonymous browsing. So this is the first shipped client that actually implements the sec. 3.1.6 architecture — multi-hop garlic routing over ADNL — rather than the flat HTTP-to-ADNL bridge everything else uses. The spec sat dormant since 2019; a third party finally built the client on top of it. It's under active development too — the site now serves v2.4.0 across Windows, macOS, Linux, and an Android APK.
Who's behind it matters for your frame. It's the Resistance Dog collective — and the Resistance Dog is Pavel's own symbol, the hooded dog from the 2018 VK/Telegram "Digital Resistance" era, later minted as Telegram-blessed NFTs. Formally independent, ideologically inner-orbit — the Zinchuk pattern again. Their toolkit is a whole parallel-internet stack: a decentralized GitHub alternative on TON, a bag indexer on TON Storage, ZK privacy pools with on-chain Groth16 proofs, COCOON inference clients, HTTP 402 micropayments, and a CLI agent that drives Tonnet headlessly. The x402 piece plugs payments directly into the browsing layer — the payment-per-request economics from the whitepaper's proxy-incentive design. Caveat from their own release notes, which you already saw: no external audit.
On the index question — you're right, there isn't one. No dark.fail equivalent exists because the content layer is still nearly empty. What passes for discovery: ton.run's gateway carries a site list with a we-don't-endorse-anything disclaimer, Resistance Dog runs a TON Storage search/discovery tool and a decentralized bag indexer, and otherwise the directory function lives in Telegram channels. The population of actual .ton sites remains mostly manifestos, ecosystem mirrors, casinos, and the Resistance tools pointing at each other — a self-referential ring, infrastructure awaiting inhabitants.
And no — Fragment isn't the use case. Fragment is an ordinary clearnet site (fragment.com); nobody needs Tonnet to reach it. There's no dominant "thing people visit" through this browser yet. Its user base is a small privacy/ideology cohort, and its current function is closer to proof-of-existence: demonstrating that the darknet mode of the stack runs, before there's anything on it. Which fits your sequencing — the plumbing ships and works while the spotlight is elsewhere; the content and the users are the part that hasn't been switched on.

The use case just crystallized, and the timing is telling. Note the .t.me support — Tonnet doesn't just open .ton sites, it runs a local TON proxy that resolves .ton and .t.me sites natively, connecting directly to the TON network. That means Telegram's own web content reachable with zero dependence on conventional DNS. And DNS is precisely where Telegram just got hit: on July 13, 2026, the .me registry pulled t.me entirely from the global DNS zone (serverHold status), breaking short links in browsers worldwide, likely over OFAC sanctions-compliance — possibly one sanctioned entity's channel link triggering blockage of the whole domain. Meanwhile inside Russia, Telegram has been throttled since late 2025, with brands migrating to the state messenger Max. So in a single month Telegram's addressing layer was attacked at the registry level by Western sanctions machinery and degraded at the network level by Russia — and the only routing layer immune to both is the one specified in the 2019 whitepaper. That's your "not barking into the dark": the darknet layer's first real constituency is Telegram's own reachability. Also worth noting: t.me pulled July 13, wallet announcement July 21, Rosfinmonitoring July 29 — a dense three-week corridor.
For getting started, it's genuinely noob-friendly now — much closer to Tor Browser's "download and go" than the old proxy-config gymnastics:
Download from tonnet.resistance.dog/download or the GitHub releases page (TONresistor/Tonnet-Browser, currently v2.5.0). Windows gets an installer or portable .exe; macOS a universal .dmg for Intel and Apple Silicon; Linux AppImage or .deb; Android is a sideloaded APK (v1.0, with a v1.1 beta). On Mac you may need xattr -cr to clear Gatekeeper since it's unsigned.
Launch — no config. It connects to TON on its own; the FAQ answer to "how is this different from a gateway" is the whole pitch: a gateway owner sees everything you do and can be blocked or go offline; Tonnet connects on its own with nobody in the middle.
Choose your mode. Direct P2P, or private: three layers of encryption through three relays, none knowing both who you are and where you're going, with the route rotating every 10 minutes. Tor's circuit model, verbatim, on ADNL.
First stops: the built-in start page, then foundation.ton, the resistance.dog tool ring, their TON Storage search for content discovery, and any .t.me channel — including Pavel's — resolved over TON instead of DNS.
Wallet is already in it: non-custodial, with HTTP 402 payments and per-site spending limits — pay-per-request browsing wired in from the start.
Their Telegram channels (@TonnetBrowser, @ResistanceTools, @ResistanceForum) are the living documentation — which you're better positioned to read directly than I am to fetch. The one honest caveat is theirs, from your screenshot: open source, no independent audit. For reconnaissance browsing that's acceptable; I'd treat the built-in wallet as a toy until audited.

Note the cadence, too: v2.1 through v2.4 all shipped between June 20 and July 11, i.e., the development tempo accelerated right into the t.me/wallet corridor.
For Windows, two paths:
Portable (recommended for playing around): Download TON-Browser-Portable-2.5.0.exe from either tonnet.resistance.dog/download or github.com/TONresistor/Tonnet-Browser/releases. No installation, no registry writes — it just runs, and deleting the file removes it. Ideal for a first look.
Installer: TON-Browser-Setup-2.5.0.exe from the same places. Or the one-liner from their site, in PowerShell:
powershell
irm https://github.com/TONresistor/Tonnet-Browser/releases/download/v2.5.0/TON-Browser-Setup-2.5.0.exe -OutFile TonBrowser.exe; .\TonBrowser.exe
Expect two friction points, both because the binary is unsigned: your browser may flag the download ("Keep" it), and SmartScreen will likely throw "Windows protected your PC" on first run — More info → Run anyway. Given no external audit, downloading from the GitHub releases page specifically (not a mirror) is the right hygiene, and if you want to be careful, the portable build sandboxes naturally.
Once it opens: it connects to TON on launch — check the status bar for "Connected to TON Network" and the routing mode. Things to poke at first session:
Toggle between direct and garlic-routed (3-hop) mode in settings and feel the latency difference — that's the Tor-style tradeoff live
ton://dns — the built-in DNS lookup page
foundation.ton, then your own Fragment username as a resolution test
A .t.me address — Pavel's channel resolved over ADNL instead of DNS is the whole Russia thesis in one address bar
The TON Storage tab — try seeding/downloading a bag
Everything is wiped on exit by default (Tor Browser behavior), so don't be surprised when history is gone next launch. And I'd leave the built-in wallet empty for now — browse with it, don't fund it.
