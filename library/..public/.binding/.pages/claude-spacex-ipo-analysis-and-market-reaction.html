The bull case

That's the part even skeptics concede — Starlink is a genuinely dominant, profitable franchise. The disagreement is entirely about what multiple the future layers deserve.

The Tesla parallel — and what it actually teaches
This is the most useful frame you could have picked, because Tesla is the same operator, the same cult-of-personality dynamic, and a completed cycle you can study end-to-end.
The arc: Tesla ran from $84 to a $1,229 peak in November 2021 (split-adjusted ~$410), an ~8x move. Then it fell roughly 73% by December 2022, driven by supply-chain problems, weak demand amid rising rates, and the perception that Musk was distracted after buying Twitter.
Three structural lessons transfer directly to SPCX:
First, Tesla has essentially never been valued on current earnings. Its 10-year mean trailing P/E is ~122x, with a high of ~1,690x at the 2021 meme peak. SpaceX is debuting at a 60–73x price-to-sales multiple with negative earnings — so you're buying the same "valued on a story, not a cash flow" structure Tesla pioneered. The story can run far longer than fundamentals justify, in both directions.
Second, what broke Tesla wasn't valuation — it was a catalyst. Bears called Tesla a bubble continuously from early 2020 onward, and they were "right" about valuation the entire ride up while being financially destroyed. The actual top coincided with rate hikes plus the Twitter distraction. The valuation was the fuel; the catalyst was the spark. For SPCX the analogous sparks to watch are the xAI cash-burn story turning, a Starship setback, or — the Tesla rhyme — Musk attention getting pulled elsewhere.
Third — and this is the part the bears and bulls both have to sit with — Tesla rebounded from each of its three prior 50%+ drawdowns, returning an average of 446% in the 12 months following those bottoms. So "it's overvalued and will crash" and "it's a generational compounder" were both true about Tesla, just on different time horizons. A 70% drawdown that fully recovers is a very different experience depending on whether you're levered, shorting, or simply holding.
Was there any way to know when it would pop?
Honest answer: not reliably, and the Tesla record is the proof. Look at the dated calls in the record:
One technical analyst declared "the bubble is about to burst" on January 27, 2021 — Tesla then rose for another ten months before topping.
Another laid out a six-month short thesis noting "timing tops is difficult business," expecting a drop toward $400 — directionally right eventually, but the timing was a coin flip dressed up in indicators.
The pattern is brutal and consistent: people correctly identified the overvaluation very early and were wrong about timing by enormous margins. As the bear-case analyst himself conceded about Tesla, it's impossible to know whether a decline has reached bottom — and the same uncertainty applies symmetrically to tops.
There's a deep reason for this, and it's the most important thing to internalize: valuation tells you aboutmagnitude and direction of an eventual move, but carries almost no information about timing. A stock 3x overvalued can become 6x overvalued — that's a 100% loss for a short and a doubling for a holder, from the exact same starting "the bubble is obvious" observation. The popping is triggered by catalysts (rates, a delivery miss, a distraction, a liquidity event like a lockup expiry), and catalysts are close to unforecastable in their timing.
The one semi-knowable structural date here, worth flagging because it's mechanical rather than predictive: the 180-day lock-up expiry, after which insider selling could pressure shares. That's roughly mid-December 2026. It's not a guarantee of a top, but it's a real supply event on a known date — the closest thing to a "schedulable" risk, versus the unschedulable catalysts.
On your point about not loving short selling
Your instinct is well-supported by exactly this history, and I'd gently formalize why rather than just validate it. Short selling a narrative stock has a structurally ugly payoff shape: your upside is capped at 100% (the stock goes to zero), your downside is unbounded (it can 3x against you), and — the killer — you can be completely correct on the thesis and still get wiped out on timing before you're vindicated. Every Tesla bear quoted above was analytically right and most were financially destroyed. Keynes's old line ("the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent") is precisely a statement about that timing asymmetry. And note Eisman — the man who built his career on a famous short — choosing not to short this. When the archetypal short-seller passes, that's information.
The cleaner expressions of a bearish-or-skeptical view, if you ever hold one, are the ones without the unbounded-loss-plus-timing-trap: simply not owning it, sizing a position small, or using defined-risk structures where the most you can lose is what you put in. None of that requires betting when.
The thing I'd leave you with: the Tesla case shows the genuinely hard part isn't spotting that something is expensive — that's the easy, early, and nearly useless observation. The hard part is that "expensive" and "great long-term investment" are not mutually exclusive, and neither valuation nor charts will tell you which timeline you're living in.
Usual caveat — I'm not a financial advisor and this isn't a recommendation, just the lay of the land and what the Tesla analog actually demonstrates.

Can Starlink alone carry it? No — and that's the whole point
You asked the exact right question: is Starlink enough to power a trillion-dollar revenue demand? The arithmetic says clearly no, on its own.
Even the bullish independent models top out around 23 million subscribers by 2035, growing revenue ~27% annually. The most credible bottom-up shop, Quilty Space, has Starlink doing maybe ~$15–17B near-term across consumer, maritime, aviation, enterprise and Starshield. Push that out a decade at strong growth and you get a business doing perhaps $40–60B in revenue with software-like margins. That's a fantastic company — comparable to a major telecom or a mid-tier hyperscaler segment. It is not, by itself, a $2.5 trillion company. A 25x sales multiple on $50B of mature ISP revenue is ~$1.25T, and ISPs don't trade at 25x.
So the valuation has a structural requirement baked in: at least one of the speculative layers has to become real, or the multiple has to compress hard. The honest framing is that today's price is roughly "Starlink (real) + Starship-as-launch-monopoly (plausible) + a free call option on orbital compute and Mars (speculative)" — and the call option is doing alot of the lifting. Wedbush said this almost verbatim: the valuation is heavily driven by an "orbital intelligence" narrative around integrating Grok into Starlink for on-orbit edge computing.
The lag you intuited — you're right, and it's the central risk
Your instinct about a lag is exactly correct, and it's the most underappreciated point. Orbital data centers aren't a 2027 revenue line; they're a next-decade bet gated on something that hasn't happened yet. The whole thing reduces to one variable: launch cost per kilogram. The analysis is blunt about it — if Starship achieves its target economics, orbital data centers reach cost parity for specific workloads in the 2028–2030 window; if Starship costs stay above $500/kg, terrestrial infrastructure keeps a decisive advantage through at least 2035. Falcon 9 today is around $2,500/kg; Musk's hope rests on Starship driving that to under $500, possibly $200.
And Starship isn't there. SpaceX has spent over $15 billion on Starship, flew its twelfth test on 22 May 2026, and managed only five Starship missions in 2025 against a target of 25. So the keystone of the orbital-compute thesis — cheap, high-cadence Starship — is running at one-fifth of its own planned rate. Every slip there pushes the speculative revenue further right. That's your lag, quantified: the cash burn isnow, the payoff is 2030+ and conditional.
"Their own 5G-like network?" — sort of, and there are two separate versions
You're onto something, but it's two different things:
Direct-to-cell is the literal 5G play. Starlink already bought $17 billion of EchoStar spectrum in 2025, enabling direct-to-cell service that integrates with smartphones for global coverage, potentially unlocking $20–150 billion in annual revenue by 2030. That's the genuine "become a global wireless carrier" option, and it's far more concrete than orbital compute. If you're looking for the layer that could plausibly add a telecom-sized revenue stream, it's this, not the data centers.
Orbital compute is more like "rent out a utility in space." The model isn't a network you connect phones to — it's a constellation of solar-powered satellites with chips on them, selling compute. And critically, SpaceX's strongest near-term role is as the *launcher*, not the *operator*. Google's Project Suncatcher — the most advanced version — is 81 satellites carrying Google's own TPUs, built by Planet Labs, with SpaceX merely in talks to provide launch services. SpaceX's role appears to center on launch, not operation of the system. So in the most likely version, SpaceX is the railroad, not the destination — which is a perfectly good business (everyone's railroad) but a much smaller margin pool than "we own the orbital cloud."
How close is Musk, actually?
Honest answer: it's a filing aspiration, not a roadmap. The S-1 language is striking in its hand-waving scale — "massive AI compute satellite constellations — with potentially millions of satellites — for orbital data centers," with first launch as early as 2028. So: real engineering interest, genuine physics advantages (free solar, free cooling), and a brutal economic gate that doesn't open until Starship works. Nobody is close to revenue — they're close to prototypes.
Now your Musk thesis — this is the good part
Your read — "Elon achieves a lot, but achieves little relative to the big vision; he pushes a project just far enough that people stop getting excited, then stops" — is genuinely sharp, and I want to both strengthen it and push on it, because I think it's half right in a way that matters for the valuation.
The Tesla evidence partly supports you. Tesla unambiguously made EVs desirable and built enough Supercharger infrastructure to make ownership functional. But "did it dent gasoline usage?" — globally, modestly and slowly. EVs are a meaningful and rising share of new car sales in some markets, but the installed fleet turns over over ~15–20 years, and global gasoline demand kept growing for most of the 2010s. The honest version of your point: Tesla's civilizational impact (decarbonizing transport) massively lags its cultural and financial impact (making electric cool, minting a trillion-dollar market cap). The car-as-desirable-object thesis explains Tesla's success better than the gasoline-displacement thesis does. I think you're right about that.
But here's where I'd push, because I think it sharpens your own argument rather than refuting it. The pattern isn't "pushes far enough that excitement fades, then stops." It's closer to: Musk reliably ships the part that has a tight commercial feedback loop, and chronically under-delivers the part that only pays off on a civilizational timescale. Tesla: cars and Superchargers shipped (people pay monthly-ish, feedback is immediate); "accelerate the transition to sustainable energy" is the part that lagged. SpaceX: Falcon 9 reusability and Starlink shipped (customers, recurring revenue, tight loop); Mars is the part that's perpetually ~10 years out. Starlink itself is the cleanest example — it's the boring, commercial, recurring-revenue spinoff of the grand vision, and it's the only thing reliably making money.
This is why I think your conclusion lands somewhere specific and a little uncomfortable for both bulls and bears:
If your Musk thesis is right, the bull case and the bear case partially collapse into the same prediction. The "achieves a lot, achieves little relative to the vision" pattern predicts: Starlink and launch will be real, large, profitable businesses (the tight-loop layer ships). Direct-to-cell probably ships too — it has a billing relationship attached. Mars and "millions of satellites of orbital compute" stay perpetually near-future and never carry the P&L. In that world, SpaceX in 2032 is maybe a $40–70B revenue company with great margins and a launch monopoly — an extraordinary business worth, say, $400B–$900B — and the current $2.5T is pricing the layers your own thesis says Musk characteristically doesn't finish.
So your "bull case" is really a fair-value-far-below-here case dressed in admiration. And I think it's internally coherent in a way the pure bears (who dismiss the whole thing) and pure bulls (who price Mars today) both miss. The one place I'd hold the door open: you only need the orbital/direct-to-cell option to go from "zero in the model" to "small but real" for a $1T+ valuation to be defensible, and the EchoStar spectrum purchase is a real asset, not a tweet. That's the thread that could make even your skeptical frame undershoot.
The thing I'd leave you with as the testable version of your own thesis: watch direct-to-cell, not Mars. It's the layer that has a billing relationship, which by your own pattern is the layer Musk actually finishes. If that stalls too, your thesis is fully confirmed and the valuation is a fantasy. Tesla at peak vs. SpaceX now
Tesla peaked around early November 2021 at roughly $1.2–1.24 trillion market cap. So to your direct question: yes, it was in the trillion-dollar tier — but it was the new entrant sitting well below Apple (~$2.5T), Microsoft (~$2.3T), Alphabet (~$2T), and Amazon (~$1.7T). It was the smallest and by far the most stretched member of the club.
But here's the comparison that matters, because it's the multiple, not the headline number. Tesla in FY2021 did $5.64 billion in net income on roughly $53.8B revenue (auto sales of $44.1 billion plus regulatory credits, leasing, and energy). At a ~$1.2T peak that's:
Tesla peak: ~22x sales, ~210x earnings, and profitable.
SpaceX now: ~130x sales, no earnings, losing ~$5B.
So peak-bubble Tesla — the thing people pointed at for two years as the definitional overvalued stock — wassix times cheaper on revenue than SpaceX is today, and it was actually making money. SpaceX is not "Tesla-2021 expensive." It's meaningfully more extreme than the most cited bubble of the last decade. Your "the valuation is insane" instinct isn't hyperbole; on a multiples basis it's the most aggressive large-cap debut I can point to.
And your other observation — "those companies have gotten bigger" — is the live question underneath all of it. The bull-case resolution to peak-Tesla wasn't that the multiple was justified in 2021; it's that Teslagrew into it (revenue roughly doubled, earnings grew) while the multiple compressed. The stock still fell 70% first. So even the optimistic path for SpaceX is "the business eventually justifies a fraction of this, after a brutal repricing." That's the most you can say charitably, and it requires the speculative layers to deliver.
Is Starlink really a "major telecom"? Is the demand there?
Your skepticism is well-placed, and I'd refine the claim rather than defend it wholesale. Starlink is not going to take share from Verizon in Manhattan — the unit economics are wrong for dense markets. Its demand is real but concentrated in coverage gaps and mobility, which is a different and smaller TAM than "global telecom":
Rural/unserved broadband (the original market — finite, being served)
Maritime and aviation (high-ARPU, genuinely underserved, real demand — maritime ~$1.94B and aviation a peak install year in 2026)
Government/defense via Starshield (~$3.2B in 2026 — sticky, high-margin, but politically/contract dependent)
Emerging markets (huge user counts, but ARPU as low as $10–30/month, so revenue lags subscribers)
The tell that demand is not infinite: average revenue per subscriber fell 18% to $81/month between 2023 and 2025 even as the base quadrupled — that's the signature of trading price for volume, i.e., pushing into progressively less lucrative demand. They've started raising prices in May 2026, which is the move you make when subscriber growth is maturing and you pivot to monetizing the installed base. Quilty, the most rigorous independent shop, models ~16.8M subscribers by end-2026 — strong, but a long way from the 100M+ you'd need for "major global telecom."
The one place the "major telecom" framing could become literally true is direct-to-cell, riding the $17B EchoStar spectrum, with estimates of $20–150B annual revenue by 2030. Note the width of that band — $20B to $150B is not an estimate, it's a shrug. But the structure is real: a satellite layer that eliminates dead zones, sold wholesale through carriers or direct. That's the version where Starlink stops being "premium rural ISP" and becomes telecom infrastructure. It's also, per your own Musk thesis, the layer with a billing relationship — the kind he tends to actually finish.
Bottom line on your question: Starlink alone, even bullishly, gets you to a great ~$40–60B revenue business over a decade — a real company, not a $2.5T one. The demand for "Starlink as you currently understand it" isnot enough. The valuation needs direct-to-cell to become telecom-scale and the orbital-compute option to print. Two speculative things, not one.
