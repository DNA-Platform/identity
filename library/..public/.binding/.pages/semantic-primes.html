### 5.1.2. Semantic Primitives Wierzbicka (1972) believed that it is possible to describe every human language by using a limited number of universal semantic primitives. Decision-making that runs on concrete concepts can be completed through the upper-level abstract concepts that contain those concrete concepts. The first set had 14 elements: I, YOU, SOMEONE, SOMETHING/THING, WORLD, THIS, WANT, DON'T WANT (which Wierzbicka originally coined as "diswant"), FEEL, THINK (OF), IMAGINE, SAY, BECOME, and BE A PART (OF).
Two details I'd flag, since they bear on how you think about reference:
The count is reported as both 13 and 14 in the literature — including by Wierzbicka herself in different papers. The instability comes almost entirely from whether WANT and DON'T WANT are one prime or two. She treated them as separate in 1972, the field collapsed them in the 1990s, and DON'T WANT was formally restored as distinct only in 2014. That's a nice example of a primitive's status being a live, revisable claim rather than a fixed fact — the inventory has a history of supersession, not just accretion.
The casualties are also telling: IMAGINE, BECOME, and WORLD didn't survive cross-linguistic testing. WORLD got absorbed by the spatial primes (WHERE~PLACE, HERE). Eleven of the fourteen are still in the modern 65, which Wierzbicka reads as vindication of the early intuitions.

Here's the thing worth flagging first, because it reframes the question: NSM has no production rule in the generative sense. There's no S → NP VP–style derivation that outputs "I need to buy an aardvark as a pet" from the primes. Wierzbicka built NSM explicitly against that picture — her early fight was with Chomsky's autonomous syntax, the idea that you generate surface strings from unobservable underlying structures. So if you go looking for a rewrite procedure that manufactures the sentence, you won't find one, and that absence is a design choice, not a gap.
What NSM offers instead runs in the opposite direction: reductive paraphrase (called explication). You don't generate the complex expression from primes — you decompose it into a paraphrase stated entirely in primes, and the claim is that the paraphrase is substitutable for the original (same truth conditions, no circularity, translatable into any language's exponents). "Productive" in the NSM literature means the primes are expressively complete — they can paraphrase anything — not that there's an algorithm that derives sentences. The decomposition is done by hand, by the analyst. (That manual character is exactly why the recent papers on using LLMs to generate explications are notable — it had never been automatable.)
Three pieces do the actual work:
1. The universal grammar — combinatorial frames, not rewrite rules. Each prime comes with a small set of canonical contexts it's allowed to appear in. SOMEONE can DO something; SOMEONE can WANT to do something; SOMETHING can HAPPEN; SOMETHING can be GOOD or BAD. The "grammar" is just the set of well-formed prime combinations — and crucially, it's the syntax of the metalanguage itself, not of English. It also forbids constructions: no relative clauses (someone who did this), no comparatives, and primes don't get illegitimate valencies (FEEL has no "about," so feel bad about X is disallowed NSM).
2. Semantic molecules — the layering trick. You cannot in practice build "aardvark" straight out of I, DO, KIND, PART…; the explication would be monstrous or impossible. So NSM permits molecules: intermediate meanings (marked [m]) that are themselves explicated in primes and then function as single units higher up. hands [m], water [m], long [m], creature [m], money [m]. This gives a hierarchy — primes → molecules → richer molecules → words — and it's where most of your example's complexity actually lives.
3. The adequacy test. A good explication can be swapped in for the word with meaning preserved, uses only simpler terms, and survives translation into any tested language.
Now the example. Almost every content word in your sentence is a molecule, not a prime — need, buy, aardvark, and pet are all deep. Here's an illustrative explication (my construction in NSM style — there's no canonical published "aardvark" explication, but this follows the conventions faithfully):

A meaning qualifies as prime only if it survives two filters, and the first one is the exclusion test you're after. To be a plausible NSM prime, a word-meaning must be indefinable — ultimately simple — in addition to being well attested across a wide range of languages. The operative move is reductive paraphrase: you attempt to define the term in simpler words. If you succeed, it's excluded — it was composite. You find the core by semantic experimentation: attempting to actually explicate meanings, always aiming to reduce the terms of the explications to the smallest possible set. A prime is what's left when every further paraphrase attempt comes back circular — you can only define it using itself or something no simpler.
Their own worked exclusion is eat. A word like eat is a non-starter as a prime because it is clearly not undecomposable — it involves doing, mouth, etc. — and it is known not to have equivalents in some languages. Both filters reject it: it decomposes (filter 1), and it isn't universally lexicalized (filter 2). The same applies to many impressionistically basic English items like go, hot, and bird. So "primality" is defined negatively: a term is prime iff you cannot paraphrase it without circularity. Everything definable is excluded from the core and pushed down a level.
