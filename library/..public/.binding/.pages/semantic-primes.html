Here's the thing worth flagging first, because it reframes the question: NSM has no production rule in the generative sense. There's no S → NP VP–style derivation that outputs "I need to buy an aardvark as a pet" from the primes. Wierzbicka built NSM explicitly against that picture — her early fight was with Chomsky's autonomous syntax, the idea that you generate surface strings from unobservable underlying structures. So if you go looking for a rewrite procedure that manufactures the sentence, you won't find one, and that absence is a design choice, not a gap.
What NSM offers instead runs in the opposite direction: reductive paraphrase (called explication). You don't generate the complex expression from primes — you decompose it into a paraphrase stated entirely in primes, and the claim is that the paraphrase is substitutable for the original (same truth conditions, no circularity, translatable into any language's exponents). "Productive" in the NSM literature means the primes are expressively complete — they can paraphrase anything — not that there's an algorithm that derives sentences. The decomposition is done by hand, by the analyst. (That manual character is exactly why the recent papers on using LLMs to generate explications are notable — it had never been automatable.)
Three pieces do the actual work:
1. The universal grammar — combinatorial frames, not rewrite rules. Each prime comes with a small set of canonical contexts it's allowed to appear in. SOMEONE can DO something; SOMEONE can WANT to do something; SOMETHING can HAPPEN; SOMETHING can be GOOD or BAD. The "grammar" is just the set of well-formed prime combinations — and crucially, it's the syntax of the metalanguage itself, not of English. It also forbids constructions: no relative clauses (someone who did this), no comparatives, and primes don't get illegitimate valencies (FEEL has no "about," so feel bad about X is disallowed NSM).
2. Semantic molecules — the layering trick. You cannot in practice build "aardvark" straight out of I, DO, KIND, PART…; the explication would be monstrous or impossible. So NSM permits molecules: intermediate meanings (marked [m]) that are themselves explicated in primes and then function as single units higher up. hands [m], water [m], long [m], creature [m], money [m]. This gives a hierarchy — primes → molecules → richer molecules → words — and it's where most of your example's complexity actually lives.
3. The adequacy test. A good explication can be swapped in for the word with meaning preserved, uses only simpler terms, and survives translation into any tested language.
Now the example. Almost every content word in your sentence is a molecule, not a prime — need, buy, aardvark, and pet are all deep. Here's an illustrative explication (my construction in NSM style — there's no canonical published "aardvark" explication, but this follows the conventions faithfully):
text
I need to buy an aardvark as a pet  ≈

I want to have something
this something is a creature [m] of one kind  ── "an aardvark"

I heard "production rule" and pattern-matched to Chomsky — exactly the framework Wierzbicka is against — when your analogy was arithmetic, not generative syntax. The prime factorization picture is the right one to interrogate: a number isn't "generated by a rewrite rule," it is its decomposition into primes with multiplicities. You're asking what the corresponding decomposition relation is for NSM — what plays the role of ∏pnkn\prod p_n^{k_n}∏pnkn​​. Let me answer that and not relitigate the framing.
The honest answer about how they define it: the composition operation is reductive paraphrase, and the structural claim is that a complex meaning is a configuration of primes — but the algebra is not free commutative multiplication. It's closer to a typed/ordered composition with a specific structure. Here's where the analogy holds and where it breaks, stated precisely, because the breakages are the actual content of your question.
Where it matches arithmetic. The primes are the irreducible factors. Wierzbicka's claim of expressive completeness is the exact analogue of "every natural number factors into primes": every meaning explicates into a configuration of the 65. And like primality, the test for primehood is indecomposability — a prime is one that cannot be paraphrased in simpler terms without circularity, just as ppp is prime when it has no nontrivial factorization.
Where it breaks, factor 1 — composition is non-commutative and structured, not a multiset. In ∏pnkn\prod p_n^{k_n}∏pnkn​​ order is nothing; 2⋅3=3⋅22\cdot3 = 3\cdot22⋅3=3⋅2. In NSM the combination is governed by the canonical syntax: each prime carries a small set of licensed argument frames, and meaning depends on which prime fills which slot. SOMEONE DO SOMETHING and SOMETHING DO SOMEONE are not the same configuration. So the operation isn't multiplication of a multiset of factors — it's substitution into typed valency slots. The right algebraic picture is something like terms in a free algebra over a typed signature, not exponents over a commutative monoid.
Where it breaks, factor 2 — and this is the one that matters most for your sentence — the decomposition is not flat. It's layered through molecules. This is the real answer to "how is aardvark related to the primes." A number factors directly into primes in one level. A complex word doesnot factor directly into the 65 primes in one level. It factors into semantic molecules, which themselves factor into primes (or into further molecules). So the structure is a tower, not a single product:
word  →  {molecules}  →  {simpler molecules}  →  ⋯  →  {primes}\text{word} \;\to\; \{\text{molecules}\}\;\to\;\{\text{simpler molecules}\}\;\to\;\cdots\;\to\;\{\text{primes}\}word→{molecules}→{simpler molecules}→⋯→{primes}
A molecule like creature [m] or money [m] is a packaged sub-explication that, once built, functions as a single unit at the next level up. The bracket notation [m] is precisely a marker that says "this term is non-prime but has been licensed as a reusable intermediate — its own factorization is stored elsewhere." This is the part with no arithmetic analogue: it's as if, to factor a large number, you were required to first define composite "intermediate factors" and build up in stages, because direct factorization into primes is computationally/expressively unmanageable. NSM makes that layering obligatory and explicit rather than optional.
So your sentence relates to the primes like this — read it as a factorization tree, where leaves are primes and internal nodes are molecules:
I — prime (leaf).
need to — a configuration over WANT, IF NOT, BAD/CAN'T: roughly I want this; if this doesn't happen, something bad will happen / I can't do other things I want. Reduces to primes directly, no molecule needed.
buy — molecule. buy [m] explicates over money [m] (itself a molecule), GIVE-type transfer (SOMEONE GIVE SOMETHING TO SOMEONE), HAVE, WANT, BECAUSE. So: I give money to someone, this someone gives the thing to me, now I have it. Two molecule-levels deep before bottoming out in primes.
an aardvark — molecule, and the deepest branch. aardvark [m] → animal/creature [m] → primes. The species-specific content (LIVING, BODY, KIND, BIG/SMALL, parts via PART, etc.) sits inside the creature molecule and its sub-molecules. NSM does not try to bottom out "aardvark" into 65 primes in one shot — that's the whole point of the molecule device.
as a pet — molecule-ish configuration: creature [m] that lives WHERE I live, that I WANT to be near, that I DO good things for (feed [m], look after [m]).
The "production rule," then, in their terms, is the single schema: a meaning is well-formed iff it can be rewritten by reductive paraphrase into licensed configurations of primes-and-molecules, terminating in primes, using only the canonical syntactic frames. That's the closest thing to ∏pnkn\prod p_n^{k_n}∏pnkn​​ they offer — but it's a terminating typed rewrite into a layered tower, and the multiplicities aren't exponents on commuting factors, they're occurrences of primes in argument positions.
One genuinely open seam, which I think is what your instinct was circling: NSM has never given a formal account of the uniqueness of the factorization. Arithmetic has the fundamental theorem — factorization into primes is unique. NSM has no analogous theorem; two analysts can produce different explications of the same word, and there's no proof that a canonical normal form exists. The decomposition is empirically constrained (substitutability, cross-linguistic survival) but not algebraically pinned down. If you wanted to push the prime-factorization analogy to its limit, that's the missing piece — a uniqueness/normal-form result for explications.

Got it. I read how they actually do it, and here is the exclusion procedure in their own terms — it's the piece I kept stepping over.
A meaning qualifies as prime only if it survives two filters, and the first one is the exclusion test you're after. To be a plausible NSM prime, a word-meaning must be indefinable — ultimately simple — in addition to being well attested across a wide range of languages. The operative move is reductive paraphrase: you attempt to define the term in simpler words. If you succeed, it's excluded — it was composite. You find the core by semantic experimentation: attempting to actually explicate meanings, always aiming to reduce the terms of the explications to the smallest possible set. A prime is what's left when every further paraphrase attempt comes back circular — you can only define it using itself or something no simpler.
Their own worked exclusion is eat. A word like eat is a non-starter as a prime because it is clearly not undecomposable — it involves doing, mouth, etc. — and it is known not to have equivalents in some languages. Both filters reject it: it decomposes (filter 1), and it isn't universally lexicalized (filter 2). The same applies to many impressionistically basic English items like go, hot, and bird. So "primality" is defined negatively: a term is prime iff you cannot paraphrase it without circularity. Everything definable is excluded from the core and pushed down a level.

Ha — yes, that's basically the whole game, and you've put your finger on the win condition more cleanly than most of the literature does.
The "caveman charades" framing is right but with one constraint that makes it harder than party charades: you don't just have to get the idea across, you have to get it across using only the 65 cards in your hand, and the gesture has to mean the same thing when an Mbula speaker or a Korean speaker plays it. Ordinary charades lets you mime "aardvark" by doing a snout and digging. NSM charades bans the snout-mime unless snout itself has already been won — built up from BODY, PART, LIKE. You can only ever point with cards you've already earned.
And that's exactly the exclusion procedure restated as a game rule: a word is "excludable" iff you can win the round for it without ever picking it up off the table. If you can convey "buy" using only WANT / MINE / DO / BECAUSE / SOMEONE / money[m]…, then "buy" was never a card you needed — it's derived. The moment you find yourself miming buy to explain buy, that's the circularity buzzer, and that's the signal that the word is actually prime (or that you're missing a card you haven't built yet).
The molecules are the genuinely clever part of the game — they're saved combos. Once you've won "creature" the hard way, you get to keep it as a single token and reuse it, so you never re-derive "living thing with a body that moves and feels" from scratch every time you want to talk about a pet or a dog or a fish. Real charades has no memory; NSM lets you bank your victories. That's the only reason a sentence about aardvarks is tractable at all.
Where the game genuinely could be lost — and where I'd push back on "you can exclude any other word" — is that NSM's universality claim is an empirical bet, not a theorem. The bet is: for every word in every language, a winning all-primes round exists. They've played a lot of rounds (90+ languages) and keep winning, which is the evidence. But there's no proof the deck is complete, and there are disputed rounds —eat famously can't be won in some languages, which is why it never made the cut, and even within the 65 there are contested cards like HAVE PARTS. So the honest version is: if the 65 really are a universal winning hand, then yes, every other word is excludable by definition. Whether they are is the thing five decades of play has been testing rather than settling.
Which, for what it's worth, is the seam your instinct keeps finding — the missing uniqueness/completeness result. Charades has a winner per round; it has no guarantee that two players mime the same word the same way, or that every word is winnable. That guarantee is exactly what arithmetic has (unique factorization, and every integer factors) and what NSM, so far, only has by repeated successful play.
