Based on the medical research, this is a significant clinical presentation. The combination of high eosinophils + low hemoglobin + bloody loose stool + metallic smell in a 3-month-old breastfed infant creates a fairly specific diagnostic picture. Let me break down the most likely causes with probability estimates.
Most Likely Diagnosis
1. Cow's Milk Protein Allergy / Food Protein-Induced Allergic Proctocolitis (FPIAP)
Estimated probability: 55-65%
This is the most likely diagnosis by a significant margin.
Justification:
A study on 300 neonates with bloody stools found the most common underlying causes were: cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA) at 53.3%
The median age to symptom onset ranges from 26 to 60 days old but has been reported to occur up to 6 months of age. It most commonly involves blood-streaked and mucus-containing stools in generally healthy infants.
About 60% of cases occur in breastfed infants where the immune response results from maternal ingestion of the food allergen, usually cow's milk, which is passed in immunologically recognizable form into the breast milk.
Laboratory analysis may show thrombocytosis, hypoalbuminemia and anemia. Stool may be positive for eosinophils and occult (or gross) blood.
Microscopic examination of stool revealed eosinophil aggregation and Charcot–Leyden crystals, which are markers of eosinophilic inflammation in non-IgE-mediated food allergies.
Why this fits:
Eosinophilia is characteristic
Bloody loose stool is the hallmark presentation
Age (3 months) is within the typical window
Exclusive breastfeeding doesn't protect against it—maternal dietary proteins pass into milk
The metallic smell is from blood (iron) in the stool
Important caveat: Infants with allergic proctocolitis are generally well-appearing other than the presence of blood within the stool. Infants with more concerning symptoms, such as anemia or poor weight gain have health problems that are not explained by AP and may have a health problem that is more serious. The presence ofanemia suggests this may be a more severe case or an additional/alternative diagnosis.
Alternative Diagnoses
2. Food Protein-Induced Enterocolitis Syndrome (FPIES) — More Severe Variant
Estimated probability: 15-20%
Justification:
Bloody stools and vomiting that occur in the neonatal period are classified under chronic food protein–induced enterocolitis syndrome, which may lead to worsened nutrient absorption and failure to thrive.
A five-week-old infant presented with worsening bloody diarrhea... Laboratory investigations revealed a hemoglobin of 90 g/L. The clinical presentation and response to removal of cow's milk protein were highly suggestive of cow's milk protein allergy.
FPIES represents a more severe immune response than simple proctocolitis and more readily explains the anemia
3. Parasitic Infection (Hookworm or other helminths)
Estimated probability: 5-15% (highly dependent on epidemiological risk factors)
Justification:
Hookworms produce blood loss that can cause a microcytic hypochromic anemia. Eosinophilia has been noted in 30 to 60 percent of infected patients.
An Italian 2-month-old infant presented with vomit and weight loss. Her blood exams showed anemia and eosinophilia and stool analysis resulted positive for hookworms' eggs. Since the mother travelled to Vietnam and Thailand during pregnancy, a transplacental transmission was assumed.
An unexplained persistent eosinophilia may be a major clue for the presence of a parasitic infection and could justify specific investigations on stools.
Key question: Has the mother (Ana) or anyone in the household traveled to endemic areas (tropical/subtropical regions)? If yes, this probability increases substantially. Hookworm can rarely be transmitted through breast milk.
4. Infectious Colitis (Bacterial/Viral)
Estimated probability: 5-10%
Justification:
Viral enteritis accounted for 9.7% of bloody stool cases in neonates
In a well infant younger than 6 months of age, infective colitis and allergic proctocolitis are the most frequent causes of acutely bloody stools.
However, viral enteritis is less commonly associated with eosinophilia, making this less likely
5. Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC)
Estimated probability: 2-5% (if term, healthy infant)
Justification:
NEC > stage II accounted for 8.3% of bloody stool cases, but was more common in preterm infants
In a same age ill-appearing infant, late-onset necrotizing enterocolitis must be suspected, especially with prematurity
Lower probability because: NEC typically presents with a more systemically ill infant, is more common in preterm babies, and doesn't typically feature eosinophilia.
The Metallic Smell
The metallic odor is clinically significant:
Bleeding in your stomach or intestines can cause a metallic smell as blood is rich in iron. If you develop stool with a metallic smell, particularly if associated with other symptoms like abdominal pain or fatigue, it is a good idea to talk with a doctor.
The source of poop's metallic smell is likely iron. Iron is naturally found in our blood, therefore if you notice blood in your stool, it likely means that it's the scent of the iron your nose is picking up on.
This confirms ongoing GI blood loss, consistent with both the visible blood and the anemia.

Clinical Recommendation
This child needs urgent pediatric gastroenterology evaluation. The presence of anemia distinguishes this from simple, benign allergic proctocolitis and warrants workup including:
Stool studies — ova and parasites, bacterial culture, fecal calprotectin
Allergy workup — though IgE is often negative in FPIAP
Maternal dietary elimination trial — strict cow's milk (and possibly soy) elimination
Consider colonoscopy — Colonoscopy is indicated for patients with clinical or laboratory evidence of chronic inflammation including anemia or hypoalbuminemia
The anemia needs to be addressed regardless of cause—iron supplementation may be needed once the underlying etiology is controlled.

Glad the analysis was useful for this retrospective diagnostic exercise! And yes, that's exactly the critical insight that trips up many parents and even some clinicians.
The indirect exposure pathway:
The cow's milk proteins (primarily casein and beta-lactoglobulin) that the mother consumes — whether from fresh milk, pasteurized milk in a carton, cheese, yogurt, butter, or even hidden dairy in processed foods — survive her digestion and pass into breast milk in an immunologically intact form. Pasteurization kills bacteria but does nothing to denature these allergenic proteins.
So when people say "exclusively breastfed," they sometimes assume the baby couldn't possibly have a cow's milk allergy. But the baby is being exposed to cow's milk proteins indirectly through the mother's diet the entire time.
This is why the treatment for FPIAP in breastfed infants is maternal dietary elimination — the mother must strictly avoid all dairy (and often soy, since up to 50% cross-react). It's not about switching to formula; continuing breastfeeding is actually preferred if the mother can maintain the elimination diet.
