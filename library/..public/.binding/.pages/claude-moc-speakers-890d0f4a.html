Here is all of Monday. Only one slot has a choice, 11:00–12:20. Everything else is plenary.
Fixed program
Before 9:00: registration in the HCØ foyer.
9:00 Megan Peters (UCL), keynote. She argues that consciousness indicators and tests can't be assumed to mean the same thing in systems they weren't validated on. Her alternative is an extended "iterative natural kinds" program combining philosophy, experiments and computational epistemology.
10:00 Niccolò Negro (Tel Aviv), showcase. Because consciousness matters practically, non-epistemic values seep into statistical choices and theory selection. Which theory counts as "best" then becomes relative to purpose.
11:00–12:20, four rooms (20-minute talks, so you can switch between them)
Auditorium 2: Testing for consciousness in patients, infants and animals
11:00 Streicher (Montréal): an LLM-assisted review of about 865 studies of consciousness tests in disorders-of-consciousness patients, mapped into topics, building toward an open database and a causal graph of how the tests relate.
11:20 Yeung (Copenhagen): mirror self-recognition in 66 toddlers. Gaze data support a third "partial recogniser" group between pass and fail.
11:40 Wai (York): multidimensional consciousness profiles across species have an aggregation problem. Sum, weighted, threshold and distance metrics give conflicting rankings.
12:00 Kleiner (Bamberg): "direct" tests for consciousness that use covariates and don't depend on a known correlate, borrowing mathematics from evaluation research. Infants are his worked example.
Auditorium 3: Language models, agency, interpretability and risk
11:00 Malagutti (Lisbon): against treating LLMs as Dennettian intentional systems. He argues the design stance predicts better.
11:20 Sekatskaya (Cologne): a functionalist account of LLM agency, separate from the hard problem, built on five measurable dimensions. She argues alignment should become coordination rather than containment.
11:40 de Villiers-Botha (Stellenbosch): a dilemma for building conscious AI. The safest kind, human-like, removes the reasons for building it and carries full moral status.
12:00 Moskalewicz (Warsaw): a proposed experiment on "AI-induced psychosis." It tests whether LLMs hand off to a human therapist when they detect a user in a prodromal state.
Auditorium 4: Altered states and neurophenomenology (mostly psilocybin)
11:00 Høgsgaard Andersen (Copenhagen): a guided music-visualization exercise used to prepare people with alcohol use disorder for psilocybin, with a thematic analysis of nine sessions.
11:20 Beyköylü (Leiden): a 15-week case study of a couple at a psilocybin retreat (fMRI, daily sampling, interviews) analysed with recurrence networks and cross-mapping.
11:40 Ciobanu (Leiden): the same case study, focusing on the method of applying identical nonlinear network measures to the neural and the experiential data.
12:00 Jedlovszky (UCL): 40 people on psilocybin. After dosing, language shifts toward more self-reference and meaning-making and less modular semantic networks, and the shift predicts wellbeing.
Auditorium 5: Formal and information-theoretic frameworks
11:00 van Loo (Tilburg): a measure-theoretic language that separates event spaces, information channels and bridge laws. IIT becomes a deterministic channel, and he adds a probabilistic "Sampled Consciousness Theory."
11:20 Mason (AMCS): three hypotheses: consciousness is mathematical, weakly mathematical, or non-mathematizable. He gives results on what each implies for consistent formal measures, framed within ontic structural realism.
11:40 Fougea (ENS Paris-Saclay): global workspace theory formalized with timed automata, with a refinement relation for building theories in increasing precision and deriving testable properties.
12:00 Ebner (Greifswald): an information-theoretic argument that projecting high-dimensional information into low dimensions must preserve distances up to a factor, which gives consciousness thresholds.
Afternoon (fixed)
13:30 John O'Keefe (UCL, Nobel 2014), keynote. Parallels between introspective properties of consciousness and the hippocampal cognitive map: three operating modes in the same neurons, holistic 3-D representation, goal selection, and theta oscillations.
14:30 Signorelli: introduction to the conference's collective consensus-paper project.
15:00–16:00: consensus discussion groups (rooms A101–A112).
16:00–18:30: welcome reception at the Niels Bohr Building, with a talk on the history of the "Copenhagen spirit."

Here are the headline speakers in day order. The keynotes and showcases are plenary, so you'll see all of them without choosing.
Monday
John O'Keefe (UCL), 13:30. The most decorated person at the conference. He discovered hippocampal place cells in 1971 and, with Lynn Nadel, wrote The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map (1978), the founding text of spatial-memory neuroscience. He shared the 2014 Nobel Prize with May-Britt and Edvard Moser. Talk: a property-by-property comparison between features of consciousness found by introspection and the hippocampal map's three operating modes, with theta oscillations given a special role.
Megan Peters (UCL), 9:00. A leading computational researcher on metacognition and perceptual confidence: how subjective confidence relates to the evidence a system actually has. She co-authored the widely cited 2023 report on indicators of consciousness in AI. Talk: a critique of that very indicator approach. Indicators validated in humans can't be assumed to mean the same thing in AI, so she proposes an iterative "natural kinds" program instead.
Tuesday
Liad Mudrik (Tel Aviv), 9:00. A central figure in empirical consciousness science. She is known for work on unconscious processing and for co-leading the Cogitate adversarial collaboration, which pre-registered competing predictions of global workspace theory and IIT and tested them head-to-head. Its results appeared inNature in 2025 and challenged both theories. She was also a co-author on the AI-indicators report.Talk: "prediction maps," which represent a theory as a core–periphery network of claims and use graph centrality to find the experiments that would hurt it most.
Jakob Hohwy (Monash), 13:30 showcase. One of the main philosophers of predictive processing. His book The Predictive Mind (2013) is standard, and he coined "self-evidencing," the idea that an agent's inference continually gathers evidence for its own existence as a model. He founded Monash's consciousness centre.Talk: how self-evidencing could be positioned as a framework for consciousness.
Wednesday
Lenore Blum (Carnegie Mellon; AMCS president), 9:00. A major theoretical computer scientist. The Blum–Shub–Smale model, a theory of computation and complexity over the real numbers, is hers with Shub and Smale. She is also known for the Blum Blum Shub generator and was an early president of the Association for Women in Mathematics. With Manuel Blum she developed the Conscious Turing Machine, a formal workspace-style model. Talk: world models and how a "first-person" perspective could emerge in an AI.
Evan Thompson (UBC), 18:00 at the Louisiana Museum, receiving the Mind-Matter Prize. Co-author with Varela and Rosch of The Embodied Mind (1991), which launched enactivism. He also wrote Mind in Life (2007), Waking, Dreaming, Being (2014), Why I Am Not a Buddhist (2020), and The Blind Spot (2024, with Frank and Gleiser). He is probably the most widely read philosopher of mind at the meeting. Talk: "Consciousness in Life," his thesis that mind and life are continuous.
Thursday
Mirja Hartimo (Helsinki), 9:00. The leading scholar of Husserl's philosophy of mathematics; her book Husserl and Mathematics came out from Cambridge in 2021. Talk: Husserl on mathematics and logic. It sets up the afternoon showcase (Ramos de Andrade's mathematical model of Husserlian time-consciousness) and the Time-Consciousness session.
Friday
Guillaume Dumas (Montréal/Mila), 9:00. A pioneer of hyperscanning, recording two brains at once during interaction. His 2010 EEG study showing inter-brain synchronization during imitation is a founding paper of that field, and he works on computational psychiatry and social AI. Talk: a formal "generative neurophenomenology" running from coupled brains to metacognitive artificial agents.
Harald Atmanspacher (ETH Zurich, Collegium Helveticum), 13:30. The main current exponent of Pauli–Jung dual-aspect monism. He was the founding editor of the journal Mind and Matter and wrote the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on quantum approaches to consciousness. Talk: the roles meaning plays in dual-aspect monism.
Well-known names in the parallel sessions
Johannes Kleiner (Mon A2): one of the founders of the mathematical consciousness science movement.
Kenneth Williford (Wed A2): self-representational theories of consciousness.
Wanja Wiese (Thu A3): predictive processing and the philosophy of consciousness.
William Seager (Thu A5): panpsychism and the metaphysics of mind.
Albert Newen (Tue A3): embodied cognition and the self.
These are career facts as I know them, and the affiliations are the ones in the programme. Should I check any of them, or go on to Tuesday's choices in the same detail as Monday?

Here's the trip from Staldgade to the conference, step by step.
Walk to København H station (5–10 minutes). Head to Copenhagen Central Station. The metro is underground: follow signs with a white M on a red square, and in the station ask for "the metro" if you can't find them.
Buy a ticket before you go down. The metro has no barriers, but inspectors fine people without tickets. In the DOT Tickets app, buy a ticket for 2 zones from København H to Vibenshus Runddel. The ticket machines at the station take cards and have an English option. Buy it right before you travel, because it's only valid for a limited time.
Find the right platform: M3 (Cityringen). Line M4 also stops at København H, so check that the sign over the platform says M3 and lists Rådhuspladsen as the next stop.
Ride 8 stops, about 13 minutes. The trains are driverless and come every few minutes. The stops are Rådhuspladsen, Gammel Strand, Kongens Nytorv, Marmorkirken, Østerport, Trianglen, Poul Henningsens Plads, then Vibenshus Runddel, where you get off. Screens and announcements name each stop. If you board the other direction by mistake, the M3 is a loop, so it still reaches Vibenshus Runddel; it just takes a stop or two longer.
Walk to HCØ (about 10–15 minutes). Once you're up at street level, put Universitetsparken 5 into your phone's maps app and follow the walking directions. I don't know the street route well enough to give turns reliably.
On Monday, leave Staldgade around 7:45. That gets you to HCØ for 8:15–8:30, with margin for buying the ticket and finding the platform.

O'Keefe is the one on Monday who matters most to the field, and Megan Peters is the one most actively shaping current debates.
Plenary (everyone attends)
John O'Keefe (13:30). He discovered place cells in 1971 and co-wrote The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map (1978), and won the 2014 Nobel Prize. He is a towering figure in systems neuroscience but an outsider to consciousness science, so this talk is him bringing the cognitive map into the field.
Megan Peters (9:00). A computational cognitive neuroscientist, now at UCL according to the programme, who works on metacognition and perceptual confidence. She co-authored the influential 2023 report on indicators of AI consciousness, and is one of the most active people setting the field's methodological agenda. Her talk argues against the indicator approach she helped popularise.
Niccolò Negro (10:00). An early-career philosopher of consciousness science at Tel Aviv, the base of Liad Mudrik's group. He writes on how the field chooses and tests theories, including IIT. He's rising rather than established.
Camilo Miguel Signorelli (14:30). A local organiser at Copenhagen who works on mathematical, especially category-theoretic, models of conscious experience. He introduces the conference's consensus-paper project.
Parallel speakers worth knowing
Johannes Kleiner (A2, 12:00). A mathematical physicist and one of the central figures in "mathematical consciousness science," closely associated with founding the association that runs this conference. He is probably the most significant name in the morning parallel sessions.
Jonathan Mason (A5, 11:20). A mathematician and a long-standing figure in the same association, working on the foundations of formal measures of consciousness.
Marc Ebner (A5, 12:00). A computer science professor at Greifswald working on evolutionary computation and AI, who writes on consciousness from that side.
Maria Sekatskaya (A3, 11:20). A philosopher of free will and agency, now applying that to AI.
Marcin Moskalewicz (A3, 12:00). Works in phenomenological psychiatry.
Most of the remaining speakers are PhD students or postdocs (Streicher, Yeung, Wai, Malagutti, Fougea, van Loo, the Leiden psilocybin group, Jedlovszky). Their talks are often fresh work, but they aren't established names yet.
