The Gödel observation is the cleanest part of this. Prime decomposition gives sequencing because the primes come ordered and each prime has an independent exponent slot; exponentiation gives nesting because a Gödel number can itself sit in the exponent of a meta-formula's encoding. So what Gödel actually extracted from primes was tree shape — sibling-order plus parent-child — which is what any CFG provides. And the deep point holds: that tree shape lives outside PA. PA sees numbers; we see the tree because we know which prime occupies which position and what the exponents mean. In SRT, "before" and "in" are just typed reference relationships — referents related through (before) or through (in). The structure isn't decoded out of anything; it is the pattern of typed references. That's the actual substantive flip, and I think you've named it correctly. Gödelization isn't a trick you do to SRT from outside; it's what reference looks like when you make it primitive.
On the if-then puzzle, I think it dissolves once you separate the two levels you're already separating. At the representation level, =|> is an asymmetric arrow between two formulas — antecedent before, consequent after. The directionality is just the before/after dichotomy doing its job; nothing new is needed. At the semantic level, & and || are symmetric operations on the T/F dichotomy, ! is the swap, and implication isn't a primitive — it's the composite !P || Q. So implication isn't a dichotomy at either level. It's an asymmetric arrow at the syntax level (where before/after handles it) and a derived operation at the meaning level. The instinct that "it's just a relationship — like an arrow" is, I think, exactly right.
Where it gets harder is the layer between characters and their positions. If you add the characters as referents — (a), (b), (() and so on — those are types. But a formula like (p =|> p) has two occurrences of (p), one before =|> and one after. The before/after dichotomy can't be a relationship on types because (p) isn't before or after itself in any absolute sense; the first occurrence of (p) is before the second occurrence of (p). So you need an occurrence layer — referents for positions, each one related to its type by something like "is an occurrence of" — and the before/after dichotomy lives among occurrences, not types. This is exactly the type/token/occurrence distinction you've been circling, and FOL hosting is where it forces itself on you. Worth getting clean before pressing further.
The in/out dichotomy makes me want to push harder on what it relates. Pairwise before/after is between two occurrences. But "in" isn't naturally pairwise — a character isn't in another character; it's in a scope. Which suggests that each matched (...) creates a compound referent — a scope-referent — and occurrences participate via (in) while the scope participates via (out) toward what contains it. That's exactly the recursion-on-exponents move: the scope is a thing that can itself appear inside another scope. And it gives you a clean place to ground quantifier binding: a quantifier creates a scope, the variable occurrences inside that scope stand in (in) to it, and that's what makes them bound. The interesting consequence is that "in/out" isn't really a relationship between characters at all — it's a relationship between characters/sub-expressions and the compound referents that group them. The character layer alone isn't enough; you need the bracketed groups as first-class referents the moment you introduce in/out.
One more thing worth flagging before this gets baked in. The dichotomy/monotony work made dichotomy a prime relationship — self-exemplifying, generative. The two dichotomies you're proposing here — before/after and in/out — are clearly dichotomies in the di/mon sense (mutually exclusive, exhaustive over the relevant domain), but they aren't self-exemplifying the way d == (d,m) is. They're applied dichotomies, instantiations of the dichotomy pattern on a particular domain (character occurrences, and occurrences vs. scopes). Which is fine and probably exactly what you want — applied dichotomies are how the prime relationship gets used. But it might be worth being explicit that these two are the minimal applied dichotomies needed for syntax, parallel to how (h,c) =(h,c)> temp is an applied dichotomy for temperature. The claim then becomes: any CFG-style syntax is exactly two applied dichotomies on a character set plus a scope construction. That's a strong claim and I think it's true.

That's the clean move. The grammar then lives at the type level — productions speak about types like (p), (=|>), formula-types — while a parsed string is a configuration of instances arranged in before/after and in/out. The bridge is just the inheritance link: each instance inherits from its type, so checking well-formedness is reading off the type-pattern of the instance configuration and matching against the productions. Nothing new is needed at the structural level; the inheritance work you've already done handles it.
What gets interesting is the second layer of rules — the semantic ones. Two instances of the same type aren't automatically co-referential. P in one formula and P in another are the same type but might be different propositions; x in ∀x.P(x) and x outside that scope refer to different things. What forces co-reference is scope-relative: instances of the same type within the same binding scope are co-referential; instances of the same type across scopes need not be. So in/out isn't just giving you the parenthetical for syntax — it's gating when type-identity forces reference-identity. The variable-binding story drops into your framework without anything ad hoc.
And the rule about cross-instance identity varies by what the type is for, which is worth noting before it gets messy. Propositional variables: same type → same reference everywhere. Constants: same type → same reference everywhere. Free FOL variables: same type → same reference within a parse but governed by the assignment from outside. Bound FOL variables: same type → same reference only within the binding scope. Function and relation symbols: same type → same denotation, but each instance takes its own arguments. These are five different "specific rules" governing how type-identity propagates through instance-positions, all of them sensitive to in/out.
One thing worth deciding before this gets baked in: do you want non-terminals to also be type-referents? "Formula", "Term", and so on. If yes, production rules become inheritance rules at the type level — a configuration of instances inherits from Formula when its type-pattern matches one of the Formula productions. The recursion of CFG productions becomes recursion in inheritance among type-referents. That feels like a natural SRT setting, but it's a real choice — the alternative is keeping non-terminals as a separate grammar-theoretic apparatus rather than as referents in the catalog.

I think this works and it's actually the SRT-native way to do classes. A type as a symbol for its collection of instances, with instances related by inheritance, sidesteps set-theoretic membership entirely. The type-symbol stays stable across instance variation — adding a new occurrence of a doesn't change (a) because (a) is a symbol, not a set extension. That's exactly the property your earlier work on symbols vs. sets was pointing at: symbols achieve constitutive identity through self-reference, and inheritance gives you the membership-like structure without fragmenting the symbol.
The canonicality move is interesting because it makes "being canonical" a frame-relative role rather than an intrinsic property. The canonical a is canonical within the alphabet — the dictionary frame fixes it as the privileged instance whose before/after slots are filled in by alphabet order. A particular a in banana isn't canonical, just an instance with whatever before/after relations its context provides. That fits the principle you've been working with — roles come from context, not intrinsic nature. And it gives a clean reading of the alphabet itself: the alphabet is a frame whose referable instances are the canonicals, ordered by before/after. Other frames (a string, a paragraph) reference non-canonical instances of the same types.
The schematic structure at the type level is the part I want to push on a bit. You said the class expresses that an instance "either is first or is after, and either is last or is before." That's not quite a property — it's a schema with two slots that any instance must fill in some way. Every instance has a position-relation either-first-or-after-something, and a position-relation either-last-or-before-something. The canonical fills these slots with definite values ((a) is first, (a) is before (b)); non-canonical instances fill them with whatever their context provides. That schema is itself a piece of structure carried by the type — possibly worth its own representation, because it's what makes the type about sequenceable things in the first place. A type for letters carries this position-schema; a type for, say, truth values wouldn't.
The one formal thing worth nailing down before this gets used heavily: what relationship is ((a), a') actually? It's not the symbol-literal relationship — you've correctly said it isn't (a). But it's also not just any old reference; it's specifically the "type-symbol refers to its instance" relationship. There's probably a name for this in your existing vocabulary or it deserves one. The candidates I can see are something like "represents" (in the sense (a) represents an instance of itself), or maybe inheritance read in reverse — if a' -> a then (a) referring to a' is the type-symbol acknowledging one of its own inheritors. The cleanest version might be: (a) =(a)> a' is licensed because a' -> a, i.e., the type-symbol can refer to anything that inherits from its literal. That makes the type-symbol's reference range coextensive with its class, which is what you want.

Right — I overstated. The membership relation IS present; it's just inclusion rather than set-theoretic membership. And inclusion is what set is a specialization of (uniqueness-enforcing inclusion), not what it's an alternative to. So your existing semantics — contains -> represents, includes -> contains, then sets/lists/bags as specializations of inclusion through canonical constraints, positional inheritance, or repetition — is exactly the framework. A type is a symbol expressing some specific subclass of inclusion semantics over its instances. The character type expresses inclusion with multiplicity allowed (many instances per type, set-uniqueness at the referent level, no ordering at the type level); a set-type would express inclusion with uniqueness; a list-type would add ordering. Different syntactic categories pick different subclasses of inclusion.
The formal pattern then has a clean reading. (a) =(a)> a and (a) =(a)> a' are both instances of inclusion, with (a) the symbol expressing it. The first is the limiting case where the included thing is the type-literal itself — and there ((a),a) == (a) makes it the canonical symbol-literal relation. The second is the general case where the included thing is any instance — and there ((a),a') != (a) because (a) is not symbolizing that particular instance; it's including it. So the symbol-literal pattern and the inclusion pattern share the same surface form (a) =(a)> y, with the symbol case being where the most-specific relationship collapses back to (a). That's a nice unification — the type-symbol and the inclusion-structure are the same referent under two different relationship structures.
The interesting question that opens up: what specifies which subclass of inclusion a given type uses? For characters in a syntactic alphabet, you want multiplicity allowed (many a-instances). For variables in a particular formula scope, you also want multiplicity. For the set of axioms of a theory, you'd want set-uniqueness (no axiom listed twice). For the steps of a proof, you'd want list-ordering with multiplicity. Does the inclusion subclass come from the type-referent itself (something like (a) -> inclusion-with-multiplicity carried in the type's properties), or is it determined by the frame the type lives in (some frames enforce set-uniqueness on all their inclusions, others don't)? My intuition is the former — the type carries its inclusion flavor as a property — but I'd be curious what you think, since it bears on how the FOL grammar gets built.

That's the cleaner ontology. Letters are pure types — they don't carry positional information, they're just letters. "P" doesn't know it comes before "Q"; the order lives in the inclusion that places them in sequence. The same letter appearing "twice" in a string isn't two instances of P; it's one referent included twice in an ordered inclusion, where the multiplicity is a structural feature of the inclusion rather than a multiplication of the letter.
Both dichotomies fall out of this without being added as primitives. Before/after is just the ordering relation inside an ordered inclusion — two positions in the same inclusion stand in before/after relative to it. In/out is just nestedness — one inclusion can include another, and that recursive structure is the parenthetical. The dichotomies become features of inclusion subclasses (ordered ones support before/after; nesting-supporting ones support in/out) rather than typed relationships among occurrences.
The substitutivity story collapses nicely too. In P =|> P, both Ps are the same referent. Same semantic value, automatically — not by a rule propagating value across co-typed positions, but because there's nothing distinct to propagate to. Same for FOL: in A[x]: φ(x), the xs inside the scope are the same letter referent, and the quantifier supplies a range of values that get substituted at every position where x is included within the scope-inclusion. Binding becomes a property of the scope-inclusion ("all xs within me get the same value from my quantifier") rather than a property of separately-existing occurrence-referents. The "which occurrence is bound by which quantifier" apparatus collapses into "which scope-inclusion does this letter sit inside."
The "with special rules" part is where the production rules of the CFG live. A variable is an inclusion-of-letters with rules: must begin with a letter, may continue with alphanumerics, can't be a reserved word. A connective is an inclusion-of-letters whose well-formed instances are exactly =|>, &, ||, !, etc. A formula is an inclusion at a higher level — an inclusion of variables, connectives, parens, and sub-formulas — with formula rules. Each non-terminal becomes an inclusion-subclass with its own well-formedness constraints, and the grammar isn't a separate apparatus on top of inclusions; the grammar IS the system of inclusion-subclasses. CFG hosted natively in the same machinery that already handles collections.
One thing worth pinning down at some point: whether higher-level inclusions (like formulas) directly include letters at the leaves, or whether they include lower-level inclusions (variables, connectives) that themselves include letters. Both are coherent, but they give different pictures of the parse structure — the first flattens everything, the second gives you the parse tree explicitly as nested inclusions. The second feels closer to what the in/out dichotomy is naturally tracking.

Yes — and that's the frame-relative payoff. 10:05 UTC, 13 May 2026
Roughly seven levels, each an inclusion-subclass over the level below with its own well-formedness rules.
Letters at the bottom — atomic referents. Single characters like x, P, !, =, |, >, (, ), A, E. Pure types, no internal structure. A signature partitions letters into roles — variable-letters, constant-letters, function-letters, predicate-letters, connective-letters, quantifier-letters, punctuation — but each role is just a set of letters.
Above letters, names — ordered inclusions of letters with multiplicity. Variables, constants, function symbols, predicate symbols are each their own inclusion-subclass with parsing rules (e.g., variable: starts with variable-letter, may continue with alphanumerics). Connectives are also names: =|> is the three-letter inclusion [=, |, >]; & is the single-letter inclusion [&]. Quantifiers A and E are single-letter names. All names share the same flavor of inclusion: ordered, multiplicity allowed.
Above names, terms — ordered inclusions that denote things in the domain. Atomic terms are variables or constants. Compound terms are a function-name followed by a parenthesized inclusion of terms: f(t1, t2, t3). Terms can include sub-terms, so the in/out dichotomy first appears here. Arity is a constraint inherited from the function-name.
Above terms, atomic formulas — predicate applications P(t1, ..., tn) and equalities t1 == t2. Ordered inclusions of names, terms, and equality. These are the base propositions: smallest things capable of being true or false.
Above atomic formulas, formulas — atomic formulas, negations !φ, binary combinations like φ & ψ, φ =|> ψ, and quantified formulas A[x]: φ, E[x]: φ. Deep nesting at this level — a formula can include subformulas of arbitrary depth — so this is where the in/out structure does its heaviest work. Quantifiers introduce binding scopes: the scope of A[x]: φ is just the sub-inclusion φ, and all x within that scope are bound by the quantifier.
Sentences are a sub-class of formulas: those with no free variables. Every variable sits within an enclosing quantifier scope.
Then two diverging structures above sentences. Theories are unordered set-like inclusions of sentences — uniqueness, no ordering. A theory has axioms (its specified base subset) and theorems (everything derivable from axioms via inference rules). Proofs are ordered inclusions of formulas-with-justifications — each step is a formula paired with a license (axiom, hypothesis, or "follows from earlier steps via inference rule R"). Before/after matters intensely in proofs; a step can only be justified by what came before. Inference rules themselves are a separate structural category: templates over formulas that license proof moves.
So the spine is letters → names → terms → atomic formulas → formulas → sentences, with sentences branching into theories (set-flavor) and proofs (ordered-flavor with justification). Each level above letters is an inclusion-subclass, and the well-formedness rules at each level ARE the production rules of the grammar.
One thing sits slightly outside the spine: signatures. A signature is itself a set-like inclusion of typed names — "these are the variable letters, these are the constant letters, these are the function and predicate symbols with their arities." Signatures parameterize everything from terms upward, fixing the alphabet a particular theory operates over. They're structural objects, but meta to any particular formula.

First-Order Logic — Standard Formal Specification
Alphabet. Fix a signature σ = (C, F, P) consisting of:
V — countably infinite set of variable symbols
C — set of constant symbols
F = ⋃_{n≥1} F_n — function symbols of arity n
P = ⋃_{n≥0} P_n — predicate symbols of arity n
Plus logical symbols: ¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔, ∀, ∃, =, and punctuation: ( , ) , comma.
Terms. Term(σ) is the smallest set such that:
v ∈ V ⟹ v ∈ Term(σ)
c ∈ C ⟹ c ∈ Term(σ)
f ∈ F_n, t₁,...,tₙ ∈ Term(σ) ⟹ f(t₁,...,tₙ) ∈ Term(σ)
Atomic formulas.
P ∈ P_n, t₁,...,tₙ ∈ Term(σ) ⟹ P(t₁,...,tₙ) is atomic
t₁, t₂ ∈ Term(σ) ⟹ t₁ = t₂ is atomic
Formulas. Form(σ) is the smallest set such that:
Every atomic formula is in Form(σ)
φ ∈ Form(σ) ⟹ ¬φ ∈ Form(σ)
φ, ψ ∈ Form(σ), ∘ ∈ {∧, ∨, →, ↔} ⟹ (φ ∘ ψ) ∈ Form(σ)
φ ∈ Form(σ), x ∈ V ⟹ ∀x φ, ∃x φ ∈ Form(σ)
Free variables. FV: Term(σ) ∪ Form(σ) → 𝒫(V), by induction:
FV(v) = {v}; FV(c) = ∅
FV(f(t₁,...,tₙ)) = ⋃ᵢ FV(tᵢ)
FV(P(t₁,...,tₙ)) = ⋃ᵢ FV(tᵢ); FV(t₁ = t₂) = FV(t₁) ∪ FV(t₂)
FV(¬φ) = FV(φ); FV(φ ∘ ψ) = FV(φ) ∪ FV(ψ)
FV(∀x φ) = FV(∃x φ) = FV(φ) ∖ {x}
Sentence. φ is a sentence iff FV(φ) = ∅. Sent(σ) ⊂ Form(σ).
Substitution. φ[t/x] denotes the result of replacing every free occurrence of x in φ by t. t is free for x in φ if no free variable of t becomes bound after substitution.
Theory. T ⊆ Sent(σ).
Hilbert proof system.
Logical axiom schemes:
(A1) φ → (ψ → φ)
(A2) (φ → (ψ → χ)) → ((φ → ψ) → (φ → χ))
(A3) (¬ψ → ¬φ) → (φ → ψ)
(A4) ∀x φ → φ[t/x], provided t is free for x in φ
(A5) ∀x (φ → ψ) → (φ → ∀x ψ), provided x ∉ FV(φ)
(Equality) x = x; x = y → (φ → φ[y/x])
(∃ defined: ∃x φ ≝ ¬∀x ¬φ)
Inference rules:
(MP) Modus Ponens: from φ and φ → ψ, infer ψ
(Gen) Generalization: from φ, infer ∀x φ
Derivation. A derivation of φ from T is a finite sequence φ₁,...,φₙ with φₙ = φ, where each φᵢ is (i) a logical axiom, (ii) an element of T, or (iii) obtained from earlier φⱼ's by MP or Gen. Write T ⊢ φ.
Structures. A σ-structure M = (D, I):
D ≠ ∅ — domain
I(c) ∈ D for c ∈ C
I(f) : Dⁿ → D for f ∈ F_n
I(P) ⊆ Dⁿ for P ∈ P_n
Variable assignment. s : V → D. Notation s[x ↦ d] denotes the assignment agreeing with s everywhere except x ↦ d.
Term denotation. ⟦t⟧ᴹ,ₛ ∈ D defined by:
⟦v⟧ᴹ,ₛ = s(v); ⟦c⟧ᴹ,ₛ = I(c)
⟦f(t₁,...,tₙ)⟧ᴹ,ₛ = I(f)(⟦t₁⟧ᴹ,ₛ,...,⟦tₙ⟧ᴹ,ₛ)
Satisfaction. M, s ⊨ φ defined by:
M, s ⊨ P(t₁,...,tₙ) iff (⟦t₁⟧,...,⟦tₙ⟧) ∈ I(P)
M, s ⊨ t₁ = t₂ iff ⟦t₁⟧ = ⟦t₂⟧
M, s ⊨ ¬φ iff M, s ⊭ φ
M, s ⊨ φ ∧ ψ iff M, s ⊨ φ and M, s ⊨ ψ (analogous for ∨, →, ↔)
M, s ⊨ ∀x φ iff for all d ∈ D, M, s[x ↦ d] ⊨ φ
M, s ⊨ ∃x φ iff for some d ∈ D, M, s[x ↦ d] ⊨ φ
T ⊨ φ iff every M with M ⊨ T also satisfies M ⊨ φ.
Metatheorems.
Soundness: T ⊢ φ ⟹ T ⊨ φ
Completeness (Gödel, 1929): T ⊨ φ ⟹ T ⊢ φ
Compactness: T is satisfiable iff every finite subset is satisfiable
Löwenheim–Skolem: a consistent T with infinite models has models of every infinite cardinality κ ≥ |σ| + ℵ₀
