A few solid fits in that ring around Batumi — far enough that a cab doesn't make sense, close enough for an easy day trip:
Shekvetili Dendrological Park (~45 km north, ~50 min) — probably the strongest pick for a 9- and 11-year-old. Opened in 2020 on 60 hectares, built by Bidzina Ivanishvili, with giant eucalyptus, sequoia, and ginkgo trees, plus lemurs, parrots, toucans, storks, and flamingos and pelicans on an island lake. Entry is free. The Tsitsinatela amusement park is right nearby if you want to stack an evening onto it, and Ureki's magnetic black-sand beach is a few minutes further — shallow, warm water with a gentle entry, good for kids.
Makhuntseti waterfall + Queen Tamara arch bridge (~30 km east toward Keda, ~40 min into the Adjarian mountains) — a 20 m waterfall where kids can swim in the small pool at the base in summer, plus the medieval stone arch bridge a short walk away. Gets crowded in peak season; go early.
Mtirala National Park (~30 km, ~1 hr, last stretch is rough road — genuinely car-required) — rainforest hiking trail to a waterfall and a small lake, and there's a zipline over the gorge. More active-day option if the kids like trails.
Honorable mention: Machakhela National Park (~1 hr) has a WWII-era bunker and mountain walks, which an 11-year-old might find cooler than another waterfall.
If I had to rank for those ages: Shekvetili dendropark (+Ureki beach combo) first, Makhuntseti second, Mtirala if they're hikers.

The drive. ~45–50 km north on the E70 coastal highway, about 50–60 minutes. It's flat, paved main road the whole way — the same highway that continues to Poti/Tbilisi — so no mountain driving. The one slowdown is summer traffic crawling through Kobuleti's strip. Everything below sits right on or just off that highway, within a ~10 km stretch of each other, so it chains into one loop naturally.
The dendropark (2–3 hours). Two zones: giant transplanted trees (sequoia, eucalyptus, bald cypress) and the birds/lemurs section. Honest expectations on the creatures: parrots, toucans, hyacinth macaws, peacocks, cranes are in aviaries right along the path, and the lemurs partly roam free — those are guaranteed, up-close, and reviewers consistently say kids were delighted by the lemurs and parrots. The island is different: the flamingos and scarlet ibises live on an island in the middle of the lake, and you can't get close — they're visible from a distance, best from the gazebo and the path near the lemur end. Some visitors saw only "pink dots far away" or didn't spot them at all. So: lemurs and parrots are the show; flamingos are scenery, not an encounter. Entry is free. One caveat: the park reportedly operates 5 days a week — I haven't verified which days it closes, so check before driving out. A nice detail: one exit leads straight onto the beach.
The magnetic sand beach (as long as you want — this is your anchor). Fine black magnetite sand, genuinely warm shallow water. For beach people with kids, note there are two options: Ureki is the famous one but it's a small dacha-village with no infrastructure beyond the beach and crowded in July–August. The Shekvetili beach right there has the same black magnetic sand, is cleaner and less crowded, with warm shallow water — arguably better with kids than Ureki, plus a gradual entry where depth only starts 50–100 m out, and pine forest along the shore. The sand is mildly magnetic — bring a magnet from home; a 9- and 11-year-old dragging a magnet through black sand and watching it grow a beard of iron filings is a legitimately great trick.
Tsitsinatela amusement park (2–3 hours, evening only). This is the key scheduling fact: it opens at 18:00 and runs to about midnight, June–September — it's a night park, all lit up (the name means "firefly"). It's Georgia's biggest amusement park: 8 rides for little kids, 17 family rides, 11 adult ones, plus bowling, karting-style cars, and arcade games; you buy a plastic card for 1 lari and load money onto it, tapping at each ride. Kids taller than 120–130 cm can ride most attractions, which covers both of yours. Rides are individually cheap (a few lari each). Caveat: in bad weather many rides shut down or the park may not open, so skip on a rainy forecast. <cite name="28-1" index="28-1">There's free parking right at the entrance off the E70</cite>.
The natural sequence: leave Batumi late morning → dendropark midday (shade under the giant trees, so heat-tolerable) → beach all afternoon → Tsitsinatela at 18:00 as it lights up → drive home ~21:00. Food is the weak link — <parameter name="none"></parameter>there's little beyond street food at Tsitsinatela, so bring a picnic and water, or eat properly in Kobuleti on the way.

You shouldn't be on a rugged road at all — the entrance is directly off the E70 highway itself, so if you're on dirt or a narrow lane, the GPS has routed you a back way. Turn around and get back on the main highway.
Here's the landmark chain coming from Batumi: stay on the E70 north, pass through Shekvetili village, pass the Black Sea Arena (the big concert hall — hard to miss) and the Music Park, and the turnoff is on the left (sea side) about 2.5 km after that, right past the Paragraph Resort hotel. There's a proper paved access road lined with tall thuja trees leading to the main entrance, with a free guarded parking lot.
If Google Maps keeps misbehaving, drop these coordinates in instead: 41.962494, 41.765830 — and just refuse any turn that takes you off the paved highway before you see the thuja-lined road.
