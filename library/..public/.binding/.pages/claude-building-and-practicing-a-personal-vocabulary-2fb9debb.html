Infuse vs. weave — close, but not quite synonyms
They overlap, but here's the difference:
infuse is about filling something with a quality — like a feeling, energy, or spirit. It's absorbed from the inside out. "She infused the lesson with enthusiasm." → the enthusiasm soaks through everything.
weave is about incorporating something skilfully into a structure — threading one thing through another. It's more about technique and craft. "She wove humour into her speech." → placed carefully in the right moments.
A simple test: you infuse tea (the flavour spreads through the water), but you weave threads into fabric (each one placed deliberately). Same idea applies to language.

The structures you love — let's name them first
These are called polite sequencing requests and they have two parts that work together:
Part 1 — the embedded question: "Could you check if everything looks good?" "Could you let me know if this makes sense?" "Could you tell me whether this sounds natural?"
Part 2 — the follow-up chain: "Once you've done that, could you also..." "If that works, could you then..." "After that, it would be great if you could..."
Native speakers use these constantly — in emails, in conversation, with colleagues, with friends. They sound confident and considerate at the same time. That's precisely why they feel so polished.
Your practice strategy
Think of it in three levels — each one builds on the previous:
Level 1 — Recognition (this week) Start noticing these structures in the wild. When you read English emails, articles, or even messages in this chat — pause when you spot a "Could you check if..." or "Once you've done that..." and say to yourself: "there it is." You can't deliberately use what you haven't consciously noticed yet. Add any new variations you find to your vocabulary journal under the category "useful structures".
Level 2 — Substitution (next week) Take a simple request you'd normally write — like "Check this please" — and deliberately rewrite it using the structure. Do this once a day. It doesn't need to be a real message. Just the exercise of translating a blunt request into a polished one trains the muscle. For example:
"Check this please" → "Could you check if this sounds natural? Once you've done that, could you also suggest a stronger ending?"
You can practise this with me every day. Just send me any rough request and I'll help you polish it into the structure.
Level 3 — Live use (ongoing) This is where it gets real. Every message you write to me, to Doug, to Giulia — before you send it, ask yourself: "Am I making a bare request, or a sequenced one?" If you catch a bare request, take ten seconds to infuse it with the structure. Over time it stops being a conscious effort and becomes your natural voice.

The "that" rule — finally explained
Here's the truth: "that" is optional in many English sentences, and native speakers almost always drop it in casual and professional writing. The rule is simple:
When "that" introduces a clause after verbs like *hope, think, know, say, feel, believe* — you can almost always cut it.
With "that"Without "that"
I hope that you feel betterI hope you feel better ✓
I think that it's readyI think it's ready ✓
I know that she's rightI know she's right ✓
The version with "that" is not wrong — it's just slightly formal and stiff. In everyday professional English, dropping it almost always sounds more natural.
When to KEEP "that" — when removing it makes the sentence confusing or ambiguous: "I told Doug that Hannah was sick" — here "that" helps separate the two people clearly. Dropping it can cause a brief moment of confusion.
A simple test: read the sentence aloud without "that" — if it flows naturally, cut it.
Add this to your grammar notebook — it will sharpen your writing immediately and deliberately! 🎯

Collocations vs. phrasal verbs
They look similar on the surface but they're quite different things:
Phrasal verbs are verbs combined with a preposition or adverb that create a completely new meaning — one you can't guess from the individual words.
"trip up" — "trip" + "up" → to make a mistake. Nothing to do with physical tripping or the direction up."fall through" — "fall" + "through" → a plan fails. Nothing to do with falling physically.
The meaning is unpredictable. You simply have to learn it as a new word.
Collocations are word pairs that sound natural together — not because they create a new meaning, but because native speakers simply always put them together. The meaning stays logical, but the combination is fixed by habit.
"shocked by" not "shocked with" — both feel logical, but only one is natural "make a mistake" not "do a mistake" — both feel logical, but native speakers always say "make" "give advice" not "say advice" — same logic, fixed combination
The simple test
Ask yourself: "Does the combination create a surprising new meaning?"
Yes → phrasal verb (fall through doesn't mean falling) No, it just sounds right → collocation (make a mistake, not do a mistake)
Both are worth tracking in your vocabulary journal under their own categories — you already have entries for each type. The more you collect them deliberately, the more your English will stop sounding translated and start sounding genuinely native. 🎯
