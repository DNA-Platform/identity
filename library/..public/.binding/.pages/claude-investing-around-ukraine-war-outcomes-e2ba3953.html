There's a fairly legible analyst landscape on both halves of this, and the market has already built explicit machinery around the second half — literal "peace baskets" and defense baskets that trade as mirror images on headlines. Here's the map.
On ending the war. Putin's stated terms have been static since mid-2024: no military blocs for Ukraine, non-nuclear status, recognition of Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson and Zaporizhzhia as Russian, and sanctions relief — and his recent framing of talks "based on the Istanbul agreements" is read by most analysts as a stalling tactic, since those 2022 terms would leave Ukraine defenseless and guarantee a Ukrainian no. The hard-line analytical view (Atlantic Council) is that Putin cannot accept any settlement that safeguards Ukrainian statehood, because Moscow would read it as historic defeat — making escalation his most rational path absent much greater Western pressure. The softer view is that the pressure is now real: Ukrainian strikes have knocked out roughly a fifth of Russia's refining capacity, taking it to its lowest level in 21 years, and Stanovaya argues Russia's military advantage is dissipating, with escalation the only response to a situation the Kremlin can't control. The most interesting tell: a February 2026 presentation inside Putin's own administration, shown to Kiriyenko, reportedly argued "one must know when to stop" — i.e., the Kremlin is internally workshopping how to sell a non-triumphant end as victory, while Levada polling shows 57–62% of Russians now favoring talks. The counter-pressure running the other way: Ukraine intercepted only 14 of 54 Russian ballistic missiles in June and none of 23 that hit Kyiv in early July, with US Patriot interceptor production at only 60–65/month and German-financed stocks not arriving before 2028 — so time pressure cuts both directions.
On Putin's removal. Near-consensus that it's low-probability while he lives: the regime is assessed as resilient to shocks, with only Putin's death or serious illness likely to collapse it; elites are fragmented, dependent on his arbitration, and the system's core weakness is the absence of any succession mechanism. OSW documents mounting erosion across economic, fiscal, social and military domains — and Western reports of removal plans circulating in May 2026 — but finds insufficient evidence of imminent elite revolt. Two non-obvious points worth holding: CSIS argues any removal effort would more likely emerge from a faction wanting to end the war than escalate it, and that the "successor would be worse" consensus may be unfounded; and FPRI notes that even a ceasefire is regime-threatening, because demobilizing a war economy unleashes expectations that wartime sacrifice can end.
The investable expression. The tape already shows you the elasticities. On a single strong peace headline in January: Ferrexpo +19%, Wizz Air +9%, Raiffeisen +6%, TUI +4%, materials names (Buzzi, Heidelberg, Rockwool) up ~3%, while defense fell 2.3% with Saab −4.3% and Rheinmetall −3.7%.
Fast-end / short-war expressions accessible to a US account:
Kyivstar (KYIV, Nasdaq) — the first Ukrainian company ever listed on Nasdaq (Aug 2025), ~$2.7B cap; the most direct pure-Ukraine equity you can buy in a US brokerage. Swarmer (Nasdaq, drone software) IPO'd March 2026 and rose 520% day one — interesting because it wins in both scenarios (long war and reconstruction-era defense).
Peace-basket Europeans via NYSE/ADR: CRH (NYSE), Raiffeisen, Heidelberg, Holcim, Wienerberger OTC. Raiffeisen is the asymmetric one — its trapped Russian subsidiary and frozen profits are essentially a call option on sanctions relief.
A dedicated Ukraine Reconstruction UCITS ETF (UKRN) launched on the LSE in March 2026 — UCITS wrappers aren't buyable through US brokers, but its VettaFi index is a useful free stock-screen. Total reconstruction is estimated at over $500B. Caution on Ferrexpo specifically: its owner Zhevago is wanted in an embezzlement case, it's running at roughly a quarter of prewar capacity, and it faced LSE suspension in May.
Long-war expressions:
European defense ADRs (Rheinmetall, BAE, Saab, Leonardo, Thales, Hensoldt/Renk OTC) — but here's the genuinely contested question: Morningstar and others argue the peace-headline selloffs are overstated because valuations rest on structural European budget increases, not Ukraine revenue, and rearmament won't reverse on a deal; Renk says the threat scenario is unchanged and the highest since the Cold War. If they're right, defense has headline beta but limited fundamental beta to war duration — meaning thepurer duration plays are the Ukraine-exposed small caps, which move in multiples rather than percents.
US LNG (Cheniere, Venture Global, Expand): the EU has now formally adopted law banning Russian LNG imports from the start of 2027 and pipeline gas from autumn 2027, and US exporters shipped ~59.5 Mt to the EU last year, up 60%+, supplying roughly 68% of Europe's LNG imports. Notably this is legislated, so it's mostly duration-insensitive — the scenario question is whether a durable peace eventually creates political pressure to repeal it.
Air-defense supply chain (RTX, Lockheed) given the interceptor-scarcity data above.
One non-obvious duration play from the same logic as Raiffeisen: overflight-dependent airlines (Finnair is the canonical example — its Asia route economics were built on Siberian airspace), where reopened Russian airspace is a step-function, though US access is thin OTC. This is information rather than a recommendation, of course — you'll price the scenarios yourself. A deeper investigation could build the full scenario-keyed matrix: every US-accessible ticker in the peace and long-war baskets, with each name's actual revenue/asset exposure to war duration rather than just its headline beta.

Yes — there's a real camp on the other side, and it's gotten considerably louder through 2026. It helps to first split what my earlier summary compressed: whether the regime survives and whether the war survives are different questions. The opposite-way camp mostly isn't predicting Putin falls — it's predicting continuation becomes unaffordable, which is a claim about the war.
The strongest versions of that case:
Craig Kennedy (the energy-finance analyst) argued in December that the new US/EU/UK sanctions would substantially cut Russia's oil revenues in 2026, amid what he called the worst crisis in Russia's energy sector since the 1990s. The fiscal-coupling version, laid out this month: Russia's Q1 2026 budget deficit exceeded the full-year target in just three months, fiscal buffers are spent, war capacity is now directly coupled to hydrocarbon export income — and absent an effective Russian counter to the growing strike campaign, the war economy hits financial crisis sooner rather than later. The Atlantic Council has been running the same thesis for months: with the front lines barely moving, the economic front may ultimately prove decisive — Putin stops when he concludes he can't win, and strikes plus sanctions are what produce that conclusion. Jamestown adds the structural point that makes the campaign scalable: Russian air defense has proven structurally incapable of protecting refineries dispersed across that much territory, turning the fuel crisis from regional disruptions into a chronic problem with political risk attached.
On the generalization you're pointing at — it's explicit and very recent. ISW counted at least 12 Wildberries warehouses struck since July 17, destroying more than half of its biggest distribution centers in about a week, and Zelensky framed it as direct reciprocity for Russian strikes on Nova Poshta, plus interdiction of dual-use components moving through the warehouses. And Ukraine's internal debate isn'twhether the economic campaign works — it's target selection. Omelchenko at Razumkov argues refinery strikes erode Russians' social and psychological stability but aren't sufficient alone, because the war budget runs on crude export revenue, not refining — so forcing the end requires generalizing to export logistics, Moscow, and Crimea. That's essentially Ukraine's stated theory of victory: strikes are sanctions Kyiv can enforce unilaterally, aimed at flipping Putin's cost calculus rather than toppling him.
The honest crux where the two camps actually meet: adaptation versus tempo. At the 2025 peak, 20% of refining capacity offline translated into only a 6% drop in refining volume — yet oil revenue still fell to near its lowest level of the entire war. Meduza's economists put the fuel shock at 1–1.5 points of added inflation, with the ultimate effect depending on whether Ukraine can sustain or intensify the pace, while RANE's measured counter is that strikes erode fiscal room but won't force a war scale-down in the short term. So the live variable is really a production race — Ukrainian deep-strike output growth against Russian repair and air-defense adaptation — which is a far more tractable thing to model than Kremlinology, and probably where the actual answer lives.
