Short version: Pollan's claim is real, plants genuinely are knocked out by anesthetics, and plants do have glutamate-receptor homologs — but the clean "xenon is an NMDA antagonist, so it must be hitting plant NMDA receptors" story doesn't hold up. Here's the nuance.
The Pollan attribution. It's from his 2026 book A World Appears: A Journey into Consciousness. He says more or less exactly what you remember: xenon is chemically inert, yet it puts both us and plants "out," which he treats as part of the deep, poorly-understood mystery of anesthesia. His go-to example is the anesthetized Venus flytrap that no longer closes on an insect — and his provocative framing is "if an anesthetic removes consciousness in animals, what is it removing in a plant?"
Are plants actually affected? Yes, and it's well-replicated. The anchor paper is Yokawa, Pavlovič, Mancuso & Baluška (Annals of Botany, 2018): diethyl ether immobilized Venus flytraps and pea tendrils, blocked the flytrap action potential, and also impeded seed germination, chlorophyll accumulation, and endocytic vesicle recycling/ROS balance in Arabidopsis roots. One caveat worth flagging as a neuroscientist: the robust plant work usesdiethyl ether (plus lidocaine, and now ketamine — there's a 2024 Pavlovič paper on ketamine blocking Ca²⁺/electrical signaling in Arabidopsis). I did not find a dedicated xenon-on-plants experiment. So Pollan's specific "xenon puts plants out" is plausibly an extrapolation from the broader anesthetic literature rather than a documented xenon trial. (And your "natures" is almost certainly nitrous — N₂O, xenon, ketamine all being NMDA antagonists.)
Do plants have something NMDA-like? Sort of, and this is the genuinely interesting part. Plants have GLRs (glutamate receptor-like channels) — Arabidopsis has 20 — and they are bona fide homologs of the animal ionotropic glutamate receptor superfamily, the same family that diversified into NMDA/AMPA/kainate in animals. They're ligand-gated, calcium-permeable cation channels with the conserved iGluR domain architecture. There's even a real NMDA-flavored detail: glycine has been shown to play a role in gating plant "NMDA-like" receptors (Dubos 2003), echoing the glycine co-agonist requirement of mammalian NMDARs.
But they are not NMDA receptors. Two big divergences: plant GLRs are a separately radiated branch (closer in some analyses to ancestral K⁺-type channels), and their ligand specificity is far broader — a single GLR can be gated by as many as seven different amino acids, versus the tight glutamate/glycine/D-serine selectivity of an NMDAR. Functionally, GLR3.3 and GLR3.6 carry the wound-induced, glutamate-triggered long-distance Ca²⁺/electrical wave (Mousavi 2013, Toyota 2018) — the closest thing plants have to a propagating "nervous" signal.
The mechanism punchline. The transferable-mechanism story is weak in both directions. On the plant side, the commentary literature notes that while anesthetics probably do target glutamate receptors in plants as in animals, the only known plant effect is on the slow-wave/electrical wound signal — which animals don't even produce, and ether's plant effects implicate a whole convergent set of targets (anion channels, K⁺ channels, Ca²⁺ channels, vesicle trafficking), not a discrete NMDAR block. On the animal side, xenon's anesthesia isn't purely NMDA either — it also acts on TREK-1 two-pore K⁺ channels, and there's even exotic work proposing an electron-spin/radical-pair contribution. So the honest read: anesthetics shut down plant excitability through broad effects on membrane and channel dynamics (GLRs plausibly among them), but "xenon anesthetizes plants via NMDA-receptor antagonism" isn't supported — plants don't have the receptor that mechanism requires.
Worth noting this whole area (plant "neurobiology," anesthesia-as-consciousness-probe) is scientifically contested — Baluška/Mancuso are at the assertive end, and many plant physiologists push back hard on the consciousness framing while accepting the electrophysiology. Taking your three questions in order, because the answers diverge sharply — and the divergence is the interesting part.
Coincidence detection. This is the sharpest question, because the Mg²⁺-block AND-gate is what actually makes the NMDAR special. And the honest answer is no — plant GLRs are not documented to have it. The NMDAR's coincidence detection comes from a voltage-dependent Mg²⁺ block of the pore that's relieved by depolarization, so calcium only flows when strong depolarization coincides with glutamate binding — a genuine molecular coincidence detector. That property is pinned to a specific asparagine at the Q/R/N site in the M2 pore loop; change those residues and you lose both Mg²⁺ sensitivity and high Ca²⁺ permeability. Plant GLRs are characterized as the ligand-gated group precisely in contrast to voltage-gated channels — "plants do not solely rely on membrane voltages to activate ion channels" — and the GLR3.4 structure shows a non-swapped domain arrangement and distinct gating features that set it apart from the rest of the iGluR family. So the single-channel AND-gate isn't there.
But two caveats keep it from being a flat "no." First, the property is tunable within the family: you can engineer an AMPA receptor into an NMDA-like coincidence detector just by swapping in the N-site asparagine, at which point it picks up voltage-dependent block by MK-801, memantine, and ketamine at NMDA-like affinities. Nobody (that I can find) has shown a plant GLR sitting at that configuration, but the knob exists in the shared architecture. Second — and more interesting — coincidence detection in plants seems to be implemented at the cell/system level rather than the channel level. The flytrap is the clean case (below): it requires two action potentials within a time window to act, which is temporal coincidence detection, just built from calcium integration plus a threshold instead of a Mg-gated pore. The computation is conserved; the implementation isn't.
Memory, even if not LTP. Yes, and the flytrap is the textbook example. A first action potential raises cytosolic calcium below threshold; a second within ~30 seconds sums onto it and crosses the threshold to snap the trap — and if the second is too late, the first is erased. Hedrich and Neher explicitly framed this as a cellular "calcium clock" underlying the flytrap's short-term memory and counting, later confirmed by GCaMP imaging and by a "DYSCALCULIA" mutant that loses the ability to count action potentials. Here's the part you'll appreciate: this isn't really analogous to LTP — it's analogous to short-term synaptic facilitation, which in neurons is also a residual-calcium-decay memory. Same computational primitive (a leaky integrator written in free Ca²⁺), arrived at independently.
There's also a faint receptor-level memory: GLRs show a desensitization hierarchy where glutamate desensitizes the depolarization response to glycine, but glycine does not desensitize it to glutamate — so the channel's current responsiveness depends on its recent ligand history. That's adaptation/state-dependence, not Hebbian potentiation, but it's a real form of order-sensitivity. For genuinely long-term memory, plants use other substrates entirely — defense priming (the systemic GLR3.3/3.6 wound wave sets up a primed state for faster future responses) and chromatin-based memory like vernalization. None of that is GLR-intrinsic, and none of it is a use-dependent potentiation of a specific transmission pathway. There's no demonstrated plant LTP. (The Gagliano Mimosa habituation work claims weeks-long learned non-response, but it's contested and hasn't replicated cleanly, so I'd hold it loosely.)
Homomorphism — and your lineage intuition. Your instinct is right and can be pushed further back than the eukaryotic ancestor. The iGluR is a modular fusion: a ligand-binding domain derived from bacterial periplasmic amino-acid-binding proteins — a fold literally nicknamed the "Venus flytrap" domain for how it clamps shut on its ligand — bolted onto an inverted, pruned K⁺-channel pore. That architecture predates the plant/animal split; cyanobacteria have GluR0, a prokaryotic iGluR with a K⁺-selective filter. So the hardware is true homology, not convergence. (The fact that the actual Venus flytrap runs its counting on receptors built from the "Venus flytrap" fold is a coincidence I find hard not to enjoy.) The pore transplant work nails the homology functionally — a plant GLR pore region dropped into a mammalian iGluR scaffold still conducts Na⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺.
So the mapping is layered. At the molecular level: genuine structural homology — same ancestral machine. At the signaling level: strong convergent analogy — all-or-none APs, propagating glutamate-triggered Ca²⁺ waves, thresholds, refractory periods, systemic long-distance signaling. At the computational level: partial overlap — temporal integration, coincidence-over-time, state-dependence. Where the homomorphism breaks is exactly the nervous-system-specific elaborations: no synapses, no fast directional point-to-point transmission, no Mg-block Hebbian rule, mostly glacial timescales (Arabidopsis APs last over a minute and can't refire quickly — the flytrap's ~1 Hz is the exception), and the "wiring" is vasculature, not axons. So the cleanest way to say it: it's a homomorphism, not an isomorphism — homologous parts wired into a convergently-evolved architecture that runs a different, slower, non-synaptic computation. The deep conserved logic is "glutamate as a damage/status signal that gates calcium," which turns out to be eukaryote-wide; animals layered the synapse and the coincidence-detecting pore on top of it.
The thing I'd genuinely flag as open: whether any plant GLR has a meaningful voltage-dependent divalent block has, as far as I can tell, not been carefully measured for most of the 20 Arabidopsis paralogs — most electrophysiology is on a handful (3.3, 3.4, 3.6). So "plants don't do NMDA-style coincidence detection" is better stated as "it hasn't been found and the consensus model doesn't predict it," not "it's been ruled out." Want me to dig into the actual pore-residue alignment — whether any plant GLR carries the N-site asparagine — since that's the one residue that would settle it?

Both of your pushes land. Let me take them separately, because they pull in opposite directions — one tightens a disanalogy, the other opens up a real analogy I was too narrow to see.
No, it is not a Na⁺/K⁺ action potential — you're right to catch me. I was using "action potential" at the level of the phenomenon (regenerative, all-or-none, thresholded, self-propagating) without flagging that the ionic engine is almost entirely different. In a neuron the upstroke is Na⁺ influx through voltage-gated Naᵥ channels. Plants don't do that: the depolarizing phase is not mediated by Na⁺ channels — there's no suitable Na⁺ gradient and Na⁺ is toxic to most plant cells — so depolarization is carried by Cl⁻ efflux and Ca²⁺ influx instead. Repolarization is K⁺ efflux, which is shared with neurons, but even the housekeeping differs: plants restore the resting potential using an H⁺-ATPase rather than the animal Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. So the resting potential itself is a proton-pump potential, not a Na/K one.
The history here is almost too on-the-nose: Cole and Curtis, the pioneers of squid-axon excitability, studied the alga Nitella side by side with the squid — and found that Nitella and Chara release chloride to depolarize and use K⁺ to recover, with anions rather than sodium initiating the upstroke. So excitability was characterized in plant and animal in the same breath, and they already diverged at the ions. This sharpens the earlier framing: even the action potential is convergence, not homology — two lineages independently discovering that a regenerative membrane event is a good way to broadcast a signal, built from whatever ions were cheap and non-toxic. K⁺ efflux is the one genuinely conserved piece. The GLRs are the homologous parts; the AP is a convergent computation.
On coincidence detection — you're right that I was being parochial, and the reframe is the better question. Here's the thing I should have led with: the NMDAR is actually a two-layer coincidence detector. The famous Mg²⁺/voltage layer sits on top of a more basic one — obligateco-agonism. The NMDA tetramer needs two ligands at once: two subunits bind glycine, two bind glutamate, and the channel only opens when both are present. That bottom layer — "detect Glu AND a second ligand simultaneously" — is exactly your "Glu and whatever," and it's the layer worth asking about in plants, because it doesn't need voltage at all.
And the plant architecture is genuinely suggestive here. Plant GLRs assemble as heterotetramers of subunits with different amino-acid preferences, and the binding pockets are wildly promiscuous — at least a dozen amino acids act as GLR agonists, including glutamate, glycine, D-serine, methionine, cysteine, asparagine, and others, a promiscuity never observed in animal iGluRs, with the pocket using water molecules to accommodate differently sized side chains. Crucially, different subunit combinations have different ligand logic — a GLR3.1/3.5 heteromer behaves as a methionine-activated Ca²⁺ channel, distinct from the glutamate-driven 3.3/3.6 wound channel. Put heteromeric assembly together with subunit-specific ligands and you have thesubstrate for exactly the combinatorial AND-gate you're describing: a channel that fires only when a particular pair of amino acids is co-present. Plus the desensitization asymmetry from before (glutamate silences the glycine response but not vice versa) means the subunits already interact in an order-dependent, history-sensitive way.
The honest caveat: I can't point to a plant GLR that's been proven to be an obligate two-different-ligand coincidence detector the way the NMDAR's Glu+Gly requirement is proven. What's established is heteromeric assembly, promiscuous single-ligand gating, and subunit-specific profiles — the ingredients, not the demonstrated AND-gate. So this is a real frontier rather than a settled fact, and your instinct is pointing straight at the gap. (Worth noting the pharmacology partly transfers, too: plant GLR1.4 is antagonized by MK-801 and DNQX, albeit only at high concentrations — so the open-channel-blocker chemistry that ties back to your original ketamine thread does touch plant GLRs.)
Now zoom all the way out, which is where your "slower scales, not even voltage" point really pays off — because plants are arguably more committed to molecular coincidence detection than animals, just biochemically rather than electrically. The cleanest examples are the hormone "molecular glue" coreceptors. Auxin perception is a literal AND-gate: the TIR1 receptor only acts when auxin and its Aux/IAA substrate are simultaneously docked — auxin is sensed only as the coincidence of the two, not by either alone. Jasmonate works identically (COI1 + JAZ + the JA-Ile ligand — the same COI1/JAZ module that runs the flytrap's digestive program). Above that sit combinatorial Ca²⁺ signatures — stimulus-specific frequency/amplitude codes read out by CDPK and CBL-CIPK decoders — and combinatorial promoter logic where defense genes fire only on the conjunction of jasmonate + ethylene signals. And priming is coincidence detection stretched across days to weeks: a first cue leaves an epigenetic mark so that a later second cue produces an amplified response.
So the corrected picture: plants substitute slow, biochemical, combinatorial coincidence logic for the fast, voltage-gated coincidence wiring of a synapse. The NMDAR's trick is to collapse a coincidence computation into a single channel on a millisecond timescale; plants spread the same logic across heteromeric receptors, glue-type coreceptors, calcium codes, and chromatin, on timescales from seconds to weeks. The function you're asking about is everywhere — it's the voltage-based, single-molecule, millisecond implementation of it that's the animal specialization.
The genuinely open question your framing surfaces — and I'd flag it as the actually-interesting unknown — is whether plants ever use the homologous GLR hardware as a multi-ligand coincidence detector, or whether they always farm coincidence out to the hormone/transcription machinery and leave the GLRs as broad, promiscuous "amino-acids-are-present" sensors. Nobody seems to have tested a heteromeric GLR for obligate dual-ligand gating head-on. Want me to chase down whether the GLR3.3/3.6 or 3.1/3.5 heteromer reconstitutions actually probed two-ligand requirements, versus just cataloguing single agonists?

You're right to call the smear — and the honest answer is that "which cells" has a real, mapped answer that I should have led with. Plants are emphatically not a sheet of identical neurons. Excitability is localized, the time course is ~1000× slower, the memory has a known molecular substrate, and the architecture is neither a nervous system nor uniformly diffuse. Let me take those in order.
Which cells, and the time course. In the algae you mentioned, the AP is a property of single giant internodal cells — the classic Chara/Nitella prep is one cell, centimeters long. And its job isn't network computation: the Chara AP stops cytoplasmic streaming in response to a mechanical stimulus — a single-cell wound/protective reflex, not a wire between cells. The time course is nothing like a neuron: Chara APs fire from a very hyperpolarized resting potential and are several seconds long, all-or-none like the animal AP — but resting potential is around −183 mV with the AP peaking near −5 mV, so it's a ~180 mV event lasting seconds, versus a neuron's ~100 mV event lasting ~1–2 ms. The Cl⁻/K⁺ current transients underlying it have a half-maximal duration of roughly one second. So: bigger, ~1000× slower, different ions. Same logic (threshold, all-or-none, refractory period), different physics.
In a vascular plant, the long-distance wound signal (the "slow wave potential") is carried by specific cells in the vasculature, and this got mapped quite precisely. GLR3.3 is expressed mainly in the phloem, GLR3.6 in the xylem region, and only double mutants removing GLRs from both cell types strongly knock out leaf-to-leaf signaling. Sharper still: GLR3.3 localizes to ER in phloem sieve elements while GLR3.6 sits in xylem contact cells, and a 2024 study showed phloem companion cells are critical, having to continuously renew GLR3.3, making the GLR3.3 response phloem-specific. So the conductive tissue is the phloem sieve-element/companion-cell complex plus xylem-associated cells — and GLR3.3 also shows up in stomatal guard cells and trichome base cells, i.e., other locally excitable spots. The flytrap and Mimosa add dedicated excitable organs (trap epidermis, trigger-hair podium, the pulvinus motor organ). The point: a minority of cells, anatomically channeled.
The substrate of memory — yes, you can point to it. This is the satisfying part, because plant memory has more molecular precision than most synaptic memory does, just at a different substrate. Three timescales:
Short-term is the flytrap's calcium clock from earlier — the memory trace is literally residual cytosolic Ca²⁺ that hasn't yet been pumped back down, decaying over ~30 seconds. That's the tightest neural analogy in the whole conversation: it's mechanistically the same as short-term synaptic facilitation, which is also residual-Ca²⁺-based. Not LTP — facilitation.
Long-term is where you can put your finger on a molecule. Vernalization is the gold standard: prolonged cold triggers Polycomb silencing of the FLC locus via the repressive mark H3K27me3, and that silencing is stably maintained after return to warmth. The memory is a chromatin mark at one gene, and crucially it's physically copied through cell division — the H3K27me3 Polycomb domain at FLC is inherited from parent to daughter chromatin at the DNA replication fork via CAF-1, LHP1, and PRC2, conferring a winter-cold memory that's mitotically stable but meiotically reset. And it's quantitative in a way that's almost digital: longer cold produces more stable silencing because the switch is per-cell and stochastic, so the plant "counts" winter as the fraction of cells that have flipped to the silent state. So the substrate of this memory is a specific histone modification at a specific locus, replicated mitotically — an engram written in chromatin, read out months later as the decision to flower. (Pathogen-defense priming sits in between — a primed plant responds faster to a second attack via persistent histone marks at defense promoters and pre-accumulated inactive signaling kinases. That one is genuinely plasticity-like in the functional sense: experience produces a lasting increase in a pathway's responsiveness. Still chromatin and biochemistry, not synaptic weights.)
So the clean statement: plants have no synaptic-weight analog because they have no synapses, but they robustly satisfy the functional definition of plasticity — a use-dependent, persistent change in future response — and the substrate is chromatin state (long-term) and calcium kinetics (short-term), not connection strengths.
Nervous system, or diffuse? Neither, exactly — it's a channeled, decentralized system. No neurons, no synapses, no axons, no brain or ganglion, no cells specialized solely for conduction. But not a uniform diffuse sheet either: there's a privileged anatomical conduit. The phloem is built from sieve elements joined end-to-end by sieve plates into a continuous sieve-tube system — a low-resistance, symplastically continuous cable that the plant uses for sugar transport and repurposes as an electrical/calcium signaling highway. Around that, cells are coupled locally by plasmodesmata into a quasi-syncytium, so signaling is diffuse at short range and channeled at long range. The provocative "plant neurobiology" camp (Baluška, Mancuso) calls this a nerve-like network and floats the root apex as a "command center," but most plant physiologists accept the vascular electrical network while rejecting the literal brain framing — and I'd land there too.
The cleanest way I can put the whole arc: it's a decentralized signaling system that runs on homologous receptor hardware, a convergently-evolved (different-ion, far slower) action potential, biochemically-implemented coincidence detection, and a chromatin-based memory — channeled through transport tissue rather than wired through neurons. Homologous parts, analogous architecture, no central integrator.
The genuinely live debate your question lands on: whether the phloem electrical network does any integration — combining signals, not just relaying them — or whether all the integration happens at the slow chromatin/hormone layer and the electrical layer is pure broadcast. That's unsettled. Want me to look at whether anyone's shown the SWP doing something computational (summation, gating, logic) in the vasculature, versus just propagating?

Both questions are pointing at the same gap in my account — I've described signals traveling but not how they cross cells or whether traffic ever reshapes the road. Here's both.
The cross-cell carrier — you're right that there's chemistry, but it's not one synaptic transmitter. The plant does two things at once. First, unlike neurons, plant cells are often directly continuous: plasmodesmata (and the big sieve-plate pores joining phloem cells) make them electrically coupled, so a depolarization can spread cell-to-cell without any transmitter — closer to a gap junction or syncytium than to a chemical synapse. But layered on top of that direct coupling is exactly the chemical relay your intuition demands, and it's a quartet of mutually-regenerating messengers rather than one. The most neurotransmitter-like is glutamate itself: glutamate released from wounded cells triggers systemic calcium and ROS waves in unwounded tissue through GLRs and the NADPH oxidase RBOHD. Alongside it run a self-propagating ROS wave — calcium-activated RBOHD makes ROS, which triggers more calcium and more ROS, propagating the signal — plus extracellular ATP, where wound-released eATP is sensed by the P2K receptor to trigger ROS. That eATP→P2K step is purinergic signaling, molecularly echoing animal purinergic transmission, just as the glutamate→GLR step echoes glutamatergic transmission. So: direct electrical coupling for local spread, regenerating chemical waves (glutamate, ROS, Ca²⁺, eATP) for amplification and distance. The synapse-like chemistry is real; it's deployed as a traveling wave through the cell wall rather than across a cleft.
Now the real question — a memory that changes the communication substrate. You're right to set the DNA memory aside for this: vernalization is a register write. It stores a bit at FLC and reads it out as one decision (flower / don't); it doesn't touch how cells talk to each other. What you're describing — use feeding back to reshape the wiring — does exist in plants, and it has a name: auxin canalization. It's the closest thing to Hebbian structural plasticity in all of botany.
The logic is a positive feedback between a signal and its own conduit. Auxin moves cell-to-cell through polar PIN efflux carriers, and the founding idea (Sachs) is that auxin promotes the formation of its own directional transport routes — auxin flux feeds back to rearrange the polar localization of PIN exporters in the direction of flow. More flux polarizes more PIN, which carries more flux: this positive feedback gradually narrows the file of PIN-polarized cells and canalizes diffuse auxin transport into discrete channels. Those channels then differentiate into actual vascular strands. So the substrate physically grows along the path that carried the most signal — "what fluxes together, ducts together." And it's genuinely adaptive, not just developmental: after a stem wound, the auxin response rises around the wound and PIN1-marked transport channels form from previously homogeneous tissue, with the gradual PIN repolarization preceding the new vascular tissue — the plant reroutes and regrows its plumbing around damage, with about 90% of wild-type plants regenerating vascular strands around a stem incision. That's a memory of where signal flowed, written into the architecture of the network itself, via TIR1/AFB auxin perception and ARF-dependent PIN relocation.
There's a faster, reversible version too: the plasmodesmata are not fixed pipes but gated valves. ROS widens plasmodesmata pores via the proteins PDLP1 and PDLP5 to let the systemic signal through, while callose accumulation at plasmodesmata closes them and limits the calcium signal. So the conductance of existing cell-to-cell links is dynamically tuned by the very signals passing through — gain control on the wiring, on a timescale of minutes to hours, that can be left in a persistently altered state after a defense episode.
The honest boundary: this structural plasticity lives at the slow developmental layer (growing veins) and the medium layer (gating plasmodesmata), not in the fast electrical signal itself. Nobody has shown the wound electrical wave getting bigger or faster with repeated use via a lasting change in its channels — there's no demonstrated electrical LTP. The plant's "rewiring" is morphological and biochemical, not a potentiated synaptic weight.
Which is, I think, the actual answer to "what does the memory do" — and it exposes a deep difference from animals. In a brain, the communication network and the body are largely separable: synapses change weight without the animal changing shape. In a plant, the communication substrate is the growing body. The vasculature is simultaneously the plumbing, the structural skeleton, and the signaling cable, and it is continuously rebuilt by the signals it carries.
