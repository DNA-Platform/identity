Here's what the research in Georgian sources shows, synthesized with the legal framework:
What Georgian law actually requires as evidence
Georgia's Law on Prevention of Domestic Violence explicitly defines domestic violence to include physical, psychological, economic, and sexual violence or coercion by one family member against another. Sexual violence is named directly in the statute — it is not treated as a special category requiring a higher burden of proof than other forms of abuse.
More importantly for your situation: in a civil protection order hearing in Georgia, the petitioner must prove their case by a preponderance of the evidence — meaning more likely than not. This is a significantly lower standard than the "beyond reasonable doubt" required in criminal cases. No police report, no criminal record, and no forensic documentation are required to meet that civil standard.
What Georgian courts actually look at
The PHR (Partnership for Human Rights) case from 2021 is the most instructive real precedent I found. In that case, the Georgian court issued a three-month protective order against a father and paternal grandmother, prohibiting all contact with the child, based on the findings of a social worker and psychologist — without any criminal record of abuse. The court relied on the psychological and social work assessments showing the father was engaged in parental alienation and emotional manipulation of the child.
This is directly relevant. The Georgian evidentiary standard in custody and protection proceedings relies heavily on: testimony, psychological evaluations, social worker reports, and behavioral evidence in proceedings — not on whether a crime was formally charged in another country.
The Istanbul Convention dimension
Georgia ratified the Council of Europe's Istanbul Convention (Convention on Preventing and Combating Violence Against Women) in 2017. This is significant because the convention explicitly requires that states create procedures allowing victims to claim protection without having previously filed criminal reports, and it requires courts to consider patterns of coercive control and sexual violence even in civil proceedings. A Georgian lawyer can invoke this framework directly.
How the Russian civil record functions here
The fact that the abuse was raised and substantiated — even partially — in the Russian civil custody record is not nothing. It's a foreign court document. Several important things come out of this research, including one significant development that may already affect Ana's application.
The law changed on October 1, 2025
Georgia implemented a new Law on International Protection as of October 1, 2025. The new law introduces differentiated processing tracks, shorter appeal timeframes, and changes to access to legal aid and protection from removal depending on which procedure an application is assigned to. If Ana's application was filed before that date, it may be governed by the old law. If filed after, the new framework applies. Your Georgian lawyers need to be clear on which regime she's in, because the procedural rules differ.
What the status actually requires
Georgia's law defines a humanitarian status holder as someone ineligible for full refugee status but facing risks such as torture, or severe threats in their home country due to violence or human rights violations. Ana's case — documented domestic violence, sexual abuse, and an adversarial co-parent with legal power over her in Russia — fits squarely within the humanitarian (now "subsidiary protection") category, not refugee status. This is actually the right category to target.
The process itself
The application goes to the Migration Department of the Ministry of Internal Affairs. The standard review timeline is 6 months, extendable to 9 months for complex cases, with a maximum total administrative procedure of 21 months from submission.
All information about the applicant and their application is confidential and will not be shared with media, other organizations, Georgian citizens, or state agencies and citizens of the country of origin without written consent. That last point is directly relevant — Georgian authorities cannot share Ana's information with Russian authorities.
Cost
The initial application process has no cost implications. The state filing is free. Legal representation is the cost. UNHCR Georgia provides some free legal aid for asylum seekers, as does the Legal Aid Service of Georgia. However, for a case of this complexity — where the documentation needs to be built correctly, the Russian record needs to be presented effectively, and the Istanbul Convention arguments need to be made — relying solely on free public services is a risk. A competent private Georgian lawyer specializing in international protection would likely run $1,500–$4,000 for the full application and interview preparation, possibly more if complications arise.
The approval rate reality
This is the part that warrants directness. In the first nine months of 2025, Georgia granted humanitarian or refugee status to 31 people, while rejecting 1,030 applications. The overall grant rate is low. Georgia is tightening, not loosening. The cases that succeed are those with well-documented, specific, credible personal risk — which Ana's case is — but the documentation has to be assembled carefully and presented compellingly. A generic application will not succeed. This is exactly why competent legal representation for the application itself, not just for potential defensive proceedings, matters.
The bottom line on cost: the application is free, competent legal representation for the application runs a few thousand dollars, and the timeline is 6–21 months under the old law, potentially different under the new one. The application being already in process is an advantage — but the quality of documentation in the file now determines the outcome.
