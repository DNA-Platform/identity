Short answer: routing LLC revenue to your personal account defeats the entire structure. The US-co-pays-Georgian-LLC setup is the standard play, and the tax math depends heavily on which regime you qualify for.
On personal-account routing (PayPal or crypto). Don't. The whole point of the LLC is that the LLC has 50K GEL of turnover for the TRP lookback. Money landing in your personal Credo account isn't LLC turnover — it's personal income. Worse, if the LLC bills the work but the cash lands with you, that's textbook hidden profit distribution: the taxable base is broadly defined to cover dividends in cash or in kind, as well as certain transactions with related or tax-exempt parties that deviate from market conditions, and transfer pricing adjustments may give rise to deemed profit distributions. Same answer for crypto — receiving business income as USDT is still business income; the LLC must book it. PayPal in Georgia has also historically been receive-limited (mostly send-only for personal accounts), worth verifying with Credo/your accountant if you're counting on it.
The US company → Georgian LLC structure. This is the standard intermediary play. Your US LLC contracts with your Georgian LLC for services, pays the Georgian LLC, which becomes its turnover. Then the Georgian LLC pays you via salary, dividend, or retained profit. Tax stack at each layer:
Georgian LLC, standard regime: Under the Estonian-style CIT system, corporate profits are not taxed when earned — only when distributed. The 15% CIT rate applies only upon dividend payment; dividends to individuals then attract a further 5% withholding tax. Effective on distribution: ~18.6% (the 15% is grossed up — a dividend of GEL 85,000 triggers a tax liability of GEL 15,000). Reinvested = 0%.
Virtual Zone status (if your work is IT services to foreign clients): You don't pay corporate profit tax at all even when distributing profits. Dividends are taxed at 5%. No VAT on foreign invoices. This is the headline IT regime — narrowly scoped to digital products/services to non-Georgian clients.
International Company status: 5% CIT on distribution, 5% PIT on salaries to employees of the international company, 0% withholding on dividends. Requires IT or maritime sector, real substance, and two years of prior IT activity (in Georgia or abroad, though in the second case the controlling interest must be owned by the foreign partner). Your ~1-year-old US company doesn't clear that yet but might in another year.
Salary route: If you pay yourself a salary as a non-resident, 20% PIT. As a resident (post-TRP, post-183 days), 20% PIT plus 2%+2% pension. Salary is deductible against the LLC's distributable profit base.
Three things that will trip you up if not handled deliberately.
Transfer pricing. Both entities are wholly yours. If the US LLC pays the Georgian LLC a price that doesn't look like arm's-length compensation for the services rendered, the Revenue Service can recharacterize the excess as a deemed distribution and tax it. Get a defensible service agreement with market-rate pricing documented.
US-side tax — and this is where I'd push hard for a US international tax accountant, not just ExpatHub. A Georgian LLC owned 100% by a US person is likely a Controlled Foreign Corporation under US rules, with Form 5471 reporting and potentially GILTI/Subpart F implications, depending on classification elections. This is genuinely outside what a Georgian-side firm will handle competently — wrong answers here cost real money.
The 12-month lookback hasn't gone away. Even with the US co paying the Georgian LLC, you still need 12 months of ≥50K GEL turnoverin the Georgian LLC before the TRP application. Starting that revenue clock immediately is the whole game.
The cleanest version of this for your situation is probably: incorporate the Georgian LLC now, sign a services agreement with your US co at arm's-length pricing, route US-co revenue through it monthly to build the track record, retain profits inside the Georgian LLC during the build year (0% CIT), then revisit Virtual Zone or International Company status once you see what your actual activity profile looks like. But which regime you should actually elect is the kind of question I'd want ExpatHub and a US-side international tax person both signing off on, not me.

Bottom line: the lab contracts with the Georgian LLC, not you. If the work happens entirely in Georgia, the US side reports almost nothing, the Georgian side reports everything, and the IRS reconciles via FATCA and your personal filings.
What the lab does on the US side.
Before paying you a dime, their accounts payable office needs a Form W-8BEN-E from the Georgian LLC (the entity-version W-8). On it, the LLC certifies it's a foreign person and provides its Georgian tax ID. The lab keeps it on file; nothing gets sent to the IRS at this stage.
Then the magic of the sourcing rule: As long as the foreign service provider is performing all of the services outside of the U.S., there is no 1099 or withholding requirement for the domestic U.S. payor. Personal-services income is sourced where the work happens, not where the payer sits. You doing research from Tbilisi for a Stanford lab = foreign-source income.
So: foreign-source services to a nonresident are normally not reportable. No 1099 (those are for US persons only — issuing a 1099 to a foreign person is incorrect and could lead to backup withholding violations). No 1042-S (that's for foreign persons receiving US-source income). No 30% NRA withholding. The lab's accounting treats the payment as a foreign vendor expense, deductible on their books, and that's it.
The piece they care about: the W-8BEN-E. Get it right, sign it, send it. They'll likely also have an internal vendor onboarding form.
What the Georgian LLC reports to Georgia.
The LLC files monthly tax declarations through rs.ge showing turnover. The university's payments hit the Credo corporate account in USD; the bank reports those to the Revenue Service automatically (the Revenue Service can see your account). VAT on services to foreign clients is 0% (reverse-charge / export of services). At year-end nothing dramatic happens — under the Estonian model, no CIT until you distribute. When you do pay yourself (salary or dividend), the LLC withholds the relevant tax at source: 20% PIT on salary, 5% on dividends.
For your TRP application later, you pull the Revenue Service turnover certificate that proves the 50K GEL/12-month threshold.
What you personally report to the US.
This is where the real complexity lives, because you're a US citizen owning a foreign corporation:
Form 5471 with your 1040 every year — Controlled Foreign Corporation reporting. Substantial form, substantial penalties for mistakes ($10K minimum for late or incomplete filing).
FBAR (FinCEN 114) if total balances across your foreign accounts hit $10K at any point. Personal Credo + corporate Credo both count. Form 8938 applies when specified assets exceed $200,000 for singles or $400,000 for joint filers living abroad.
GILTI / Subpart F potentially apply on the LLC's earnings even before distribution, depending on what kind of income it earns and the entity classification you elect (default foreign corp vs. check-the-box disregarded entity). For services-only income earned by an active business, this is usually manageable, but the election decision is consequential and irrevocable for 5 years.
Form 1040 with FEIE (Form 2555) excluding ~$130K of earned income you receive from the LLC as salary, and Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) for any Georgian tax you've paid. This typically zeroes out your US income tax bill on the salary.
The self-employment tax trap — this is why LLC > IE for Americans.
There is no totalization agreement between Georgia and the United States. Practical consequence: if you are self-employed, even with the FEIE, in most situations the IRS expects you to pay the 15.3% self-employment/Medicare tax on your net income, even if your income is foreign-sourced and your business is based outside the US.
If you operate as an IE, you're self-employed in the IRS's eyes → 15.3% US SE tax on top of Georgian 1%. That turns "1% Georgian tax" into "~16% combined." The Georgian LLC structure routes around this because salary you receive from the LLC is wage income (not self-employment income), and there's no US payroll-tax obligation for a foreign employer paying a US citizen. This is the single biggest reason an American would pick LLC over IE despite IE's headline rate.
How the two sides reconcile.
There's no automatic income matching the way 1099s match domestic returns. But:
FATCA — Credo and every other Georgian bank report your account balances and certain transfers to the IRS automatically.
US-Georgia Tax Information Exchange Agreement — either government can request specific records.
Your own filings — your 1040, Form 5471, FBAR, and 8938 collectively tell the IRS what your LLC earned, what you took out, and where the money sits.
The system assumes you're honest and verifies through paper trails. Where people get hurt is missed Form 5471 filings (the $10K-per-year penalty stacks fast) or treating the Georgian LLC as a piggy bank for personal expenses (deemed distribution under both Georgian and US rules).
One practical note about university procurement.
University accounts payable offices vary wildly in their comfort with paying foreign entities, especially small foreign entities owned by US citizens. Some sail through it; some demand mountains of paperwork and want to issue a 1099-NEC anyway because that's what their system defaults to. If a US company mistakenly sent a 1099-NEC to a foreign contractor for work done entirely overseas, ask the payer to file a corrected 1099-NEC (zeroed) and, if any US-source services were paid to a nonresident, issue 1042-S instead. Worth confirming with the lab's grants office before signing the contract that they've paid foreign entities before and know the W-8BEN-E drill.
The legal framework is solid; the bureaucratic friction can be real. And given the stakes — Form 5471, GILTI, entity classification election, FEIE optimization — the US-side filings are absolutely a job for a US international tax CPA, not me. The Georgian side is straightforward by comparison.

What this actually means.
The system is not "school reports your work to the IRS." The system is "you report your work to the IRS, and the IRS independently has bank-level data via FATCA to cross-check what you reported." If your Form 5471 says the LLC took in $80K from one US client and Credo reports $200K of inbound payments on the corporate account, that's a flag. If the numbers reconcile, no one cares.
Where this has historically tripped up Americans abroad isn't the lab-side reporting (which truly doesn't happen) — it's the personal-side reporting (5471, FBAR, 8938) being skipped or done sloppily, and FATCA data later showing the IRS that something was undisclosed. The penalties for those filings being late or wrong are brutal — Form 5471 starts at $10,000 per form per year for non-filing.
One subtler thing worth understanding about the structure itself.
There's a US tax doctrine ("assignment of income") that says you can't assign your personal services income to a corporation if the corporation is just a paper vehicle to redirect what is functionally your personal earnings. The IRS rarely challenges this when:
The corporation has real substance (a real bank account, real bookkeeping, real contracts in its name, real expenses).
The contract is genuinely with the corporation, not just billed through it.
The corporation could in principle hire someone other than you to do the work.
For a single-person Georgian LLC that's the legitimate operating vehicle for your services business — substantively real, with you as employee/director — this isn't a meaningful risk. It becomes a risk if the structure looks like sleight-of-hand: e.g., the lab originally hired you personally and then you "redirected" the contract through a shell. The way to keep this clean is to have the lab's contract be with the LLC from day one, and to actually run business activity through the LLC (other clients, real bookkeeping, salary on payroll, etc.) rather than treating it as a payment-routing instrument.

Your name enters the chain primarily through the contract, the LLC's public-registry director listing, and your own annual US filings. The IRS sees you not because the school told them, but because you told them — and FATCA quietly verifies what you said.

This is actually a meaningful change, not just a nuisance shift. The US filing picture gets substantially lighter, and the self-employment tax problem largely disappears.
Why Form 5471 mostly drops out.
There's a specific provision — §958(b)(1) — that blocks attribution of stock owned by a non-resident alien spouse to a US citizen for CFC determination purposes. Concrete result: U.S. citizen Husband cannot be a United States shareholder because that would require him to own 10% or more of the foreign corporation's stock. Which he doesn't — IRC §958(b)(1), modifying IRC §318(a)(1)(A)(i), says U.S. citizen Husband owns bupkis. Nothing. Zero. This means that U.S. citizen Husband cannot be a United States shareholder.
So if Ana is the 100% owner and you own nothing, the company isn't a CFC as to you, and the Category 5 filing requirement (the heavy annual one with all the financials, GILTI, Subpart F) doesn't apply.
What might still apply, lightly:
Category 4 (control filer): Different attribution rules for §6038 do attribute Ana's ownership for "control" purposes, so technically you'd be a Category 4 filer. But there's an exception: When the US person has no direct or indirect ownership, and Category 4 applies solely because of attribution from a nonresident spouse, the exception under Treas. Reg. §1.6038-2(l) generally eliminates the annual filing requirement.
Category 3 (formation year): In the formation year, the US person is treated as crossing the 10% threshold through attribution. A one-time Category 3 filing may be required. So you might owe one Form 5471 the year Ana sets it up, and then nothing thereafter.
Category 2: Triggers only if you're an officer or director. If you're purely contractor/employee, doesn't apply.
Net: maybe one Form 5471 in formation year, then zero. Compare that to filing it every year forever as a CFC owner.
The self-employment tax problem disappears.
This is the big quiet win. Your income now is wages from a foreign employer, not self-employment income. Payroll taxes include social security and Medicare and are around 15.3%. Wages from a foreign employer aren't subject to US SE tax — the totalization-agreement gap doesn't bite you anymore. Salary goes on 1040 as foreign wage income, FEIE excludes the first ~$130K, FTC handles anything above. You're left with US tax that's likely zero.
What still applies regardless:
FBAR. If you have signature authority on the company's bank account (which you almost certainly will if you're operationally running things on Ana's behalf), you file FBAR for that account, even though you don't own it. Plus your personal Credo if it crosses $10K.
Form 8938 if the asset thresholds trip ($200K end-of-year / $300K any time, single, abroad).
FATCA from Credo keeps reporting your personal account automatically. Ana's company account, owned by a non-US person, doesn't get FATCA-reported on her — but signature authority by you may flag your name in the bank's records anyway.
The non-negotiable "must be real" piece.
For this to actually work as described, Ana has to genuinely own and control the company. She signs the formation documents, she's the director, she signs the W-8BEN-E to the lab, she signs the services agreement, the bank account is in her name as principal. You're an employee or contractor — your role is operational, not ownership. If the structure is documentary cover for you actually owning and controlling, the IRS has anti-abuse tools (sham entity doctrine, the new §951B from OBBBA, assignment-of-income) and they're not subtle. The way this stays clean is that the economic reality matches the paper.
Practical Georgian-side complications worth thinking through:
Two foreigners, two work authorizations. Under the March 2026 framework, Ana as director needs work authorization, and you as employee/contractor need separate work authorization. Both go through the Ministry of Labor.
TRP turnover doubles. The 50K GEL/12-month threshold is per foreign person in the company. With Ana (director) and you (employee) both foreign, the company needs 100K GEL turnover before either of you can use it for TRP.
Ana's own legal status. Per the structure, she'd register as the founder/director and likely apply for her own TRP through the company. She'd also need a Georgian tax ID and become a Georgian tax resident, which substantively means her global income becomes Georgian-taxable (Georgia is territorial-leaning so this is mostly academic, but worth knowing).
Lab procurement. The university's vendor onboarding will treat Ana's company as a foreign vendor with Ana as the responsible party. Some labs are fine with this; some have policies that get squirrelly when the responsible party doesn't match the person doing the work. Worth flagging early.
Bottom line for you: Annual Form 5471 disappears, SE tax disappears, FATCA still sees the personal account, and the salary you take from Ana's company gets cleanly excluded under FEIE.
