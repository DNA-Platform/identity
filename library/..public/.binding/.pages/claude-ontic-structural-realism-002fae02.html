Objects are reconceptualised, not (simply) abolished. French's own canonical gloss of the program is that physical objects, qua metaphysical entities, should be reconceptualised, or, more strongly, eliminated in favour of the relevant structures — and that "or" is the whole spectrum of the field, not loose phrasing. Even in his eliminativist mode, the move is representational, not a denial that there's anything there: objects are introduced for representational purposes and then ontologically reconceived, scaffolding you build with and then dismiss from the fundamental ontology. And he reconceives the relations in the same breath — not as relations holding between antecedently existing objects, but as features of the structure of the world, such as laws and symmetries, that yield the properties then associated with the objects. So your PA reading is their reading: structure (laws, symmetries) is the generator; the objects and their properties are what it yields. The numbers don't vanish; their generation is what's fundamental.
There are still relata — they say so explicitly, against the "relations without relata" slogan. The objection you'd expect — you can't have relations with nothing related — is one they consider answered: the objection has no force against OSR because there are still relata according to OSR; the relata are reconceived, not removed. The disagreement is only about how thin the relata are:
Moderate OSR (Esfeld, Lam): full mutual dependence — there cannot be relations without objects that stand in them, nor objects without relations in which they stand; objects exist but have no intrinsic essence, being neither bare substrata nor bundles of properties, which they frame as rejecting the property-oriented metaphysics dominant since Aristotle.
Thin objects (Ladyman, Saunders): objects exist derivatively and depend on structures, with identities given contextually by the structure rather than intrinsically.
Eliminativist (French & Ladyman 2003, French 2014): push it furthest — no fundamental objects, only relations, no identity conditions for objects, since there are no objects in the last resort — but even here "object" survives as a representational/derived notion, not a fundamental posit.
So your correction is exactly the field's self-understanding: OSR is not an ontology lacking nouns. It is one where the nouns are derived and not fundamental, where the structure is the real, propertied thing. The incoherence charge ("structure of what?") is one they think they've met by making the relata structural rather than denying them.
Individuality — this is where your 0 actually meets them, and the contrast is sharp. The program was born from individuality: French & Redhead (1988) argued quantum statistics leave it metaphysically underdetermined whether particles are individuals or non-individuals, and the structuralist response was to stop grounding identity in primitive thisness. Saunders (2003), borrowing Quine, built the positive account: two objects are absolutely discernible by a one-place predicate, relatively discernible by a two-place predicate holding in one order, and weakly discernible when an irreflexive two-place predicate relates them. Fermions in a symmetric singlet state share every qualitative property yet stand in the symmetric but irreflexive relation "has opposite direction of each component of spin to," on the basis of which they are weakly discernible; bosons can fail even that, which leads Saunders to treat them not as objects but as modes of the corresponding quantum field. The upshot they care about: no need to accept a numerical diversity of objects as primitive — diversity is constituted by the relational structure.

When Lam and Wüthrich state the radical thesis, the objects to be gotten rid of are the objects in the ontologies of fundamental theories in physics — the world consisting not of fundamental objects, which may or may not be individuals with well-defined intrinsic identity, but of physical structures. The set-theoretic objects are representational; the realist commitment is to the physical structure.
