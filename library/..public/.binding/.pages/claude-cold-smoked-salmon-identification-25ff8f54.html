This looks like cold-smoked salmon or trout fillet. The Georgian text reads ფილე პირველური (file pirveluri) as the subtitle, meaning something like "premier fillet" or "first-grade fillet." The large title text appears to be the fish name in Georgian — likely ორაგული (salmon) or ქორჭილა (trout), both of which are commonly sold this way in Georgian supermarkets.
The vacuum-sealed blue packaging and refrigerated display are typical of cold-smoked fish rather than lox. Lox is salt-cured but not smoked, while this style of Georgian supermarket fish product is almost always cold-smoked — similar to what you'd find labeled "smoked salmon" in the US, with that characteristic firm-but-silky texture and mild smoky flavor.
The orange sticker in the corner likely shows the price (looks like it may say 25-something GEL).

Is this ground espresso bean interpret the Georgian?
Claude
15:32 UTC, 29 March 2026
This is Carte Noire Selected Arabica — Cezve, Extra Fine Grind, 80g. The package is actually mostly in English/French, not Georgian.
It's not espresso grind — it's specifically ground for a cezve (also called a jezve or ibrik), which is the small long-handled copper/brass pot shown on the package, used to make Turkish/Georgian-style coffee. The grind is extra fine, actually finer than espresso, almost like powder. You brew it by simmering the grounds directly in water in the cezve, unfiltered.
If you're looking for espresso, this would over-extract and clog a machine. It's purpose-built for stovetop Turkish-style coffee.

But is it the same type of bean?
Claude
15:33 UTC, 29 March 2026
Yes — arabica is arabica. The bean species is the same as what's used in most quality espresso blends. The difference is purely in the grind size and intended brew method, not the bean itself.
Whether it would taste good as espresso if you reground it coarser is a separate question — roast profile and blend composition matter too, and Carte Noire tends toward a medium-dark roast, which is actually quite typical for espresso. So it's not an unreasonable crossover.

Would this work with a French press?
Claude
15:34 UTC, 29 March 2026
Not well. French press needs a coarse grind — the opposite end of the spectrum from cezve grind. The extra fine particles would pass right through the mesh filter, leaving your cup full of muddy sediment, and they'd over-extract quickly, making it bitter.
If a French press is what you have, the Jacobs Monarch on the shelf behind it would actually be a better fit — it's typically a medium grind more suitable for immersion brewing.
