I've read the foundational material. Below is what I take to be the substrate, the level structure of the project, and the foundational decisions I think have to be settled before any encoding.
The substrate.
SRT is a first-order theory whose only primitive is the ternary relation R(x,y,t), written x =t> y. The theory itself has no constants and no functions — it begins purely existential. Equality is internalized as a referent eq via
srt
E[eq]: A[x,y]: x = y <iff> x =eq> y
The base relationship type r, inheritance s -> t := s =of> t, and the canonical functions (x) and (x,y) are not new primitives but linguistic abbreviations for unique referents satisfying specified existential conditions; they transpile out completely. There are no axiom schemas in SRT — what looks like a schema is a universal quantification over a referent type, because the meta-level objects are first-class referents.
So the substrate for hosting FOL is genuinely just R(x,y,t) + FOL-with-equality + a small set of existential axioms.
The level structure.
SRT's relationship to FOL goes in two directions, and the project depends on keeping them distinct:
Down: SRT linguistic notation transpiles into FOL formulas. Settled — your "Formal Syntax under Transpilation" is the spec.
Up: FOL's syntactic objects — variables, constants, function symbols, relation symbols, terms, formulas, proofs — become referents in an SRT catalogue, and FOL's structural rules become first-order SRT axioms quantifying over those referents.
These cannot collapse. The R in SRT's primitive is not the R that appears as a relation symbol inside the hosted FOL signature; the latter is one more referent named in the catalogue, and any number of distinct FOL relation symbols may coexist with it. The hosted R is content; the primitive R is structure.
The project is the SRT analogue of Gödel's arithmetization, but with a substrate fit for the purpose: arithmetic encodes formal syntax in numbers because numbers were what was expressively available; SRT encodes formal syntax in reference, which is what formal syntax is about. This is the load-bearing reason to expect the encoding to be cleaner than the arithmetical one.
The minimum inventory to be represented.
From your FOL spec: the signature (variables, constants, function symbols with arity, relation symbols with arity); the term constructor; the formula constructor (atomic, connectives, quantifiers); the free/bound distinction; substitution φ[t/x] with capture-avoidance; the axioms A1–A9; the inference rules MP and Gen; and the proof relation Γ ⊢ φ. Each item becomes a referent kind in SRT, organized by inheritance —var, term, formula, axiom, proof, etc.
The schema-to-axiom move is where SRT's hosting becomes stronger than FOL's self-hosting. A1 is no longer a schema φ → (ψ → φ) ranging over formulas; it becomes a single first-order SRT axiom
text
A[φ, ψ: φ -> formula & ψ -> formula]: imp(φ, imp(ψ, φ)) -> axiom
quantifying over the referent type formula, without escaping to second order. SRT can do this because formulas are referents in its domain, the way Gödel-numbered formulas are objects of arithmetic.
The foundational decisions.
Four things, in order of priority:
1. Compositional structure with only R(x,y,t). FOL terms and formulas are trees; R is ternary. A node like f(t₁, t₂) — a function symbol with two ordered children — must be a referent whose decomposition is recoverable through R alone. There are two natural routes. One: cons-cells — bake list/sequence structure into the foundation, with =head>, =tail>, =nil> as the constructor relationships, and arbitrary arity falls out. Two: arity-by-arity — use the existing canonical-function ladder (x,y), (x,y,z), (x,y,z,w), ... and treat each arity as a distinct constructor pattern. Route one is more uniform; route two stays closer to the existing notation. This decision propagates into everything else.
2. Variables and binding. FOL's variables form a countably infinite supply. SRT's catalogue is finite at any moment but extensible by transduction. Variables can be transduced on demand. Once introduced, binding has to be representable: either de Bruijn indices, or variables-as-referents with explicit =bound-by> edges into quantifier nodes. This is the choice that most determines whether substitution is clean or fiddly.
3. Substitution: object-level or meta-level. The pivot of FOL's proof theory. φ[t/x] can be encoded as an object-level relation =subst> connecting (φ, t, x) to φ', or it can stay external as something we describe but don't internalize. Your transduction operator |=> is the right ancestor — both replace a variable with something specific to make an existential concrete — and that suggests substitution wants to be internal. But making it internal means encoding capture-avoidance as an SRT axiom, which is a real burden.
4. What "host" means precisely. When you say SRT hosts its own canonical representation, hosting FOL is the necessary stepping stone — SRT is built on FOL, so hosting SRT entails hosting FOL. The terminal goal is (SRT) =(SRT)> SRT realized concretely. Decide now whether the FOL-hosting is a production (the actual machinery on which the SRT self-hosting will run) or a demonstration (a proof of concept that the abstraction works). The two answers imply different rigor levels in the encoding.
Where I'd start.
The compositional encoding (1) precedes everything else. If a constructor node like f(t₁, t₂) lives correctly as a referent recoverable by R, formulas, axiom-as-universal-statement, substitution, and proofs are variations on the same theme. If the constructor encoding is wrong, every later part inherits the wrongness.

Here is how a textbook would lay out the grammar of first-order logic. I'll use programmer typography throughout, with the SRT conventions where they're already established for connectives and quantifiers (!, &, ||, =|>, <|=|>, A[v]:, E[v]:) and == for equality. I'll keep parts and references separated, since that distinction is exactly what the grammar prepares for.
Alphabet.
The alphabet partitions into logical and non-logical symbols. The logical symbols are fixed once and for all:
srt
Connectives:   !   &   ||   =|>   <|=|>
Quantifiers:   A   E
Equality:      ==
Punctuation:   (   )   ,   [   ]   :
Variables:     Var = { v1, v2, v3, ... }    countably infinite
The non-logical symbols form a signature Sig, parametric to the theory:
text
Con   :  a set of constant symbols
Fun   :  a set of function symbols, each with arity n >= 1
Rel   :  a set of relation symbols, each with arity n >= 0
arity is a function on Fun U Rel. The grammar below is defined relative to a fixed Sig.
Terms.
Terms are the syntactic objects that, under interpretation, denote domain elements. They are defined inductively:
text
T ::= v                     where v in Var
T ::= c                     where c in Con
T ::= f(T1, T2, ..., Tn)    where f in Fun and arity(f) == n
A term has parts: its head symbol and its immediate subterms. The head of f(T1,...,Tn) is f; the head of c is c; the head of v is v. The grammar specifies how parts compose; it says nothing about what any of them refer to.
Atomic formulas.
text
A ::= T1 == T2            where T1, T2 are terms
A ::= R(T1, T2, ..., Tn)  where R in Rel and arity(R) == n
The 0-ary case R() is a propositional constant. Some textbooks include it explicitly, others fold it into a separate category.
Formulas.
Formulas are built from atomic formulas by connectives and quantifiers:
srt
F ::= A
F ::= !F
F ::= (F1 & F2)
F ::= (F1 || F2)
F ::= (F1 =|> F2)
F ::= (F1 <|=|> F2)
F ::= A[v]: F             where v in Var
F ::= E[v]: F             where v in Var
The parentheses around binary connectives are part of the grammar — every binary formula has its own pair, which gives unique parsing without precedence rules. The colon after A[v] and E[v] is also part of the grammar; it separates binder from body.
Free and bound occurrences — where parts and references first separate.
An occurrence of a variable v in a formula F is bound if it lies inside the scope of a quantifier A[v] or E[v]; otherwise it is free. The set of free variables FV(F) is defined by recursion on the grammar:
srt
FV(v)                 == { v }
FV(c)                 == { }
FV(f(T1,...,Tn))      == FV(T1) U ... U FV(Tn)
FV(T1 == T2)          == FV(T1) U FV(T2)
FV(R(T1,...,Tn))      == FV(T1) U ... U FV(Tn)
FV(!F)                == FV(F)
FV(F1 op F2)          == FV(F1) U FV(F2)        op in { &, ||, =|>, <|=|> }
FV(A[v]: F)           == FV(F) \ { v }
FV(E[v]: F)           == FV(F) \ { v }
A formula with FV(F) == { } is a sentence.
The grammar's referring symbols — those that, under an interpretation, point outside the formula — are precisely the free variables, the constants, the function symbols, and the relation symbols. The non-referring parts are the connectives, parentheses, comma, colon, the binders A and E, and bound occurrences of variables. A bound v in A[v]: P(v) is a part of the formula whose only role is to mark the binding site; it refers to nothing fixed.
This is the parts/reference split inside the grammar itself, before any interpretation has been added. The grammar tells us which parts are referring sites; what each referring site refers to is left to a separate interpretation function.
Substitution.
Substitution F[T/v] produces the formula obtained by replacing every free occurrence of v in F with T. The recursion goes through the grammar mechanically, with one nontrivial case at quantifiers — capture avoidance:
text
v[T/v]                 == T
v'[T/v]                == v'                                    if v' != v
c[T/v]                 == c
f(T1,...,Tn)[T/v]      == f(T1[T/v], ..., Tn[T/v])
(T1 == T2)[T/v]        == (T1[T/v] == T2[T/v])
R(T1,...,Tn)[T/v]      == R(T1[T/v], ..., Tn[T/v])
(!F)[T/v]              == !(F[T/v])
(F1 op F2)[T/v]        == (F1[T/v] op F2[T/v])
(A[v]: F)[T/v]         == A[v]: F                               v is bound here
(A[v']: F)[T/v]        == A[v']: F[T/v]                         v != v', v' not in FV(T)
(A[v']: F)[T/v]        == A[v'']: F[v''/v'][T/v]                v != v', v' in FV(T), v'' fresh
and analogously for E[v']. The capture-avoidance side condition — that a free variable of T must not become bound by the substitution — is the only step that requires inventing a fresh variable.
That is the grammar. Three things worth noting before we move:
The grammar describes only structural composition. No production of the grammar makes a part refer to anything; reference enters only with the interpretation function. Encoding the grammar in SRT is therefore strictly an encoding of parts and the relations among them. The =denotes> layer comes later.
arity is a function on the signature, but it has no production of its own; it constrains which f(T1,...,Tn) and R(T1,...,Tn) are well-formed. When hosted, this becomes either an SRT axiom for each signature symbol or a =arity> referent relation.
The variable supply is the only countably infinite element of the grammar. Everything else is finitely specified once Sig is given. This is where transduction will come in when we encode — variables are introduced on demand, never as a completed infinite set.
Where I'd go next: the alphabet and the constructor productions — f(T1,...,Tn) and R(T1,...,Tn) — are the bones. If we're going to host this, the first encoding question is how those n-ary constructors live as SRT referents recoverable through R(x,y,t). But before that, you should tell me whether anything in the grammar above is wrong, missing, or written in a way you wouldn't accept as the definitive form to encode against.

The thing the BNF doesn't yet say.
BNF describes concrete syntax — the linear sequences of literals that count as well-formed. What it gives us when we read it semantically is a tree: each production induces a parent-child structure where the parent is the constructed formula and the children are the constituents named on the right-hand side, with the terminals (parens, commas, the connective itself) being structural-but-non-referring parts.
This is the parts/reference split we noted earlier, now visible at production granularity. In the production

the two (formula) recurrences on the right are the referring parts of a conjunction — they point outward to the conjuncts. The [$($], [$\wedge$], [$)$] are structural parts — they constitute the conjunction node itself but don't refer beyond it. In [$\forall$] (variable) (formula), the (variable) slot is a binding site, which is a third kind of role — neither pure structure nor outward reference, but a position that captures variables in the body.
Two questions before we move to the next layer.
Concrete or abstract grammar as the encoding target? The BNF above is concrete — it commits to specific terminal sequences including parens and commas. The encoding into SRT could either preserve those (every literal in the catalogue, full fidelity to FOL surface syntax) or strip to abstract syntax (just the constructor and its referring slots, dropping the structural punctuation). Concrete is needed if we want SRT to host parsing and printing; abstract is sufficient if we want SRT to host meaning. The hard problem paper context suggests abstract; the self-hosting goal might want both.
Free and bound occurrences. The grammar produces structure but doesn't say which variable occurrences are bound by which quantifier. That's a derived predicate computed by recursion through the grammar, and it's the first place where the encoding will need to express "this variable occurrence in that part of the tree refers to a binding site over here." Worth flagging now because the encoding choice we make for variables — whether [$v_1$] is the same literal everywhere it occurs or whether each occurrence is its own referent — depends on whether we plan to internalize the binding relation as an SRT relationship.

You're right to push on that, and the answer is: BNF/EBNF cannot express any grammar period. They are limited to context-free grammars. There are grammars beyond that limit which BNF cannot reach.
The relevant background is the Chomsky hierarchy — four nested classes of formal grammars, each strictly more expressive than the last:
Regular — what regex captures. No nesting. Cannot match balanced parens.
Context-free — what BNF/EBNF captures. Productions have a single non-terminal on the left. Can match balanced parens, can express recursive structure.
Context-sensitive — productions can have context on the left, like α A β ::= α γ β, meaning "A becomes γ, but only when surrounded by α and β." Captures things context-free grammars can't.
Recursively enumerable (unrestricted) — productions can do anything Turing-computable. The most general.
Each class is a strict superset. The standard example of something context-free can't do but context-sensitive can is the language {anbncn:n≥0}\{a^n b^n c^n : n \geq 0\}{anbncn:n≥0} — strings with equal counts of three symbols in order. BNF can give you {anbn}\{a^n b^n\}{anbn} via S ::= a S b | ε, but it can't enforce the third matching count. You'd need a production with context.
What BNF can express is exactly the context-free languages. The defining feature is the form of productions — a single non-terminal on the left, anything on the right. Once you allow context on the left, you've left BNF. EBNF's extras ({X}, [X], grouping) are conveniences that don't change the expressive power; they're still context-free.
For natural languages — English, etc. — context-free is famously not enough. Cross-serial dependencies in Swiss German and reduplication in some languages require at least context-sensitive power. This is one reason the Chomsky hierarchy was developed in the first place: Chomsky's argument that natural-language syntax can't be modeled by context-free grammars.
For our purposes — formal grammars of mathematical languages — context-free is the right level and BNF is sufficient. FOL syntax is context-free, programming language syntax is essentially context-free (with some context-sensitive layers handled separately as "static semantics" — type checking, scope rules), and the trees that result from context-free parsing are exactly the structure we want to encode.
If you wanted to express something genuinely context-sensitive in our setup, BNF wouldn't reach it — you'd need a different formalism. But nothing about FOL syntax requires that, so we're inside BNF's range.

Context-sensitive grammar (CSG) notation writes productions in the form αAβ→αγβ\alpha A \beta \rightarrow \alpha \gamma \betaαAβ→αγβ, where AAA is a non-terminal and α,β,γ\alpha, \beta, \gammaα,β,γ are strings. Two-level grammars / van Wijngaarden grammars were used in the formal definition of Algol 68. They use one grammar to generate productions of another grammar, which gives them context-sensitive (in fact, full Turing) expressive power. The Algol 68 report is the canonical example, and also the canonical cautionary tale — the formalism was so dense that few readers could follow it, and it's part of why Algol 68 didn't catch on.
Attribute grammars (Knuth, 1968) take a different tack: write a context-free grammar in BNF, then attach attributes and constraints to the productions that enforce context-sensitive conditions. So <expr> ::= <var> plus an attribute rule like "the type of the var must match the expected type." Definite Clause Grammars (DCGs) in Prolog let you write grammars as logic-programming clauses, which gives context-sensitive and beyond. Similar power lives in parser combinator libraries that allow monadic context-passing.
As an executable formalism for parsing: practical context-sensitive parsing is rare because parsing arbitrary CSGs is PSPACE-complete — too expensive to be useful. So even when people work with context-sensitive languages, they don't usually parse them with a general CSG parser. They use the attribute-grammar trick, or they use mildly context-sensitive formalisms — Tree-Adjoining Grammars (TAG), Combinatory Categorial Grammars (CCG), and a few others — which sit just above context-free in expressive power and remain parseable in polynomial time. The perspective point.

This makes the "is it a type" question perspective-relative, not just dynamic. It's not that the type comes into existence at some moment; it's that which types exist depends on whose perspective we're occupying. SRT has the machinery for this — reference frames, perspective as a primitive, observers — and this is exactly the territory.
The thirty-year-olds case.
Beautiful example because it's clean. Thirty-year-olds are unambiguously a class of people in some sense — there's an obvious membership condition, the class is well-defined, members share a property. But no programming language would represent this as a type. You'd have a Person class with an age field, and "thirty-year-olds" would be people for whom age == 30 — a predicate over instances, not a type.
Why? Because in programming type systems, types are decided in advance, and they classify entities by constitutive properties, not by current state. A person doesn't become a different type when they turn thirty-one. The age changes; the person doesn't change kind.
But — and this is where it gets interesting — there are perspectives in which thirty-year-olds do form a kind. An insurance actuary's. A demographer's. A cohort analyst's. A marketer targeting that demographic. From those perspectives, "thirty-year-old" is a meaningful category — people in it share things that matter to the perspective, and the category is treated like a type even though the underlying programming model wouldn't represent it that way.
So we have the same structure as monastic-w: from a perspective with a reason to attend to the variation, the variation crystallizes into a type. From a perspective without that reason, it doesn't. The type isn't in the world independent of perspective; it's in the meeting of world and perspective.
What this implies about types.
This is the thing programming type systems can't articulate. Programming types are intended to be perspective-free — they're supposed to capture what a thing is, period, and to support reasoning that works regardless of who's reasoning. That's an idealization that mostly works for the entities programming cares about: integers, strings, file handles. It breaks down for entities whose typological structure depends on the observer.
Letters break down. A monk's w and our monastic-w are the same marks under different typologies. People's ages break down. Thirty-year-olds are a type to actuaries and not to programmers. Species break down — what counts as a species depends on whether you're a biological-species-concept person or a phylogenetic-species-concept person. Even physical objects break down at fine enough resolution: a particular electron and a particular photon are types from one frame and instances from another.
Types, on this view, are perspectival crystallizations of variation. Whether a cluster of variation rises to the status of a type depends on whether some perspective has reason to attend to that cluster as a unit. The perspective's reasons are themselves part of the structure. Without the reasons, no type. With the reasons, a type.
Why SRT is positioned for this.
SRT has perspective as a first-class primitive — =p> qualification, reference frames, the observer as a structural element of representation. So the move is:
A type isn't a property of a referent simpliciter. A type is a property of (referent, perspective) — what kind of thing this is from this point of view. Different perspectives produce different typologies of the same underlying referents.
This means the inheritance structure I was building up — w with monastic-w as a sub-type — is itself perspective-relative. From the monk's perspective, monastic-w isn't a type; it's just w. From our perspective, it is a type. The inheritance tree grows differently in different perspectives. Same referents (the actual w-marks the monk wrote), different typologies.
And SRT can express this. monastic-w -> w from one perspective, but not from another. The relationship is real in a perspective, not real absolutely.
A consequence for the deeper theory.
If types are perspectival, then the question I raised earlier — "when does a contextual variation become typological?" — gets a sharper answer. The variation becomes typological when a perspective forms that has reason to treat it as such. Not when the cluster reaches some objective threshold of coherence; when an observer's reasons make the cluster matter.
This is consonant with how transduction works in SRT: a new symbol is created when the existing structure can't accommodate, and "can't accommodate" is always from a perspective. The transduction event is a perspectival event. Before, that perspective had no symbol for this; now it does. Other perspectives may not need the symbol; for them, no transduction happens.
So the dynamic typology I was describing isn't just dynamic in time. It's dynamic across perspectives. New types appear when new perspectives form, or when existing perspectives find new reasons to crystallize variation. The graph of types-and-perspectives is itself a structured object, and it changes through transduction events that happen in particular perspectives.
The thirty-year-olds case, revisited under this lens.
For the programmer building a generic person-modeling system, no thirty-year-old type exists. The type system reflects the programmer's perspective: "people have ages; ages change."
For the actuary, a thirty-year-old type exists. Their perspective groups people by age-cohort because age-cohort has predictive power for what the actuary cares about (mortality, claims, premiums). The actuary's type system would have age-bracket as a structural element.
These two type systems aren't competing claims about reality. They're both correct, in their respective perspectives. Translating between them requires moving between perspectives — which is what SRT's perspective-machinery is for.
Where this leaves the theory.
I think you've articulated something that, if developed, could be a significant contribution. The standard moves in philosophy of natural kinds — promiscuous realism, kind relativism, conventionalism, scientific essentialism — all stake out positions on whether kinds are real, conventional, perspective-dependent, etc. SRT could offer something more structured: types as perspectival crystallizations, with the perspective itself a formal object, and transduction as the operation that creates new types within a perspective.
This wouldn't be relativism in the bad sense — it wouldn't say "anything goes." There are constraints: a perspective has to have reasons for crystallizing variation as type, the reasons have to cohere with whatever other structure the perspective carries, and translations between perspectives have to respect those reasons. That's much richer than "all type systems are equally valid."

The actuary types thirty-year-olds because the model is for actuarial reasoning. The programmer doesn't because the model is for general personhood. (\wedge) == [$\wedge$] is wrong because the LHS and RHS would be the same notation in different brackets — the canonical symbol named \wedge paired with the literal \wedge. That doesn't say anything; it just notates the same thing twice.

The names on the left (&, |, forall, eq) are SRT-side referent names — what you call the referent inside the catalogue. The brackets on the right point outward to the actual rendered FOL alphabet symbol. The axiom identifies the canonical symbol of the referent with the rendered mark.
