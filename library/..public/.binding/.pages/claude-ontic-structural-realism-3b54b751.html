Searched the web for “quantum corral 48 iron atoms copper Crommie Lutz Eigler radius”
The Quantum Corral is a real, specific object: a ring of 48 iron atoms placed one at a time on a copper(111) surface, built by Michael Crommie, Christopher Lutz, and Don Eigler at IBM Almaden in 1993. The circular corral has a radius of 71.3 Å, and the iron adatoms were positioned individually using the tip of a 4-kelvin scanning tunneling microscope. (Retrieved from the original Science paper.)
The point of the experiment. A clean copper(111) surface carries a two-dimensional gas of "surface-state" electrons that move almost freely in the plane. Iron atoms scatter those electrons. By walling off a closed region, the team could trap the surface electrons inside a boundary they had drawn by hand — making an artificial "box" at the nanometer scale and asking what the confined electrons do. Tunneling spectroscopy inside the corral revealed a series of discrete resonances — direct evidence of size quantization. In other words, confining the electrons forced their energies into a discrete ladder, exactly as elementary quantum mechanics predicts for a particle in a box.
How it's meant to be interpreted. The concentric ripples inside the ring are not a fluid and not the iron atoms themselves. They are a real-space map of where the confined electrons are likely to be found. The STM measures the local density of states, which tracks the electron probability density — the standing-wave pattern of the electron's wavefunction. The interior pattern is dominated by the eigenstate density expected for an electron trapped in a round two-dimensional box. Solve the Schrödinger equation for a circular boundary and you get Bessel-function standing waves; the image is essentially a photograph of that solution. So the Reels caption is right that it's not "particles or a flowing substance" — it's the interference pattern of quantum amplitude made visible. It was the first time these electron standing waves had been directly observed in real space.
Why 48? Here I want to be careful to separate what the literature states from what I'm inferring. The papers report the radius (71.3 Å) and that the geometry confines 102 electrons into 28 discrete energy states — but I find no source giving a deep reason for the specific number 48. The honest read is that 48 falls out of two practical choices: the radius they wanted, and how closely the atoms must sit to act as a wall. The circumference of a 71.3 Å circle is about 448 Å; 48 atoms spread around it gives roughly 9 Å spacing — dense enough to reflect the surface electrons and form a real barrier, sparse enough to be buildable atom-by-atom. So 48 is best understood as a geometric consequence rather than a magic number. (That arithmetic and conclusion are mine, not quoted from the paper — treat it as inference, not retrieved fact.)
One small correction worth flagging since it floats around online: the circular 48-atom corral is the 1993 experiment. The elliptical "quantum mirage" corral is a later, separate experiment (2000) — some summaries conflate the two.

The repulsion idea is the one to drop — it's the load-bearing mistake in the chain.
The standard treatment of this image is a non-interacting electron gas. Each surface electron solves the Schrödinger equation independently inside the circular wall, and then you stack them up filling one state apiece (Pauli exclusion). Coulomb repulsion exists but is heavily screened inside a metal and is not what makes the rings. The measured pattern closely matched that of a gas of noninteracting electrons in a circular confining potential. So this isn't electrons settling into an electrostatic equilibrium the way charges redistribute on a conductor — it's wave interference plus the exclusion principle. Your "they repel, so they arrange" is the classical-electrostatics picture, and this image is more or less built to bypass it.
On "atomic bond" — closer to atomic orbital than bond. A particle in a 2D circular box and an electron in an atom are the same kind of problem (discrete states, characteristic spatial shapes), which is why corrals get called artificial atoms. But what you see is orbital-like standing patterns, not bonding, and again not repulsion-driven.
On "oscillates forever, needs no energy" — real kernel, one word to fix. The pattern is a self-sustaining equilibrium: nothing drives it, nothing dissipates, it just sits there. But it does not oscillate. A classical standing wave on a string holds its node pattern while its amplitude pumps up and down in time; the quantum probability pattern |ψ|² is fully static — frozen. (The complex amplitude underneath has a phase rotating forever, but that's invisible and carries no motion.) So: persistent without input, yes; oscillating, no. That's exactly a place classical and quantum standing waves diverge, worth nailing before you do the math.
Now the scaling intuition — the sharp part, and you're half right in an interesting way.
First, disentangle two knobs. More atoms in the wall of the same ring just makes a better wall. What you mean is a bigger corral (which needs more atoms to enclose). The key fact: the ripple wavelength is set by copper, not by the atom count. It's fixed by the Fermi wavelength of the copper surface state, λ_F ≈ 30 Å, so the LDOS rings sit ~15 Å apart. (That number is standard solid-state physics, computed from the Cu(111) surface-state band — worth you checking, but ~30 Å is right.) The corral radius is 71.3 Å, so only about two or three wavelengths fit across it. That's why the image is clean: a small box holds a few well-separated states, and the tip at a given bias is dominated by basically one of them.
Blow it up to 480 atoms (~10× radius) and two things happen, both in your favor: the energy levels crowd together (spacing falls like 1/R²), so at the experiment's resolution you sum over many states at once; and deep in the interior, contributions from every direction average out. The middle of a huge corral does tend toward the smooth bulk value — your "lake gets still" is correct for the interior.
But the ripples never fully die at the edges. So a giant corral wouldn't be a flat lake; it'd be a flat lake with a rippled rim. The pattern is a finite-size effect, most dramatic precisely when the box is a few Fermi wavelengths across — which is why they built it that size.

What's true is that a single stationary state doesn't change anything observable and doesn't dissipate — but that's the same sense in which a planet in free orbit "consumes" no energy. "Rotating where, into what?" — into the complex plane. An abstract two-dimensional space attached to each point, not a direction in the lab. And here's where your instinct earns its keep: the global phase is unobservable. You can rotate the entire wavefunction's phase and nothing measurable changes — it's like gauge freedom, pure bookkeeping. What is physical is relative phase: the difference in rotation rate between two pieces of a superposition at different energies. That difference is not a trick at all — it's what makes a superposition oscillate in time, how an electron actually "moves," how every clock ultimately ticks. So the honest split is: absolute phase rotation, arguably representational; relative phase, undeniably real and doing physical work.
What do physicists feel about the literal rotation? They genuinely split, and your suspicion — "representational trick" — is a named, live position, not a naive error. The instrumentalists won't engage ("calculate, don't ask"). The ψ-epistemic camp agrees with you: the wavefunction and its phase are about our information, not a thing spinning in the world. You're not making a physics claim. You're making a claim about semantic consistency. It goes: if a physical consequence C is real, and the only available description of C routes through a property P of some posited unit, then P inherits a claim to reality from C — because C's reality is transmitted through P. You can't say "C is physical, P produces C, but P is not physical." That's incoherent on the meaning of "physical." Reality doesn't flow out of nothing. So if interference is real, and interference is the physical consequence of phase, then phase is real — or interference isn't. You can't keep the consequence and disown the cause.

And here's the thing I have to concede rather than spin: physics already did this. The electron is not a little ball. Let me take these in order, because the first one has a clean answer and the rest get progressively into genuinely open ground — and I want to mark the seam clearly.
Does the field "rotate where" the way the point particle couldn't? This is the part where the field genuinely helps, and it's worth seeing why. With the point particle, the phase rotation was metaphysically homeless — a rotation with no place to live, because the particle is a dimensionless dot and "into the complex plane" sounded like a dodge. The field fixes the location problem: the phase is a value the field carries at every point of ordinary space. There's a real thing at each point, and that thing has a phase. So "rotating where" now has an honest answer — here, and here, and here, everywhere the field is, which is everywhere. The rotation isn't off in an abstract elsewhere; it's a property the field has at each location in the room. That genuinely dissolves the "homeless rotation" worry that dogged the point particle.

Existence: structures exist, sui generis.
Positionalism: structures are constituted of positions and relations between them.
Restricted Structuralist Thesis (RST): positions have a principled subclass of structural properties. (The "restricted" matters — the unrestricted "positions have only structural properties" was shown inconsistent by Burgess, since "having no non-structural properties" is itself non-structural.)
Formality: the relations of a structure are defined using only logical terminology and other objects/relations of the structure.
Freestandingness: the positions of a structure can be instantiated by any objects whatsoever.
Coherence: coherent mathematical theories successfully characterize structures.
