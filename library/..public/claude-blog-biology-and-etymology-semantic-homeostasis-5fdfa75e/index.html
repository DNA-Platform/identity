Schleicher's family tree (1863) got its rebuttal nine years later. Johannes Schmidt's Wellentheorie (1872) argued innovations spread as overlapping waves across a dialect continuum, not as clean bifurcations. Same objection you're making, from inside, immediately.
Sprachbund (Trubetzkoy, 1928) is your horizontal transfer with a name. The Balkan case is the showpiece: Greek, Albanian, Romanian, Bulgarian, unrelated or distantly related, converged on postposed definite articles, loss of the infinitive, and a future built from "want."
Contact linguistics since Thomason & Kaufman (1988) is a mature subfield with a borrowing hierarchy predicting what transfers under what social conditions.
And the tools have literally been imported from biology. Gray & Atkinson, Bryant's NeighborNet, phylogenetic networks rather than trees, chosen specifically to model reticulation. The disciplines do talk. They've been talking for twenty years.
Also worth noting: analogy was never suppressed. The Neogrammarian slogan was "sound laws admit no exception," but the whole framework only works because analogy is the sanctioned second force that overrides them. Folk etymology isn't a scandal, it's a mechanism with a chapter in every textbook. Island (isle + land, the s inserted by scribes who believed in insula), hangnail (OE angnægl, "painful nail," reanalyzed), crayfish (OF crevice, no fish involved). Those are permanent rewrites caused by a false connection, which is exactly your memetic selection.
Here's the piece I'd actually build the post around, because it's the strongest empirical version of your hippopotamus argument and the transcript never gave it to you:
Jules Gilliéron, working the Atlas linguistique de la France, found that in parts of southwest France, Latin gallus "rooster" and cattus "cat" both came out as gat by regular sound change. The rooster word was then replaced by other forms that shifted or extended their meaning: faisan (historically "pheasant"), vicaire "curate," and others, and Gilliéron's explanation was that these replacements avoided the misunderstandings the clash would have caused.
That's your point, empirically, in a dialect atlas: two lineages converge on one form, the semantic fields collide in a shared context (the farmyard), and the language kills one off. It's called homonymic clash. Deer/dear survives because the fields don't collide. Gat/gat doesn't. You predicted the selection pressure from the biology; Gilliéron measured it in 1918.

The Aeolism business is real (Varro, Quintilian, Philoxenus all entertained Latin as a Greek dialect), but it doesn't support the specific claim made. Aeolists were trying to derive Latin from Greek wholesale, not selecting catena for its resemblance to kata-.
