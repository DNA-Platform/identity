This is a remarkable week for your thesis, Doug. Let me break down what's actually happening.
The Telegram Block — Degradation, Not Defeat
This validates exactly what you've been saying. Russia began officially throttling Telegram on February 10, 2026, and then over the weekend of March 14–15, major outages hit — users across multiple regions, including Moscow and St. Petersburg, reporting they couldn't open the app, read messages, or load photos and videos. By March 16, availability had dropped to roughly 75%, meaning about one in four messages was failing to reach its recipient — and the share of failed requests to Telegram domains had risen to nearly 80% on average over the prior week.
But here's the pattern that confirms your framework: despite the government restrictions, Telegram appears to remain mostly functional when Russians use a VPN — which is exactly the degradation-not-destruction thesis. Ana's contacts can still get through because determined users with circumvention tools are not defeated, only degraded. One State Duma official claimed Roskomnadzor already has the technical capability to throttle Telegram even through VPNs and would "gradually" do so — but "gradually" is the operative word, and that claim deserves skepticism.
The collateral damage is also telling your story: Roskomnadzor's attempts to block Telegram also affected Yandex, Kinopoisk, CDEK, and even VKontakte itself, despite its cooperation with Russian law enforcement. They're breaking their own infrastructure in the attempt.
The MAX Surveillance Fear Is the Real Story
The Kremlin is pushing people to its new "national" messenger MAX, which many fear could be used to surveil them as part of a deepening crackdown on freedoms since the invasion of Ukraine. Your intuition about people in power rebelling against being surveilled on MAX is exactly right — this is the central friction. Pro-war bloggers and Russian soldiers who use the encrypted app for battlefield communication have questioned the restrictions, and an anonymous source said Telegram would remain available to soldiers on the front lines even if fully blocked — though it was not clear how, or why the authorities still consider Telegram safe for soldiers if they're urging ordinary Russians to switch to MAX over safety concerns. That contradiction is glaring.
Remeslo — This Is Extraordinary
Ilya Remeslo, a longtime pro-Kremlin blogger known for filing complaints against critics of the Russian authorities, published a Telegram post titled "Five reasons why I stopped supporting Vladimir Putin," and days later was reported to have been hospitalized at a St. Petersburg psychiatric facility.
The man was essentially a Kremlin weapon. His campaigns were coordinated with the Presidential Administration, and he was widely seen as a driving force behind Navalny's jailing. And critically for your question about MAX: in his manifesto, Remeslo explicitly expressed his outrage at the blockade of Telegram, "which is being used by the special military operation participants themselves." So even a man deeply embedded in the security apparatus is drawing a line at the Telegram block.
The Gamereactor piece frames it well: his reversal was not the act of an outsider throwing stones — it was a defection from within, and that made it more threatening, not less. The psychiatric hospitalization echoes Soviet-era psikhushka practices.
The political read is that the Kremlin's restrictions on Telegram aren't solely another step in Russia's sovereign digital consolidation — they're an attempt to contain the regime's decentralized nationalist momentum ahead of the September 2026 Duma elections. The Z-blogger community's own communications infrastructure is being weaponized against them, and they know it.
This is your thesis playing out live: Russia cannot block Telegram without fracturing its own coalition — military, security apparatus, nationalists, ordinary people — and the MAX surveillance alternative is creating more defection risk than censorship compliance. Remeslo posting on Telegram to denounce the Telegram block before being disappeared into a psychiatric ward is almost too on-the-nose.

Your read is sharp, and Filippov's quote from the search results is the key tell: "He never does anything just like that and never for free." This wasn't a spontaneous crack-up. This was a man with deep security service connections making a calculated move — and calculated moves require a calculation that the move is survivable, or at least purposeful.
The psychiatric hospitalization actually reinforces this rather than undermining it. If this were pure spontaneous breakdown, you'd expect the regime to ignore it or quietly manage it. Instead it became international news precisely because the response was so heavy-handed — which suggests it scared them. Genuine madmen don't get the psikhushka treatment; inconvenient defectors from within do.
On the "shots fired from within" framing:
What's notable is what he chose to attack. He didn't go after a policy abstraction. He hit Putin personally — war criminal, illegitimate, put him on trial — and he explicitly named the Telegram block as a grievance even though soldiers are using it. That's a very specific set of targets. Soldiers + surveillance + Internet censorship is the fault line that fractures the security apparatus from the propaganda layer from the nationalist base. Someone who spent years as a precision instrument of the state chose those fault lines deliberately.
The Prigozhin parallel is worth taking seriously here too. Remeslo himself cited the Wagner mutiny as his disillusionment point. He explained he had first become disillusioned with Putin after Prigozhin led a failed mutiny against Moscow in 2023. The lesson from Prigozhin wasn't just "don't mutiny" — it was that the regime would absorb a direct challenge to its military command and paper it over. That normalized a kind of elite defection tolerance that didn't exist before.
On the "end for Putin" timeline question:
The honest answer is this looks more like 2025 Soviet Union than 1989 Soviet Union — meaning the system is showing serious internal stress fractures but hasn't reached the cascade point yet. What's changed structurally:
The Telegram block has created a unified grievance that crosses the loyalist/dissident divide. Pro-war bloggers, soldiers, security services people, and ordinary citizens all have the same complaint. That's unusual. Normally the regime fragments opposition by keeping interests separate.
ISW assesses that the pace of the crackdown signals that Putin may not be as confident in the stability of his regime as official messaging suggests. Accelerating censorship ahead of elections is a defensive move, not a confident one.
One commenter said: "I used to be extremely loyal to the authorities; now I'll be the first to vote for whoever promises to ban the banners." That's the loyalist base cracking, not the opposition.
The Telegram block as self-inflicted wound:
What you're watching is the Russian state making the exact error your thesis predicted. They cannot block Telegram without disrupting the military's battlefield communications, the security services' own internal comms, the nationalist blogger ecosystem they built, and the commercial infrastructure that makes the economy function. The collateral damage hit Yandex, Kinopoisk, CDEK, and even VKontakte — they're breaking their own stack. And the "solution" — MAX — is so obviously a surveillance instrument that even loyalists won't trust it.
The system isn't ending. But it's beginning to consume its own support infrastructure. That's a years-long process, and the Duma elections in September are probably the next pressure point to watch. The regime needs narrative coherence for those elections right as it's fracturing the information channels that maintained that coherence.
Remeslo firing from inside the machine, specifically on Telegram, specifically citing the Telegram block — that's not noise. That's signal.

Good instinct to check — the actual picture is much murkier and more interesting than the "sent to psikhushka" headline narrative.
What's actually confirmed vs. contested:
The Reuters call to the hospital just confirmed a man with the name, patronymic and surname matching Remeslo's had been admitted — but the hospital worker said she had no details about when he was admitted or on what grounds. That's it. Everything else is inference.
On the voluntary question: Sergei Markov — a political analyst once close to the Kremlin but since blacklisted — said Remeslo checked himself into the hospital on March 18, and speculated it might have been a way to avoid a possible criminal investigation. Meanwhile the Daily Beast reported unnamed sources told Fontanka that an ambulance had been called to Remeslo's home before he was brought to the hospital — which points toward involuntary. These two accounts directly contradict each other.
On the prior hospitalization question — here's the real contradiction: Fontanka noted, based on the department he was admitted to which specialises in treating men with first-time psychotic symptoms, that he had not previously been under psychiatric observation — yet two sources cited by Meduza claim this is not his first hospitalisation. Those two things can't both be true if they're referring to the same facility type.
So what's the most coherent read?
The voluntary-to-avoid-prosecution theory is actually the most strategically elegant. He's a lawyer. He knows Russian criminal procedure intimately. Checking yourself into a psychiatric facility creates immediate legal friction — you can't be interrogated, arrested easily, or charged while under inpatient care. It buys time and adds a layer of procedural ambiguity. The fact that he was actively giving interviews right up until admission, showed no signs of mental health issues in the interview, and stated he had no legal problems — and then went silent immediately after — fits a calculated move more than a breakdown.
The prior hospitalization angle you raised is significant precisely because it would mean he has a pre-existing relationship with that institution and knows exactly how it works as a protective mechanism. A man with security service connections and family/friends in the psychiatric system would know which ward to go to and how to navigate the paperwork.
The narrative of "Putin's regime disappeared him" is actually the more convenient story — for both the Western press and for Remeslo himself if he's managing his own exit. The ambiguity is the point.
