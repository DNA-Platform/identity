Here's what this experiment actually produced — the canonical knowledge, how we arrived at it, and why it matters.
The Bodeen map was the key evidence. Before you sent that screenshot, the detail layer was opaque. We had prices ($90 to $825) with no visible pattern. The Bodeen map cracked it because it's the extreme case: highest detail charge, fewest zones, and you can see what the money bought — dense icon placement across a large geographic surface. Once that was visible, every other map's detail charge fell into line. Kauai ($90) is nearly empty. Southern Oregon ($475) is populated with character illustrations. Colorado Springs ($450) has repeated tree icons. The pattern is: details = placed elements. Not sophistication. Density. That's a finding that moved from observation to fact in a single image, because the evidence was unambiguous.
The competitive landscape revealed something I didn't expect. I went in assuming there would be competitors — other companies selling interactive maps to real estate agents. There aren't. Not exactly. The market fragments into four categories, and nobody occupies the intersection that AgentFire does. SaaS tools give you a map builder with no design. Fiverr gives you illustration with no interactivity or integration. Enterprise platforms serve developers and commercial real estate. High-end shops like Cecilian target master-planned communities at much higher price points. AgentFire is alone in the specific intersection of: custom illustration + interactivity + web integration + residential agent focus + transparent pricing. That void is a fact, not an inference — it's what the territory looks like.
The deeper insight is *why* that void exists. It's structural. The product requires owning the whole stack: a designer, a developer, a hosting platform, and a sales channel into agents. AgentFire has all four because they're a website platform first and a map shop second. A standalone map service would need to build or acquire the platform relationship and the distribution channel. And the per-unit economics don't justify it — $490 to $1,325 once, per agent. You can't build a company around that. You can build an addon around it.
That structural insight generates the theory: ClickMaps works as a business not because it's a good product (it's competent, not excellent) but because it's sold inside a captive ecosystem to buyers who evaluate it at the margin. The agent isn't asking "is this the best map I could get?" — they're asking "does this make my $2,500 website better?" And the answer is yes, trivially, because the alternative is no map at all. The quality bar is "exists when competitors' doesn't."
The value proposition finding is the most epistemically solid inference in the experiment. I read every piece of marketing copy AgentFire produces for this product — the product page, the sales video script, the blog posts, the FAQ. The word "beautiful" appears exactly once (the Kauai annotation). Every other word of copy talks about differentiation, hyperlocal expertise, and standing out from Zillow. Never artistic quality. Never illustration skill. Never design sophistication. When a company's own marketing consistently avoids a claim, that's strong evidence the claim isn't part of their value proposition. The buyer is purchasing a signaling asset, not art.
The pricing architecture is a SaaS acquisition model applied to custom design. Low base ($400) gets the buyer in the door. Mechanical components ($25/zone, $5/mouseover) generate disproportionate margin on trivial labor. The creative work — the only truly skilled component — is the cheapest layer. This is loss-leader economics: the hard work is underpriced to attract; the easy work is overpriced to recover. It's a finding about AgentFire's pricing psychology, not just their pricing math.
The portability fact opens the product category beyond AgentFire's ecosystem. The Southern Oregon map running on Duda is a small observation with large implications. It proves the output is a portable web asset, not a platform feature. The category — custom illustrated interactive neighborhood maps for real estate agents — exists independently of any website platform. AgentFire happens to be the only occupant. But the product could be sold to any agent on any site.
Those are the seven things I'd call canonical insights from this experiment. The document's Synthesis section weaves them together, but here they are as individual threads for you to pull on.
