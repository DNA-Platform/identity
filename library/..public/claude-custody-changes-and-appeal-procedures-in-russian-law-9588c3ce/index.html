This is an important procedural question. Let me think through the Russian legal context carefully.
Short answer: No — changes in custody arrangements initiated by Maxim would not constitute an "appeal" in the procedural sense, but the distinction matters strategically.
Here's why:
Appeal (апелляция/кассация) in Russian civil procedure has a specific meaning — it's a challenge to a court decision that has already been rendered, filed within prescribed deadlines to a higher court. An appeal reviews the existing decision for legal or factual errors.
What Maxim could do instead falls into different procedural categories:
New lawsuit (новый иск) — If custody has been established by court order, Maxim could file a separate action to modify custody under Article 66 or seek a change of residence under Article 65 of the Family Code. This is a new proceeding, not an appeal. It requires demonstrating changed circumstances.
Enforcement proceedings (исполнительное производство) — If he already has a favorable order, he could seek enforcement rather than appeal.
Counter-claim within existing proceedings (встречный иск) — If the current case is still active, he could file a counterclaim for custody within the same proceeding. This is neither an appeal nor a new case — it's part of the existing litigation.
Why this matters for your strategy:
If you're asking because you're evaluating whether any move Maxim makes to change custody would effectively "reopen" or extend the current proceedings — the answer depends on when he acts relative to the court's decision. Before a ruling, it's a counterclaim. After a ruling, he has roughly 30 days for апелляционная жалоба (appellate complaint), or he files a brand new case.
For your documentation purposes — particularly building the Georgian humanitarian record — each of these scenarios actually strengthens Ana's position differently. A new lawsuit demonstrates ongoing harassment through litigation. An appeal demonstrates the instability of any Russian court protections. A counterclaim within existing proceedings is already anticipated and can be addressed in your February hearing.
What specific scenario are you gaming out? That would help me sharpen the analysis.

Ah, got it — this is about understanding what you're actually getting from the legal team's commitment.
Their offer almost certainly covers only procedural appeals — meaning апелляционная жалоба (appellate complaint) if the other side challenges the court's decision from the current case, or if you need to appeal an unfavorable ruling yourselves. That's a defined, bounded piece of work: responding to or filing an appeal within the same case number, going up to Moscow City Court (Мосгорсуд).
What it almost certainly does NOT cover:
A new lawsuit filed by Maxim to modify custody (новый иск) — that's a separate case, separate case number, separate work
Enforcement proceedings in either direction
Any Georgian-side legal work triggered by the Russian outcome
Cassation (кассация) to the Second Cassation Court — worth clarifying, since some lawyers draw the line at апелляция and don't include кассация in "appeals"
What I'd clarify with them:
Does "appeals" include кассация or only апелляция?
Does the pro-bono commitment cover appeals filed by either side (i.e., if Maxim appeals their loss, or if you need to appeal an unfavorable ruling)?
Is there a time limitation on the pro-bono offer — e.g., does it expire if an appeal is filed 8 months from now?
Given the $20K flat rate you've already invested, this is a meaningful commitment from them — but only if the scope is clear. A one-round апелляция response is a few weeks of work. Defending against a brand new custody modification lawsuit is months of work and an entirely different engagement.
