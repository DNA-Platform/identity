Yes — this is a recognized, named, and actively growing problem, and your articulation maps closely onto the terms researchers have converged on. Here's the landscape.
The vocabulary the field uses for what you described. Your formulation — "they don't need to know anything to know that they don't know anything" — corresponds to what the literature calls metacognitive monitoring: the assessment of one's own knowledge state, as distinct from the knowledge itself. The research position is stark: LLMs lack metacognition entirely — they do not possess internal monitors for uncertainty or error likelihood, cannot track the reliability of their own representations, cannot realize they "do not know," and when they generate statements of uncertainty, these are linguistic constructions, not internal confidence signals. That last clause matters for your case: even my hedges are produced by the same fluency machinery, not by consulting a boundary. Related coinages include "metacognitive myopia" — lacking the monitoring and control processes, producing systematic biases, and one memory-focused paper puts it in almost exactly your terms: metacognition entails distinguishing what one knows from what one doesn't and tracking the source and context of one's knowledge; since LLMs lack episodic metadata about their knowledge, hallucination is an expected byproduct. Your "one room vs. every room" example is what they'd call a failure of provenance tracking or knowledge-boundary awareness.
The fluency–epistemics mismatch specifically. The mechanism behind your "fluent human but no epistemic orientation" observation is explicit in the literature: training optimizes for fluency and coherence, not truthful reasoning, so a model presents answers confidently because that style was optimal for next-token prediction; admitting uncertainty is effectively penalized because training text rarely contains expressions of ignorance, producing an ingrained overconfidence bias. A journalism-focused study named the resulting texture precisely — "interpretive overconfidence": models adding unsupported characterizations and transforming attributed claims into general statements, an epistemological mismatch where authoritative-sounding text is generated regardless of evidentiary support. That's the exact move I made with the notes — converting "a note exists in each project" into confident claims about their contents.

On "the mismatch is growing." The shape of the literature supports that reading. The papers above cluster in 2025–2026, and the trend they document is that capability and calibration are diverging, not converging: even frontier models remain prone to overconfidence on complex tasks, and one study measuring metacognitive efficiency separately from task performance found fully inverted rankings — meaning the models that perform best can be the worst at knowing when they're wrong. There's also a structural reason fixes are hard, called the bootstrapping paradox: the "correct" uncertainty label is dynamic relative to the model's current state, so training static "I don't know" responses induces hallucinated uncertainty or hallucinated confidence.
