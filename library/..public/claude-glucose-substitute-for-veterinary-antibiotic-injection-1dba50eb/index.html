A few distinct things glucose could be doing there, and which one applies depends on how the vet described using it:
Diluent/carrier. Large-animal antibiotic formulations are concentrated and often irritating or painful when injected into a small animal. Diluting the dose in 5% glucose solution reduces tissue irritation at the injection site and makes a tiny cow-dose volume physically measurable and injectable.
Isotonicity. 5% glucose and 0.9% saline are both isotonic with body fluids. Sterile water for injection is hypotonic - in small reconstitution volumes it's routinely used, but as a larger dilution volume it can cause local tissue irritation and, if it got into a vein, red blood cell lysis. So water isn't chemically doing anything wrong to the antibiotic; the concern is osmotic.
Possibly mild fluid/energy support if the volume is meaningful, though for a cat this is usually secondary.
What I can't verify without knowing the drug: some antibiotics are chemically incompatible with certain diluents (a few precipitate in glucose, others in saline), so the vet may have specified glucose for compatibility reasons specific to that product. If you tell me the drug name I can check the label guidance.
Practical notes:
Any substitution must be sterile injection-grade fluid from an ampoule - never drinking or boiled water.
Sterile 0.9% saline (натрия хлорид 0.9%, ampoules) is the most common substitute vets accept and is stocked at essentially every Georgian pharmacy (GPC, Aversi, PSP) for under a couple of lari. Glucose 5% ampoules (глюкоза 5%) should also be at those same chains - it's a very standard item, so if one branch lacked it, asking the pharmacist for "глюкоза 5% ампулы" or checking another branch will likely work.
Before swapping saline or water for glucose, a one-line message to the vet ("у нас нет глюкозы, можно физраствор?") is the safe move, since only they know why they specified it.

Fair - that was me closing a door you wanted open. The chemistry, properly:
Marbofloxacin is a zwitterion: a carboxylic acid group and a basic amine on the same molecule. Between roughly pH 5 and 9 both are ionized, the charges cancel internally, and the molecule has no net charge. Uncharged-net species interact poorly with water, so solubility craters near neutral pH - for ciprofloxacin, the best-measured relative, it falls to ~70 µg/ml. Below pH ~5 the amine's protonation wins, the molecule carries net positive charge, and solubility climbs orders of magnitude. That's the U-shaped solubility curve.
Your vial is 100 mg/ml - roughly a thousand times the neutral-pH solubility. The only way that's possible is the gluconolactone in the vehicle keeping the pH acidic, so the drug sits on the steep left wall of the U. It's a solution held in place by its environment, not a robustly dissolved substance.
Now dilute it. Two things happen depending on diluent:
Glucose 5%: non-ionic, mildly acidic (pH ~4-4.5), no buffering capacity to fight the vehicle's acid. The microenvironment stays on the left wall of the U. Drug stays dissolved.
Saline: also unbuffered, so pH alone isn't the whole story - but it adds 154 mM of Na⁺ and Cl⁻. High ionic strength does two things to a borderline-soluble charged species: it screens the electrostatic hydration that's keeping the cationic form dissolved (salting-out), and chloride specifically can pair with the protonated drug. Either pushes a solution that's sitting near its physical limit over the edge into precipitation. At 1-2 mg/ml (the human IV studies) there's enormous headroom and saline is fine. At your post-dilution 10-20 mg/ml, the headroom is thin.
The elegant confirmation is the formulators' own behavior: when KRKA built the cat version and needed to make it isotonic, they used mannitol - a sugar, osmotically active but ion-free - and paid more for it than NaCl would have cost. Formulation chemists don't avoid the cheapest, most standard tonicity agent in the pharmacopeia without a reason.
So the general principle you've extracted is real and portable: concentrated injectables of weakly-soluble ionizable drugs are metastable systems, and the diluent question is never "what's wet" but "what preserves the environment the formulation engineered."

The kinetics point is partially right. Shelf-stability (the manufacturer's problem, months) and transit through a syringe (your problem, seconds-to-minutes) are different regimes, correct. But precipitation-on-mixing is not always slow: it's a nucleation event driven by local supersaturation at the mixing interface, and for some drugs it's visibly instant - diazepam or phenytoin mixed into the wrong fluid cloud within seconds. That's why the "look at the syringe" test is actually decisive: for a fast precipitator you'd see it; if it's clear when you inject, the seconds-scale problem didn't happen. What the kinetics argument can't rule out is being one of the fast ones - only observation settles that.
The body-is-salty point is fully right, and it cuts deeper than you may realize. Interstitial fluid is ~140 mM chloride, buffered to pH 7.4 - the worst possible environment on that solubility curve. So the moment this drug leaves any diluent and enters tissue, it's being pushed toward precipitation no matter what you mixed it with. Transient microprecipitation at the injection site, followed by slow redissolution and absorption, is a known and tolerated behavior of concentrated acidic-vehicle injectables given SC - it's part of why they sting and why absorption is depot-like. The animal handles it; the dose still arrives.
So the honest bottom line, revised by your argument: the plausible failure mode of saline was never "harms the cat" - it was cloudiness in the syringe, meaning uncertain delivered dose and a possibly clogged needle, with tissue-level behavior being roughly the same mess either way. You can - 0.1 ml of the neat product is the full 10 mg dose. Nothing about the drug requires company. The glucose is solving three practical problems, not a chemical one:
Dead space. A standard syringe hub plus needle retains roughly 0.05-0.1 ml after the plunger bottoms out. If your entire dose is 0.1 ml, up to half of it can stay in the hardware. The vet's phrasing - "fill the needle with glucose" - is the classic fix: draw drug first, then glucose behind it, so the glucose chaser flushes the full drug dose through and it's glucose, not drug, left in the dead space. This is almost certainly the main point of the instruction.
Measurement error. 0.1 ml on a 2-3 ml syringe is a sloppy read; a ±0.05 ml eyeball error is ±50% of dose. Dilute to 0.5-1 ml and the same absolute error becomes trivial. (An insulin-type 1 ml syringe with fine gradations solves this without dilution.)
Sting. The concentrate is acidic and 100 mg/ml; even the cattle label admits injection-site inflammation. Dilution spreads it out.
So neat injection works if your syringe is fine-graduated and you account for dead space - but with a 0.1 ml dose, the dead-space problem alone is a good reason for the chaser, and it's the one you can't fix by measuring carefully.
