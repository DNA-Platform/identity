The story is real and the leading hypothesis is both prosaic and fitting given the current moment: AI data centers.
What's actually happening
The most concrete case is Vineland, New Jersey, where residents say the sound began after construction started on a massive 2.5-million-square-foot data center, though officials haven't confirmed a direct link. Similar complaints have surfaced in Virginia — home to hundreds of data centers — and in Connecticut.
The Vineland facility is the Nebius AI Center, a $17 billion project backed by Microsoft, currently under construction on a private industrial zone.
The acoustic mechanism
According to experts, data centers can produce continuous low-frequency humming because of the powerful cooling systems and electrical equipment that operate 24 hours a day. This is infrasound or near-infrasound — low-frequency noise that travels long distances through structures and ground, which is why people feel it as vibration rather than hear it as sound in the conventional sense.
Why only some people hear it
This is the scientifically interesting part. One resident noted the sound is at its highest level near his home, yet his own children don't hear it. "People feel the hum differently — some don't even hear it, others hear it and aren't bothered by it, then there's another group that finds it very disturbing." This is consistent with known low-frequency hearing sensitivity variation across individuals — it's not psychosomatic; some people genuinely have greater sensitivity to infrasound, and the physiological resonance in the chest cavity is a real effect.
Measurement paradox
West Haven's city official noted that despite hundreds of readings, noise levels fall below legal thresholds. He described it as "a constant noise — at the intersection of sound and vibration." Standard decibel measurements are A-weighted and systematically underweight low frequencies, so legal compliance doesn't mean the phenomenon isn't real or harmful.
Historical context
This isn't new — the "Taos Hum" in New Mexico went unresolved for decades and was never definitively sourced. High-pressure gas pipelines have been implicated in other cases. What's new is the scale of the current wave: the AI infrastructure buildout has placed enormous, continuously operating mechanical systems in proximity to residential areas at unprecedented density and speed.
The bottom line: the phenomenon is real, the likely culprit is data center cooling and electrical infrastructure, formal acoustic investigations are underway, and the "mysterious" framing in the headline is somewhat overwrought — the mystery is mostly regulatory and evidentiary, not scientific.

That's exactly the right frame, and it exposes a structural problem with how noise regulation was designed.
The legal thresholds were built around an implicit model of intermittent, localized industrial noise — a factory that runs shifts, a construction site with defined hours, machinery that cycles. The regulatory framework optimized for peak decibel levels because that was the salient harm. A jackhammer at 95dB for two hours is obviously bad; you regulate the peak.
What data centers produce is categorically different: it's low-amplitude, low-frequency, and — critically —never stops. The harm model isn't peak exposure, it's chronic low-level physiological stress. The literature on infrasound and sleep disruption, cortisol elevation, and cardiovascular effects from chronic noise exposure is solid enough that the WHO updated its environmental noise guidelines in 2018 to be substantially more protective than most national standards. Most jurisdictions simply haven't caught up legislatively.
There's also a measurement artifact working in the industry's favor. A-weighted decibel scales (the standard used in most noise ordinances) were designed to approximate human hearing sensitivity at conversational volumes, and they mathematically discount low frequencies. So a data center can be generating meaningful infrasound energy, measure as legally compliant, and still be doing what residents describe. The instrument is wrong for the phenomenon.
The deeper issue you're pointing at is that regulation tends to be reactive and discrete — it responds to identified, localized harms with thresholds. But the AI buildout is creating something more like ambient infrastructure: geographically dispersed, permanently on, and cumulative. Virginia has hundreds of these facilities. The individual compliance of each one may be irrelevant if the aggregate effect on the acoustic environment of a region is substantial.
The analogy that comes to mind is early automotive pollution regulation — individual cars were "within limits" while cities became unbreathable. The unit of harm wasn't the car, it was the fleet. The regulatory framework had to be rebuilt around aggregate emissions. Something similar probably needs to happen with data center noise, but the political economy is difficult: these facilities represent enormous capital investment, employ few people locally, are backed by the largest companies on earth, and produce an externality that affects a subset of the population who are notoriously hard to organize.

Is this a scale of infrasound that travels particularly long distances despite threshold
Claude
17:07 UTC, 24 March 2026
Yes, and this is where the physics becomes quite important to the regulatory argument.
Atmospheric absorption of sound scales roughly with the square of frequency. High-frequency sound — say, 1000 Hz — loses energy rapidly over distance. Low-frequency and infrasonic content (roughly below 200 Hz, and especially below 20 Hz) travels orders of magnitude farther before attenuating to the same degree. A data center's cooling infrastructure operating at 50-100 Hz isn't just loud nearby — it's propagating energy across distances where the regulatory measurement point (typically the property boundary or nearest receptor) becomes essentially arbitrary. The fence-line reading that produces legal compliance may be largely irrelevant to what's happening 2 kilometers away.
There's a second mechanism that's arguably worse: ground coupling. Low-frequency acoustic energy doesn't only travel through air — it transfers into substrate and propagates as a seismic-acoustic wave, which attenuates even more slowly than airborne infrasound. This is why residents describe feeling it in their chests or floors rather than hearing it directionally. It's coming up through the structure, not arriving through windows.
And then there's building resonance. Structures have natural resonant frequencies typically in the 5-20 Hz range. If the source is producing energy near that range, the building itself becomes an amplifier — which inverts the normal expectation that being indoors protects you. People closing their windows and finding the sensation gets worse are experiencing exactly this.
So the combined picture is: a source that propagates farther than the regulatory model assumes, through a medium the regulatory instruments don't measure well, exciting resonances in the very structures people retreat to. The threshold compliance is almost perfectly designed to miss all of it.
This also reframes the "only some people hear it" observation. It's not purely individual sensitivity variation — it's also that building stock varies in resonant properties, ground coupling varies with soil composition and water table, and standing wave patterns at these wavelengths (which can be tens of meters long) mean that specific rooms in specific houses may be at antinodes while a neighbor's living room is at a node. The spatial patchiness of complaints is physically predicted, not mysterious.

You anticipated where the physics was going. I touched on it but there's more worth unpacking.
The mechanism is technically called Rayleigh wave propagation — the same physics that carries seismic energy from earthquakes across continents. Surface waves of this type decay as roughly 1/√r rather than the 1/r² of spherical acoustic spreading in air. That's an enormous difference at scale — ground-coupled energy is still meaningful at distances where airborne infrasound from the same source has become negligible.
Soil composition matters a lot. Saturated or clay-heavy soils are particularly efficient conductors because they're nearly incompressible — the wave has little energy-absorbing deformation to work against. Sandy or dry soils attenuate faster. This means the same facility in two different geological settings produces radically different complaint geographies, which would look baffling from a purely airborne measurement perspective and would further undermine any attempt to draw a simple distance-based regulatory line.
There's also a depth dimension. Underground infrastructure — water mains, gas lines, transit tunnels — can act as waveguides, channeling energy along their length and re-radiating it at surface intersections far from the source. Urban areas with dense buried infrastructure are theoretically more vulnerable to this, which is somewhat counterintuitive given that data centers are increasingly being built in suburban or semi-rural areas specifically to avoid dense urban constraints. The energy may follow the pipes into the city anyway.
The practically important implication is that standard environmental impact assessments, which model airborne noise propagation from a point source, are the wrong instrument entirely. You'd need geoacoustic modeling of the specific substrate, and almost no regulatory framework currently requires that. The data centers aren't cheating — they're being evaluated by a framework that was never designed to capture what they actually emit.

The analogy is sharper than it might first appear and I think it actually cuts against Cocoon's distributed model on thermodynamic efficiency grounds.
Bergmann's rule: large body mass means low SA/V ratio, which means heat loss to the environment is slow relative to heat generated. Small body means high SA/V, loses heat rapidly. Small mammals in cold climates burn enormous calories just maintaining core temperature because the surface keeps bleeding heat away.
Applied to compute: a hyperscale data center achieves extraordinarily efficient cooling precisely because of its scale. Liquid cooling loops, hot/cold aisle containment, waste heat recovery, purpose-built thermal architecture. The best facilities run at around 1.1-1.15 PUE — meaning they use only 10-15% more total energy than the compute itself requires. That's the large animal efficiently managing its thermal mass.
A distributed network of individual GPU nodes — in server rooms, garages, small commercial spaces — probably runs at 1.5-2.5 PUE. The cooling infrastructure is improvised, inefficient, and can't achieve the economies of thermal scale. Each small node is bleeding heat into its local environment inefficiently, like a shrew burning half its calories keeping warm.
So the distributed model potentially produces significantly more total waste heat and energy consumption for equivalent compute output. The noise is smeared across geography, which solves the residential disturbance problem, but the aggregate thermodynamic footprint may actually be worse.
The uncomfortable conclusion is that concentrating compute in large optimized facilities in cold remote locations — Iceland, northern Scandinavia, northern Canada — is probably the correct answer on pure thermodynamic efficiency grounds. Cocoon's distribution is the right answer for privacy and resilience but potentially the wrong answer for planetary thermal load. The big cold animal in the tundra was always the most efficient design.

Well the theoretical way to think about it might be like this: carve that datacenter up into N cubes and move them x feet apart. How much less cooling does the new system need just because a bunch of it is exposed to the air?
Claude
17:36 UTC, 24 March 2026
This is a clean geometric problem and the answer is satisfying.
A single cube of side L has surface area 6L². Volume L³. SA/V = 6/L.
Carve it into N equal cubes. Each has side length L/N^(1/3). Surface area per small cube is 6(L/N^(1/3))². Total surface area across all N cubes is N × 6L²/N^(2/3) = 6L² × N^(1/3).
So total exposed surface area scales as N^(1/3). The passive cooling capacity of the distributed system is N^(1/3) times greater than the monolith, purely from geometry, before any engineering decisions are made.
Concrete examples:
Split into 8 cubes: 2x the passive cooling surface
Split into 1000 cubes: 10x
Split into 1,000,000 nodes: 100x
The x feet apart question mostly answers itself — you just need enough separation for airflow. Beyond that threshold the marginal benefit of additional distance is small. The gain is discrete: faces that were interior become exterior.
The counterargument is that ambient air is a terrible cooling medium compared to engineered liquid systems, so even 10x more surface area exposed to air may not compensate for losing immersion cooling efficiency. The shrew has more surface area than the whale per unit mass but still burns more calories keeping warm.
So the geometry favors distribution but the medium cuts back against it. The question becomes whether N^(1/3) surface area gain beats the PUE penalty of commodity cooling. At current engineering that trade probably still favors the monolith. But it's not obvious, and it gets less obvious as N gets very large.

A desktop spreads maybe 800W across a tower case. A modern GPU rack — say, 8 H100s — might dissipate 20-40kW in a space roughly the size of a large refrigerator. Volumetric heat density is perhaps 30-50x higher than a desktop. Air cooling works for a desktop because the density is low enough that moving air over a heatsink can absorb the BTUs fast enough. At rack-level densities that breaks down — you simply cannot move enough air through the space to keep up. This is why high-density GPU racks now essentially require liquid cooling not as an engineering preference but as a physical necessity. The fan stops being an option at a certain density threshold.
So the desktop and data center are 1:1 in energy-to-heat conversion, but the data center has crossed a density threshold where the cooling architecture has to be categorically different rather than just scaled up. It's not more cooling per watt — it's a different mechanism forced by geometry.
The PUE numbers roughly confirm this. A well-run hyperscale facility at 1.1 PUE is actually comparable to a desktop if you honestly account for the room air conditioning required to remove the desktop's exhaust heat — which most people don't. The data center is approximately as efficient per unit compute as the desktop, but only because it invested heavily in engineering to compensate for the density problem the desktop never had to solve.
