Where canonicals enter, using the condition from The Semantics of Canonicals (both collapses on one pair, both orders):
text
(item, container) == container        :: item represents container   (::rep.2)
(container, item) == container        :: container refers to item     (::ref.2)
The second line is container =container> item. So within container<item>, the canonical items are exactly those the container refers back to. Non-canonical items are in the collection — they represent the container — but the container does not refer to them, so nothing about the container lets you recover them. Canonicity is the recoverability: knowing the container, you know the item. That is also what "canonical representative of an equivalence class" means in ordinary mathematics, though here the analogy runs outward from the ::rep/::ref pair, not inward from quotients.

Thinking about why typical wins.
The first thing is that it's about the type, not about the observer. Indexical is about how something gets reached — by pointing rather than by name — so a dichotomy built on it is really about acts of reference. Typical is about how something stands inside its type. That's the axis you actually need for a representation as a collection of representatives: the question is never "how do I get to this member" but "what is this member's standing among the others." Canonical/typical answers that; canonical/indexical answers a different question.
Second, typical is a true complement and indexical isn't. The canonical can be pointed at, so indexical doesn't exclude it; the two cross. But within a type every member is either the one you'd study to understand the type or one you'd recognize once you had — there's no third standing and no overlap. That exhaustiveness is what makes it usable as a division of the representatives rather than as a tag some of them happen to carry.
Third, it carries the epistemic order, and the order is the inheritance direction. Canonical is prior: you understand the type through it. Typical is posterior: it becomes visible once the type is understood. So the typical members inherit their standing from the canonical, and interchangeability among the typical is what inheritance from a single source looks like from the inside. Indexical gives no order at all.
And fourth, the pair explains something you already had. A set is a collection with no canonical — every member typical, all interchangeable, membership carrying nothing but belonging. That's exactly why the set view is a real view of representation and why set identity breaks the way Symbols vs. Sets says it does: drop the canonical and only the typical remains. Symbols are where the canonical is kept. So canonical/typical isn't just a better opposite; it locates the set/symbol distinction as a special case of itself, which indexical never could.

The best one I have is the holotype in biological taxonomy, and I'd stand behind it rather than swap it out next time.
A species name is fixed to a single physical specimen in a museum drawer. That specimen doesn't have to be a good or average individual; it's canonical by an act of designation, and the name means "whatever is the same kind as this." Every other individual of the species is identified by reference back to it, and they are interchangeable with each other for that purpose — none of them carries the name. It meets all four marks: unique by convention, prior to the type (the type is literally understood through it), the rest recognized once the type is fixed, and the rest substitutable among themselves. The vocabulary even confirms the structure from the inside: biology calls the canonical specimen the type, and the word for the others is the ordinary one, typical.
Two runners-up that hold up but each miss one mark. The International Prototype Kilogram at Sèvres, until 2019, was the kilogram — every other mass was a kilogram by comparison to it. Strong on priority and uniqueness; slightly weaker as an example because the 2019 redefinition shows the canonical can be moved from an object to a constant, which is interesting but complicates it. A fraction in lowest terms — 1/2 for the class 2/4, 3/6, … — is where mathematics gets the phrase "canonical form" and is perfect on uniqueness and interchangeability, but weak on priority: you don't come to understand the class by studying 1/2.
