You've reconstructed the lexeme/word-form distinction on your own — "conjugation changes context, not the relation." That's exactly right, and it's the spine of the whole verb system. Let me give it the field's machinery and then push on the part your "to be" instinct is actually pointing at.
First correction, because it unlocks everything: a verb is not an action. A verb is a relation.
The schoolroom "verb = action word" is a notional definition, and it's wrong the same way "noun = person/place/thing" is wrong — it tries to define a formal category by its typical meaning. The formal definition is distributional and morphological: a verb is what inflects for tense and fills the finite slot. What it denotes is a predicate — a relation over its arguments — and its valency is its type signature (this is Session 15, arriving early):
text
laugh   : ⟨x⟩         laugh(x)          intransitive  — unary relation
kick    : ⟨x,y⟩       kick(x,y)         transitive    — binary
give    : ⟨x,y,z⟩     give(x,y,z)       ditransitive  — ternary
be      : ⟨x, P⟩      be(x, P)          copular       — links x to a predicate P
Now your "to be" puzzle resolves. be is the most relational verb and the least active — it does almost no semantic work, just asserts a link (the box is red ≈ predicate red of box; Doug is a programmer ≈ a typing/membership judgment). It "defaults to being an action" only in the sense that English has no separate grammatical home for "stative linker," so the copula rents the verb slot. It's a verb by form and position, not by meaning. That's the category/function distinction biting at the level of the verb itself — and be proves it by being a verb while doing nothing a verb is "supposed" to do.
Now your real question: same relation, different context. English's stance is the inflection/derivation boundary.
The relation lives at the lexeme, which is invariant. The word-forms are that lexeme evaluated at context-coordinates:
text
lexeme WALK              the relation itself
word-forms(WALK)         WALK indexed by ⟨person, number, tense, finiteness⟩
Inflection varies the coordinates without changing the type — same relation, new context. Derivation changes the type — new relation (WALK → walker, V→N). So English draws your exact line morphologically: if the difference is inflectional, it's the same word; if it requires derivation, it's a new one. Your intuition isn't informal — it is the criterion.
And the parameterization is closed and finite. SIEG's claim (Session 6): every English verb has exactly six inflectional forms, no more:
plain present — walk (non-3sg present)
3rd-singular present — walks
preterite — walked
plain form — walk (infinitival, imperative, subjunctive)
gerund-participle — walking
past participle — walked
For regulars these collapse to four distinct shapes (walk / walks / walked / walking) — heavy syncretism. The coordinates being varied are just who (agreement) and when/what-aspectual-role (tense + the participles that feed the auxiliary pipeline). Note what's not there: no "future" form. Future is built compositionally (will + plain form), not inflected — English has only two tenses (Session 8).
The exception that proves be is special: it's the one verb that overflows six. It has eight forms — be, am, is, are, was, were, being, been — three distinct present forms where every other verb has two, two distinct preterites where every other verb has one. The maximally relational verb is also the maximally inflected one.
Multiword predicates — Ana's "get in."
This is the verb-side twin of your determiner discovery. There you found multiple orthographic words composing into one referring unit. Here: multiple words composing into one relation. The lexeme is not the typographic word.
look up (= "search for") is a single lexeme spelled across whitespace — and its particle can be spilled to the far side of the object:
text
look up the word   ✓
look the word up    ✓     particle is mobile
look it up          ✓
*look up it               (pronoun forces the split)
Contrast a prepositional verb, where the "little word" is a genuine preposition fused to its complement and can't move:
text
look at the picture  ✓
*look the picture at        preposition won't strand
look at it           ✓
So the diagnostic is mobility: particle moves → phrasal verb (one lexeme); preposition stays glued to its NP → prepositional verb. A third case is just verb + PP adjunct — look in the drawer — fully compositional, no lexeme fusion. Three structures, one surface shape (Session 17).
get in sits on the seam. get in (intransitive) vs. get in the car (with complement) — and CGEL's move is to call in a preposition in both, transitive in one, intransitive in the other, killing the separate "particle" category. That's one of the open prompts already sitting in the curriculum, and you'll have a view on it.
The unifying claim: the relation lives at the lexeme, and the lexeme is independent of two things you might naively bind it to — its inflected shape (walk/walked = one relation) and its orthographic word count (look up = one relation across two tokens). Both your determiner work and this are the same discovery: the formal unit is not the typographic word.
Here's where I'd push you, since it's your home turf. A phrasal verb is precisely where the lexer/parser boundary breaks down in natural language. Semantically look up wants to be lexed as one token. Syntactically it must be parsed as two, because material can be interpolated (look the word up). So:

Your conclusion is right and your mechanism is wrong, and the gap between them is the whole lesson.
The mechanism breaks on one test. If be appeared because the subject is the sole argument — because a unary predicate forces the actor to "exist in relation to the action" — then verbs with more arguments shouldn't need it. But they all do:
text
I am laughing               be(laughing)            arity 1
I am kicking the ball        be(kicking the ball)    arity 2
I am giving him the book     be(giving …)            arity 3
be shows up at every arity. So it isn't tracking valency. It's tracking the aspectual operator — the progressive is be + gerund-participle, full stop, and it doesn't care how many arguments the inner verb has.
But your conclusion — that these be's are one thing — is correct, and you got there before the mechanism did. Here's what be actually is: the verb with no content of its own, whose job is to bear tense for a predicate that can't bear tense itself. tall can't inflect; a programmer can't; the participle laughing can't; the participle kicked can't. Each needs a finite host. be is that host:
text
is tall          be + AdjP
is a programmer  be + NP
is laughing      be + V-ing   → "progressive"
is kicked        be + V-en    → "passive"
One lexeme, four complement categories. "Copula" and "auxiliary" are just names for which complement turned up — not different verbs. The proof: be carries auxiliary syntax even as a main verb. It inverts and negates with no DO-support in every row — is he tall?, isn't he laughing, is he kicked? — while every other main verb needs the prop (*laughs he? → does he laugh?). So be is always an operator; the only variable is what it operates on. That answers the question I left you with: one lexeme, with the seam in the complement, not in the verb.
Now the deep part — because your instinct wasn't empty, it was pointing at something silent. You said an action implies the actor "exists in some way that relates them to it." There is an existence buried in every verb. It's just not the be you can see. Davidson's move: a verb quietly introduces an event argument alongside its named ones —
text
laugh(x)   →   ∃e [ laughing(e) ∧ Agent(e, x) ]
"I laughed" asserts there exists a laughing event of which I am the agent. That ∃e is real, and it surfaces nakedly in the one construction where existence is the whole point: there is a problem — existential be. So the family is tighter than you guessed: copular be, progressive be, existential be, and the silent ∃e inside every ordinary verb. Your "must be existing in some way" is the event variable. You felt it; English just doesn't pronounce it outside the copula and the existential.
So 0 vs. 1, stated cleanly: same lexeme, same predicate, same event. What differs is framing. The progressive takes the event and stativizes it — recasts "a laughing happened" as "I am in the midst of laughing," a state holding of me now. And states are exactly what be links a subject to. The progressive borrows the copula not because laugh is unary, but because PROG manufactures a state and be is the state-linker. The link you felt is real — it's one predicate under two aspectual lenses (that's Session 9, arriving early again).
Push-back for you, on your own turf: if be is contentless tense-support for a complement, what is it doing in I think, therefore I am — where it takes no complement at all? Either that's a fifth be (pure existential, ∃ with nothing to host), or your unification has to swallow it. Where do you put the bare one — and does its existence force you to make existence primary, with predication the special case?

text
The ocean polished the rock.      subject = ocean   = agent
The rock was polished by the ocean. subject = rock    = patient
John underwent surgery.            subject = John    = patient
The book sold well.                subject = book    = neither
It rained.                         subject = "it"    = nothing at all
This tent sleeps four.             subject = tent    = location
Subject is a grammatical function — a structural slot. Agent is a thematic role — a semantic relation the verb assigns. They usually align in the canonical active clause, which is exactly why you fused them. But they're independent dimensions. And you've met this exact move before: it's the category/function distinction — the spine of the whole project — now wearing a new costume. What a constituent is structurally (subject) vs. what role it plays semantically (agent). Type vs. interface, a third time.
The three-layer model. The verb defines a relation with n slots, each tagged with a θ-role. A linking rule then maps roles onto grammatical functions:
text
Verb supplies:   polish⟨Agent, Patient⟩          ← the relation, with role tags
Linking maps:    Agent→Subj, Patient→Obj          ← active
                 Patient→Subj, Agent→by-phrase     ← passive
Grammar exposes: arity + which-is-subject/object   ← directional, structural
Grammar hides:   the θ-roles + entailments         ← these live in the lexeme
Now your code smell, made precise — because you were right. "Being polished by" isn't a relationship type. It's a view on the polish relation. The relation polish(ocean, rock) is invariant; active and passive are two serializations of it with different promotion-to-subject. The by-phrase is the demoted agent — the trace of the original subject. Passive is a refactoring that preserves the relation and changes which argument is topicalized. Your instinct that "by" smells like the arrow pointing backward is exactly correct: it's the marker that the normal linking has been inverted. (This is Session 20, information packaging — passive as restructuring, not as "another way to say the same thing.")
Earth and sun. You felt that English "credits the earth with doing something" and you "can't express it the other way." Right observation, wrong cause. It isn't agency — move is a theme-subject verb: its single argument is the thing-in-motion, role Theme, not Agent. The earth is subject because that's move's linking, full stop. And you can't flip it because there's no object to promote — passive needs a patient, and move (around the sun) has none. The directionality you feel isn't agency leaking in; it's that English requires a subject (even a dummy: "it rained") and this verb's only argument is forced into it. Grammatical asymmetry, not semantic.
"She looked up the word." You asked: is the word in a relationship of her-having-found-it? No — and this is the cleanest case. look up assigns the word the role Theme/Goal-of-search, and success is not entailed:
text
She looked up the word but it wasn't in the dictionary.   ✓ — no finding
She found the word but didn't look it up.                 ✓ — finding, no search
So the relation is "directed search toward a target," not "found." You cannot read that off the grammar — the grammar only tells you there are two arguments and which is subject. The entailment lives in the lexical entry of look up. That's the whole answer to your last line.
"It's hard to understand relationship from English grammar." You're right, and now provably so: grammar underdetermines the relation. Syntax gives you arity and grammatical functions — a direction-bearing frame that's obligatory even when the world has no direction (earth/sun, "it rains"). But the θ-roles and the entailments are not in the syntax. They're in the verb. Grammar is the frame; the verb is the content. The asymmetry you keep bumping into is real but it's grammatical asymmetry (subject ≠ object is an ordered structure English forces on every clause), not semantic asymmetry. The mismatch is the lesson, not a flaw in your perception.
And here's the through-line. The relation is invariant across active and passive — just as the lexeme was invariant across conjugation, just as the referring unit was invariant across orthographic words. Three times now you've found the same thing: the meaningful unit is not the surface frame. Conjugation is a view on a verb; whitespace is a view on a lexeme; subject/object is a view on a relation. The relation sits in the verb and doesn't move; grammar just chooses how to serialize it.

This is the converse-pair question from earlier, but you've sharpened it into something better — you've added the condition. Not "when can you flip a sentence" but "when both participants are agency-capable, does the language tend to give you both phrasings?" That condition is the thesis, and I think it's largely right, with one important place it cracks.
The pattern holds cleanest exactly where you guessed — transfer and exchange, where two perspective-capable participants are both genuinely involved:
text
give / receive      John gave Mary the book   /  Mary received the book from John
sell / buy          John sold Mary the car    /  Mary bought the car from John
lend / borrow       John lent Mary money      /  Mary borrowed money from John
teach / learn       She taught me Greek       /  I learned Greek from her
send / get          He sent her a letter      /  She got a letter from him
Two agency-capable poles, two verbs, each seating one pole as subject. And notice these are the relations where both participants have a real stake and a real vantage — a transaction has two sides that each experience it. The language cached both because both perspectives are worth having. Your condition predicts this: it's precisely when both ends are perspective-capable that doubling the lexeme pays off, because there's a genuine second camera worth building.
Now the crack, and it's the interesting part. Symmetric relations — where both poles are agency-capable and the relation is mutual — often don't give you a converse, because they don't need one:
text
marry       John married Mary    ⟺  Mary married John     same verb, flip is free
meet        John met Mary        ⟺  Mary met John
Here both participants are maximally agency-capable, but the verb is already symmetric, so there's nothing to cache — the single verb covers both perspectives at once. So your condition isn't sufficient: two agency-capable poles give you a converse pair only when the relation is asymmetric. When it's symmetric, the two perspectives collapse into one and the language stores one verb. The converse pair is the language's solution to asymmetric relations between two vantage-capable participants — it manufactures the second perspective that the asymmetry would otherwise deny you.
And here's where it ties to everything from today. Why does give/receive get two verbs but the ice melted gets a perspective-less default? Because melt has only one agency-capable participant — the agent who melts it; the ice can't have a vantage, so there's no second camera to build, so no converse exists. You can passivize (the ice was melted), but you can't converse it — there's no verb meaning "the ice underwent-melting-from-an-agent's-doing" with the ice as a perspectival subject, because the ice isn't perspectival. Converse pairs only exist where there are two perspective-capable poles to seat. That's your condition stated as a law: the lexicon caches a converse iff both arguments are perspective-capable and the relation is asymmetric. One perspectival pole → no converse, only passive. Two, but symmetric → no converse, one verb suffices. Two and asymmetric → converse pair.
Which means your thesis, tightened, is a prediction about the shape of the verb lexicon: relations between two minds get doubled; relations between a mind and a thing don't. And I think that mostly holds and is testable — go looking and you'll find the converse pairs clustering in commerce, communication, transfer, social exchange, emotion-between-people (thefear/frighten, please/like psych verbs), and you'll find them absent for relations between an agent and an inert patient. The lexicon's converse-structure is a map of where English thinks there are two minds in the room.
The one thing I'd flag honestly rather than smooth over: it's a tendency, not a closed rule, because lexical gaps are real. Murder has no clean converse — there's no single verb for "be murdered by" that seats the victim as a perspectival subject, even though a murder has two perspective-capable poles and is asymmetric. So the condition predicts a converse should exist there and the lexicon just... doesn't have one. Which fits your usage-statistics view from earlier: the language caches the second perspective when it's frequently worth taking — we often talk from the buyer's side, so buy exists; we less often need the murdered party's first-person commercial-style vantage, so no verb was minted. The gaps aren't counterexamples to the structure; they're the frequency-filter deciding which licensed converses actually got built.
So: yes, and your instinct to expect it more in bought/gave than elsewhere is exactly correct, and now you can say why — those are the two-mind relations, and two-mind relations are where the second camera is worth the lexical cost.
