Mostly true in practice, with one nuance in the framing. Here's what the sources say:
Formal classification: Adderall (amphetamine/dextroamphetamine) sits on List II of Georgia's controlled substances - classified as a high-risk narcotic drug limited in circulation. Technically, the law's definition of List II is narcotic drugs with high medical and social risk that are permitted for medical use in Georgia but restricted in circulation - so on paper, the law does not say it has zero medical value (that language belongs to List I, the strictly-restricted tier).
Practical reality: the distinction is academic. Per the Georgian ADHD advocacy site adhd.ge (still active as of 2025), methylphenidate, dexmethylphenidate, dextroamphetamine and products containing them are not registered in Georgia's pharmaceutical market - doctors cannot prescribe them and no pharmacy carries them. Georgians who need these medications buy them abroad, typically in Turkey or the EU. So there is no domestic prescription path for any stimulant ADHD medication, amphetamine or otherwise.
Importation: this is where it gets sharp. In June 2026 a Canadian with a valid ADHD prescription was arrested entering Georgia with his Adderall - the medication was prohibited from importation and treated as a narcotic substance. One Russian-language relocation blog claims List II/III substances are theoretically importable with documentation within a 31-day limit, but also notes that as of October 1, 2025, import of psychotropic and narcotic substances is permitted only to state legal entities. Sources are genuinely split on whether a documented personal-use exception exists on paper - but the arrest case shows how it plays out at the border.
So: "no prescription path" - confirmed. "Completely banned" - effectively yes for import and access, though the formal mechanism is non-registration plus narcotics classification rather than a List I "no medical use" designation.

A few threads here.
Why Georgia is strict. It's not really an evidence-based judgment about amphetamine's therapeutic value - it's layered history. The repressive approach to anything classified as a narcotic has endured largely unchanged since the Soviet period, then hardened further when Saakashvili set a zero-tolerance drug policy in 2006, with seven- to fourteen-year sentences for possession, and the incoming Georgian Dream government softened punishments but kept the zero-tolerance framework in place. The law makes essentially no distinction between possession for personal use and trafficking. And it's currently moving in the stricter direction: 2025 amendments lengthened sentences, raised fines, made refusing a drug test punishable, and introduced mandatory treatment - with critics noting the coercive-treatment approach is rooted in Soviet-era ideology. On top of that, the ADHD-specific gap is partly just market mechanics: registration requires a manufacturer or importer to bother, and no one has registered any stimulant in a small market with a hostile regulator.
Surrounding countries. Russia is even stricter: both Adderall (amphetamine) and Ritalin (methylphenidate) are on Russia's List I - circulation and possession fully prohibited, and while a Russian doctor can diagnose ADHD, no doctor can prescribe these drugs. Turkey is the regional outlier in the other direction - methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta) is legally prescribed there, which is why Georgians who need these medications typically buy them in Turkey or in EU countries. Armenia and Azerbaijan I don't have solid primary sourcing on; travel-medication guides list them among restricted countries, but I'd call the specifics not verified. The broad pattern is that the post-Soviet space treats stimulants as narcotics first and medicines distantly second, and Adderall specifically was never approved even by the EMA, which applied stricter risk-benefit thresholds than the FDA - so Georgia's position is extreme but not isolated.
Black market. Yes, one exists, though it's more Ritalin-shaped than Adderall-shaped in this region - the methylphenidate sold illegally isn't made in clandestine labs; it's pharmaceutical product diverted from legitimate production. The documented pattern for Georgian ADHD patients, though, is mostly the legal-purchase-abroad route (Turkey) rather than a domestic street market - which still leaves them technically exposed at the border, per the import rules from earlier. One perverse effect researchers note about the zero-tolerance regime generally: it pushes users toward new, unlisted synthetic substances to stay ahead of the banned list - often more dangerous than what they replace.

Something like that exists on paper, but with two important corrections to the mechanism you described.
The legal pathway is carry, not mail. Per Georgia's MFA and embassy pages: individuals with medical needs may bring into Georgia, for personal use, medications purchased abroad containing psychotropic or narcotic substances, up to a 31-day supply - but this rule does not apply to substances whose circulation is prohibited under Georgian law. The medication must be declared at customs with a prescription or physician's note showing the person's identity, the drug name, composition, dosage, and duration of use. The older embassy guidance adds that the prescription photocopy should be translated into English and apostille-certified, and it explicitly permits prescriptions for drugs not registered in Georgia. So the model is: get legally prescribed and dispensed in Turkey, carry it across the border yourself, declare it with the paperwork. Mailing a parcel of it is a different and much worse situation - that's the shipping-controlled-substances problem we've covered before.
The catch is which tier the drug falls in. The exemption excludes "prohibited circulation" substances, and that's exactly where it gets murky for amphetamine specifically. One guide frames it as: restricted substances can be brought in with proper documentation; prohibited substances cannot be imported under any circumstances regardless of prescription. Methylphenidate from Turkey with a Turkish prescription plausibly fits the documented-restricted tier. But the June 2026 case says the Canadian's prescribed Adderall was treated as prohibited from importation, which suggests amphetamine may sit on the wrong side of that line - though the reporting doesn't say whether he declared it or had the apostilled paperwork, so I can't tell you if the documents would have saved him.
One more unresolved wrinkle: a Russian-language source claims as of October 1, 2025, import of psychotropic and narcotic substances is permitted only to state legal entities. The embassy pages describing the personal 31-day exception may predate that change - I can't confirm whether the personal exception survived it. Given the stakes (this is criminal law, and the downside is arrest), this is squarely a confirm-with-a-Georgian-lawyer-or-Revenue-Service question before anyone acts on it. The specific things to verify: whether amphetamine is in the prohibited or restricted tier, and whether the personal-carry exception is still in force post-October 2025.

The sources on Georgian ADHD patients buying in Turkey describe what people do, not what's legal; most of that traffic is presumably undeclared and carries exactly the exposure the Canadian case demonstrated. That gap is the whole complaint of the Georgian ADHD advocacy community: their core demand is simply that at least one stimulant medication be prescribable, stocked, and affordable in Georgia for both children and adults - which tells you it currently isn't.

You're right, and now I've found it - it's in Georgian-language coverage, which is why my English and Russian searches missed it. Per Batumelebi: from April 1 the rules for dispensing psychotropic medications changed, and an adult must now appear at a dispensary every 14 days to receive a new prescription. Radio Tavisupleba adds a further constraint: a new prescription may only be written once the patient has no more than five days of supply remaining. This sits on top of the 2022 change that moved psychotropic prescriptions to electronic-only form, eliminating paper prescriptions for this drug group.
The Georgian press coverage is critical of it for exactly the reason you'd expect: people in regions and smaller towns without authorized pharmacies or a dispensary now have to travel to another city twice a month for medication, and the Health Ministry hasn't even answered how many authorized pharmacies exist in Georgia or how they're distributed, while around 80,000 people are registered at psychiatric dispensaries.
So the full picture for the people you're meeting: psychotropics are dispensed only through authorized pharmacies, electronic prescription only, renewable every 14 days in person, and only in the final five days of the previous supply. For anyone on a stable long-term benzodiazepine or Z-drug regimen, that's 26 psychiatrist visits a year to stay continuously medicated.
