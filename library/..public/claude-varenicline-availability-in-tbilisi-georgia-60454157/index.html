Same mechanism, different molecule — here's the sourcing.
Varenicline is not a cytisine derivative in a loose sense; it was literally built from it. The Mihalak/Molecular Pharmacology characterization describes varenicline as a nicotinic ligand based on the structure of cytisine, and the 5-HT3 pharmacology paper states plainly that varenicline is a derivative of cytisine, citing Coe et al. 2005 — the J Med Chem paper that introduced varenicline.
On mechanism, Rollema's 2010 BJP paper treats them as members of the same pharmacological class: varenicline, cytisine and dianicline are all α4β2 nAChR partial agonists studied in smoking-cessation trials. A separate trial protocol puts it directly: like its analogue varenicline, cytisine acts as a partial agonist of the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. So: same target, same partial-agonist mechanism, direct structural lineage — but two distinct compounds, not one active ingredient.
Where they diverge, since it matters for what you'd actually get:
Off-target profile differs. Varenicline is a potent partial agonist at α4β2, a weak partial agonist at α3β4, and a full agonist at α7 — cytisine's subtype profile isn't identical.
Efficacy is genuinely contested. The Croatia/Slovenia head-to-head found 24-week cessation of 32.5% on varenicline vs 23.1% on cytisine (OR 0.63), failing non-inferiority — but with higher adherence on cytisine (70.4% vs 59.2%) and fewer adverse events (IRR 0.59). Against that, the 2023 systematic reviews suggest the benefit of both may be comparable (RR 1.13 and RR 1.00), though the evidence base is limited. Note the trials compare 4 weeks of cytisine to 12 weeks of varenicline, which isn't a clean dose-matched comparison.
Glovo/Wolt in Saburtalo: yes on Wolt — Tabex 1.5mg #100 appears in PSP's Wolt catalog as a listed item, and Aversi, PSP, Pharmadepot and GPC all run pharmacy storefronts on Wolt Tbilisi. Aversi Pharmacy is also on Glovo Tbilisi. Current price at Aversi is 89.24 GEL, with a 15% discount showing.

Short version: all three are worse in Georgia than in the US. Pseudoephedrine and ephedrine aren't just behind-the-counter here — they're scheduled psychotropics.
Pseudoephedrine and ephedrine. Both were added to List III (psychotropic substances) of Georgia's special-control law by the 05.03.2014 N2043 amendment, effective 15 March 2014, along with norephedrine. The note to List III extends it to combined preparations containing ephedrine, norephedrine or pseudoephedrine, and the quantity annex sets criminal thresholds — pseudoephedrine at 0.3 / 3 / 30 grams for small / large / especially large, ephedrine and norephedrine at 0.15 / 1.5 / 15. The Health Minister's Order №22/ნ list of special-control medicines opens with exactly this category, and prescribing is on the Form №2 blank, with the form number and justification recorded in the patient's chart. Georgian press coverage of the change puts the practical result plainly: these combination products can be dispensed from a pharmacy only on a Form №2 prescription, and illegal circulation carries criminal liability. Form №2 is the non-narcotic special-control blank and has since moved to electronic-only.
So Sudafed and Bronkaid are not a mail-order or walk-in proposition here. This also sharpens the earlier three-tier framing we worked out: Sudafed isn't the OTC exception in Georgia — for the parcel channel it sits in the controlled tier, documentation mandatory.
Benzedrex. Assuming you mean the propylhexedrine inhaler (actual Benzedrine was amphetamine, which is on Georgia's List II). I did not find propylhexedrine anywhere in Lists I–IV, and found no Georgian registration or pharmacy listing — but I'd call that not verified rather than cleared, partly because Georgia's new-psychoactive-substance annex controls whole chemical classes by core structure. Internationally it's UN-unscheduled, OTC in the US, pharmacy-only in the UK, prescription in Germany and Australia.
What actually works locally: topical decongestants are unrestricted. Xylometazoline is on Wolt now — Snup spray 90mcg/dose 15ml, 9.43 GEL at PSP — and oxymetazoline (Afrin) is the same tier.
Two caveats worth holding: the consolidated annex I read carries amendment markers through 2014, and Georgia's schedules move, so re-verify before acting. And I don't know whether the April 2026 dispensary tightening for psychotropics reaches Form №2 pseudoephedrine combinations — I found nothing either way.

No — it's the continental class-scheduling model, not the US analog test. Georgia enacted a standalone New Psychoactive Substances Act (№2234-IIს, 16 April 2014, in force 1 May 2014, amended 2015 and twice in 2018). Three provisions define its architecture:
Art. 3(a) defines an NPS as a substance or admixture determined by the annexes to this law that can produce psychoactive effect — closed list, not an open-textured similarity standard.
Art. 7(3) lets the State Commission propose adding whole chemical compound classes where listing substances individually is less useful for stopping their spread.
Art. 8 is the tell: inclusion of a class is not precluded by that class containing compounds which cannot produce psychoactive effect, or produce only insignificant effect, or are already regulated as controlled substances or pharmaceuticals.
That last one is the inverse of 21 U.S.C. § 802(32), which requires both structural similarity and substantially similar or greater CNS effect, litigated compound by compound with intent-for-human-consumption. Georgia deliberately writes the effects prong out for class members and accepts over-inclusion. The annex text carries German-language fingerprints throughout — Phenethylamin, Naphthoyl-Indole, Alpha-Keto-Benzylamin, Säuremide — so it looks translated from a German-model statute rather than a US one. That's my observation from the text, not a sourced claim.
Two scope limits matter: the NPS law doesn't apply to pharmaceutical product circulation or to special-control substances, and it covers only substances not already defined by the 1961 and 1971 conventions. It's a residual regime. And the transitional Art. 9(2) instructs the Minister to make prescription-only any pharmaceutical product containing an Annex 2 class member that wasn't already Rx — so class membership can convert an OTC drug to prescription rather than criminalize it.
Fluoroamphetamines never reach the analog question. They're named individually in List I of the narcotics law — the strictest tier. 2- or 3-fluoroamphetamine, p-fluoroamphetamine, 3-FMA, 3-fluoromethcathinone and flephedrone all appear by name, each flagged with the asterisk marking a new psychoactive substance, and List I's note sweeps in isomers, esters, ethers and their salts.
Ethylphenidate is the more interesting case, and it's out of the NPS law entirely. The CND placed it in Schedule II of the 1971 Convention on 16 March 2017 (CND Dec/60/7), and UNODC treats it as the one phenidate under international control since then. Since Georgia's List III is compiled on the basis of the UN conventions and the NPS law excludes convention substances, the obligation runs to List III. Whether Georgia has actually transposed it by name I could not verify — matsne serves the current consolidated annex only as a binary .doc I can't read, and the readable compiled version is 2014-vintage, predating the 2017 decision.
Worth flagging as a textual reading rather than a verified conclusion: methylphenidate sits in List III, and List III's notes cover only drug forms and salts, reference standards, and the ephedrine/norephedrine/pseudoephedrine combinations — there is no ester or ether clause, unlike Lists I and II. Ethylphenidate is the ethyl ester where methylphenidate is the methyl ester of the same acid, so if it isn't listed by name it wouldn't be pulled in by List III's notes. As for the class annex, class 9 targets the diphenylmethylpiperidine skeleton — that's the pipradrol/2-DPMP family, separately listed — not the 2-phenyl-2-(piperidin-2-yl)acetate skeleton. Class 2 is arguable: ethylphenidate is commonly described as both a substituted phenethylamine and a substituted phenidate, and a Georgian expert examination could go either way on whether the constrained ring nitrogen counts.
Propylhexedrine, searched harder. Georgian transliterations, mis.ge, vidal.ge, matsne, the pharmacy chains — nothing. No registration, no listing, no mention in the compiled schedules I could read. Internationally it remains UN-unscheduled: OTC in the US, pharmacy-only in the UK, prescription in Germany and Australia, unscheduled in Canada. On the class annexes my reading is that it falls outside all nine — it's a cycloalkylamine with a saturated cyclohexyl ring, so no phenethylamine core, and arylcyclohexylamine means the aryl and cyclohexyl share the amine-bearing carbon, which isn't its structure. But it is a structural analog of methamphetamine, which is precisely the argument an expert examination would run at, and being unregistered here it gets no pharmaceutical carve-out under Art. 2(2)(a). Practically it lands in the unregistered-medicine bucket for parcels regardless of scheduling.

It also went bad early, in a way that reads familiarly: a 1965 Archives of Toxicology case report on a near-fatal poisoning of an almost-3-year-old with Obesin cites Rose's 1958/59 paper on drug addiction through misuse of so-called appetite suppressants — Obesin named specifically. Then the molecule splits down its enantiomers. Levopropylhexedrine, the S-enantiomer, was marketed as the anorectic Eventin in Germany and patented by Smith Kline & French in 1947, and it's the stimulant half of barbexaclone, salted to phenobarbital to offset the sedation. Treat them as same class, not same drug — and the difference that matters to you is pharmacokinetic, not mechanistic.
Does it block? Yes, that's its central claim. Cytisine competes with nicotine at the receptor and blocks its effects, so the dual action reduces urge-to-smoke by triggering some dopamine release while lessening nicotine's pleasurable effects by preventing reward-center activation. Structurally identical logic to varenicline, which produces partial agonist activity while preventing nicotine from binding. So your mental model transfers intact.
But there's a real fight over whether enough of it reaches the receptor. Cytisine's half-life is 4–5 hours versus varenicline's 24, its volume of distribution is about three-fold smaller, and it crosses the blood-brain barrier less readily. Rollema's 2010 BJP modelling is blunt about the consequence: at therapeutic doses varenicline and nicotine, but not cytisine, are predicted to extensively desensitize and to a lesser extent activate α4β2 receptors — cytisine's recommended doses, despite high in vitro potency, are predicted to give brain concentrations insufficient to affect α4β2 at all. Two caveats on that: Rollema was Pfizer, and the NEJM trial group answered that the BBB data are from nonhuman species and it isn't clear they generalize to humans. Cytisine then beat placebo in that trial anyway.
Expect less of the stimulation you're describing. Varenicline's α4β2 efficacy is around 45% of nicotine's maximum in HEK patch clamp, with 40–60% lower efficacy than nicotine at driving dopamine release, and cytisinicline is characterized as structurally similar but a less potent partial agonist. Less agonism plus less brain exposure means the "something to do instead of nicotine" component is weaker. The trade runs the other way on tolerability — cytisine's preliminary edge is on sleep disturbance and emotional reactivity, which is where varenicline's vivid-dream and insomnia reputation comes from.
Standard 25-day Tabex schedule — one 100-tablet pack is exactly one course:
Days 1–3: 1 tablet (1.5 mg) every 2 hours through your waking day — 6/day
Days 4–12: every 2.5 hours — 5/day. Quit date is day 5.
Days 13–16: every 3 hours — 4/day
Days 17–20: every 4–5 hours — 3/day
Days 21–25: every 6 hours — 2/day
You taper cigarettes across the first three days; if it isn't working, stop and retry after 2–3 months. One non-obvious warning worth taking seriously as a smoker: smoking while on it can aggravate nicotine's effects to the point of nicotine intoxication — the blockade is partial, and you're stacking agonists.
The thing I'd actually flag: 25 days is probably too short. UK guidance says running it up to 12 weeks is likely more effective and makes it roughly as effective as varenicline at equal duration, though that's outside the marketing authorization and the correct extended dose hasn't been established. Trial protocols do it by following the 25-day schedule then holding 2 tablets/day through week 12. The Italian 40-day version with a slower taper got 50.5% six-month cessation versus 55.9% for 12 weeks of varenicline and 51.0% for NRT. That reframes the non-inferiority failure I cited earlier — that trial compared 4 weeks of one drug to 12 of the other.
Standard caveats that come with the label rather than from me: contraindicated in recent MI, unstable angina, significant arrhythmia, recent stroke, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and not recommended over 65 or with renal or hepatic impairment. Advanced atherosclerosis and uncontrolled hypertension are also listed, and reported effects include dyspepsia, nausea, headache, increased appetite, dry mouth, nightmares and irritability. Worth a two-minute conversation with whoever prescribes for you before starting, mostly for interaction checking — I'm reporting the leaflet, not clearing you for it.
