Feferman's connection to Turing runs through one specific, beautiful idea, and it's worth being precise about it.
Turing's 1938 Princeton dissertation under Alonzo Church was Systems of Logic Based on Ordinals — what became known as ordinal logics. The motivation was Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. A sound theory like Peano arithmetic can't prove its own consistency statement Con(PA), but Con(PA) is true, so you can honestly strengthen the theory by adjoining it: T₀ = PA, T₁ = T₀ + Con(T₀), T₂ = T₁ + Con(T₁), and so on. At limit stages you take unions. The result is a transfinite progression of theories. Because you have to actually write the theories down, the progression isn't indexed by abstract ordinals but by ordinal notations — members of Kleene's system O of recursive notations.
Turing's headline result was a completeness theorem: the consistency progression is complete for true Π⁰₁ sentences — every true universally-quantified arithmetic statement becomes provable somewhere along the progression. But the result came with a famous sting. Turing showed the completeness can already be achieved at a notation for an ordinal as small as ω+1. That exposes the catch: the ordinal isn't doing the work; the notation is, because a cleverly chosen notation can smuggle the arithmetic content in. The dependence on notation rather than order type made the whole thing feel, in Turing's own assessment, not entirely satisfactory.
Feferman's 1962 paper Transfinite Recursive Progressions of Axiomatic Theories is where this gets systematized and pushed much further. His main moves:
He separated the principles you iterate. Turing essentially iterated consistency. Feferman organized a hierarchy — consistency, then the local (or "simple") reflection schema Prov_T(⌜φ⌝) → φ, then the uniform reflection principle ∀x(Prov_T(⌜φ(ẋ)⌝) → φ(x)), which is strictly stronger. Consistency is just the instance for φ ≡ (0=1).
With that stronger fuel he proved a sharper completeness theorem: the progression based on uniform reflection is complete for all true sentences of first-order arithmetic, not merely the Π⁰₁ ones. That's a real strengthening of Turing's theorem. At the same time Feferman gave a cleaner account of why the notation-dependence pathology arises and how unavoidable it is, so the result is both stronger and more honest about its own limits.
The deeper payoff — and the part that turned Turing's somewhat pathological device into a genuine tool — came a couple of years later in the work on predicativity. Feferman (and independently Kurt Schütte) studied autonomous progressions: ones where you're only permitted to climb to a notation for α once you've already proved, at an earlier stage, that that notation denotes a genuine well-ordering. That autonomy condition bootstraps and self-limits. Starting from predicatively acceptable foundations, the autonomous progression of ramified analysis climbs to exactly the ordinal Γ₀, now called the Feferman–Schütte ordinal — the proposed precise upper bound of predicative provability. So Turing's progressions, which originally looked like a curiosity haunted by notation-dependence, became the engine for measuring proof-theoretic strength and for drawing the line between predicative and impredicative mathematics.
If you want Feferman in his own reflective voice on all this, his essay Turing in the Land of O(z) (the title puns on Kleene's O) is exactly about what Turing did, what was wrong with it, and what he himself made of it.
One caveat for rigor's sake: the conceptual lineage above I'm confident about, but I'd verify any exact ordinal-bound claim about the path length in Feferman's completeness theorem against the paper itself before relying on it — I deliberately haven't asserted a specific bound there because I'm not certain of it from memory.

Here are the contributions broken out as distinct pieces of mathematics, in roughly the order they build on each other.
1. The foundational tool: arithmetizing metamathematics generally.
Before any of the progression results, Feferman did the unglamorous but essential work in Arithmetization of Metamathematics in a General Setting (1960). Turing's construction was somewhat informal about how a theory refers to itself well enough to adjoin its own consistency statement. Feferman made the provability predicate Prov_T a genuine object of study, parameterized by a Σ⁰₁ definition of the axiom set, and showed that the intensional details of how you arithmetize a theory matter enormously — two extensionally identical theories with different Σ⁰₁ presentations can have provability predicates that behave completely differently (this is the lineage of the later Rosser-style and "Feferman provability" subtleties). One concrete payoff he isolated there and used downstream: if the reflection formula φ is restricted to Σ₁, then reflection alwaysstrictly strengthens the theory, whereas higher-complexity reflection need not. A Σ₁-formula with n free variables defines an n-ary effectively enumerable relation, and conversely — that effectiveness is what keeps the construction inside the recursive machinery.
2. The construction itself, done rigorously with the recursion theorem.
The progression assigns to each notation a ∈ O a theory T_a, by primitive recursion on the notation structure: T_1 = PA, T_{2^a} = T_a + (reflection over T_a), and at a limit notation 3·5^e you union the theories T_{{e}(n)}. The non-obvious part is that T_a has to refer to its own Gödel apparatus while being defined, so the definition is circular; Feferman discharged this with Kleene's recursion theorem, and his later commentators note he used it in increasingly entangled ways to generate non-standard definitions of theories. A consequence he was explicit about: for a ∉ O the "theories" T_a are still defined but worthless, and can even be inconsistent — the recursion theorem ensures there is an e with {e}(0) = suc(lim(e)), a notation that lies about its own structure. Well-foundedness of O is doing real work.
3. The reflection hierarchy and the jump from consistency to uniform reflection.
This is the substantive conceptual move. Turing iterated consistency, Con(T_a) ≡ ¬Prov_{T_a}(⌜0=1⌝). Feferman organized a strict hierarchy of reflection principles to iterate instead:
consistency (a single sentence),
local/simple reflection Prov_T(⌜φ⌝) → φ (a schema, one instance per sentence φ; consistency is the φ ≡ 0=1 instance),
uniform reflection ∀x(Prov_T(⌜φ(ẋ)⌝) → φ(x)), the schema over formulas with a free variable, with ẋ the numeral of x.
Uniform reflection is strictly stronger than local, which is strictly stronger than consistency — the gap between local and uniform is exactly the ability to discharge a universally-quantified provability claim in one stroke rather than instance by instance, and that gap is what powers the next result. He considered a variant of Turing's progression where local reflections are replaced with uniform reflections.
4. Feferman's completeness theorem (1962) and its sharp price.
Turing had shown his consistency progression complete only for true Π⁰₁ sentences, and there's a clean reason he couldn't do better: the union ⋃T_a has a Σ⁰₂ provability predicate, so it cannot prove all true Π⁰₂ sentences without contradicting the arithmetical hierarchy theorem. The problem left open after Turing's thesis — whether any stronger progression can be complete for Π⁰₂ — was settled by Feferman with the result that progressions based on uniform reflection are complete not just for Π⁰₂ but for all arithmetical sentences. Stated precisely: for a progression based on the uniform reflection principle with T₀ = PA, every true arithmetical sentence θ has some a ∈ O with T_a ⊢ θ.
The crucial quantitative refinement, which is where the construction's character shows: this does not require climbing high in the ordinals. Feferman's construction gives ω^(ω^(ω+1)) as an upper bound on the order type of the notations along which you must iterate reflection to obtain a proof of a given φ. So an order type below ε₀ suffices to prove every true arithmetic statement, including ones of arbitrarily high quantifier complexity. The work is plainly not being done by the ordinal — it's being done by the choice of notation, which can encode the target sentence's content into the path. This is the rigorous form of the "unsatisfactoriness" Turing already sensed: completeness is real but it is bought with notation-dependence, not ordinal height. (Pakhomov–Rathjen–Rossegger's 2024 paper sharpens this further, giving tight bounds on the order types needed level-by-level in the arithmetical hierarchy.)
5. Feferman–Spector: incompleteness along paths.
The complement to completeness, and the result that makes the pathology unavoidable rather than incidental, is Incompleteness Along Paths in Progressions of Theories (Feferman and Spector, 1962). Completeness says there exists a notation a proving θ. It does not say a single coherent route through O collects everything. Feferman and Spector showed there are paths through O — linearly ordered, cofinal chains of notations — along which the progression fails to be complete even for Π⁰₁ truths. So you cannot replace the existential "some notation works" with a uniform "follow this path and you'll get everything." The non-uniformity is intrinsic.
6. The predicativity program: autonomous progressions and Γ₀.
This is where Turing's curiosity became a measuring instrument. In Systems of Predicative Analysis (1964), Feferman — and Schütte independently (1964/65), building on Kreisel — added an autonomy condition to the progression. You iterate systems of ramified analysis RA_α, but you are licensed to advance to a notation for α only once you have already proved, at an earlier autonomous stage, that the recursive ordering coded by that notation is a well-ordering. The system bootstraps: provable well-orderings unlock taller systems, which prove longer well-orderings, and so on, and the process self-limits. Feferman and Schütte determined the limit of predicativity as the least ordinal that cannot be proved well-ordered within an autonomous progression of ramified analysis — the ordinal Γ₀, the Feferman–Schütte ordinal.
Γ₀ has a clean intrinsic description via the Veblen functions: set φ₀(β) = ω^β, let φ{α+1} enumerate the fixed points of φα, and take common fixed points at limits. Then Γ₀ is the smallest ordinal not obtainable from 0 by ordinal addition and the Veblen functions φα(β) — equivalently the least α with φα(0) = α, the least strongly critical ordinal. ε₀ = φ₁(0) sits far below it. The modern landmark that pins this down precisely is reverse mathematics: Γ₀ is the proof-theoretic ordinal of ATR₀ (arithmetical transfinite recursion), a system Friedman isolated whose strength is exactly Γ₀ and which is equivalent over a weak base to several standard theorems of ordinary mathematics.
One honest caveat, since you'll want the real epistemic status: the identification of Γ₀ with "the limit of predicativity" is the standard textbook position but it is contested. Nik Weaver has argued comprehensively that predicative reasoning extends well beyond Γ₀, and others have pushed similar objections. The mathematical fact — that the autonomous ramified progression closes at Γ₀ — is not in dispute; what's disputed is whether autonomous progressions correctly capture the informal notion of predicative provability. Feferman himself returned to the question repeatedly over his career, producing later systems (the unfolding program, Ref*(PA(P)), and others) that reach the same Γ₀ without invoking ordinals or autonomy in their description — partly in response to exactly this kind of foundational worry.
If you want the primary sources in order: Feferman 1960 (Fund. Math. 49) for the arithmetization machinery, Feferman 1962b (JSL 27) for the completeness theorem, Feferman–Spector 1962 (JSL 27) for incompleteness along paths, and Feferman 1964 (JSL 29) for predicative analysis. The Stanford Encyclopedia's proof-theory appendix B gives the cleanest modern statement of the Turing/Feferman completeness theorems with the construction spelled out.
