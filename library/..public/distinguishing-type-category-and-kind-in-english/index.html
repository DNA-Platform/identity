The three words really do carve differently — dogs are just the worst possible test case, because a breed happens to be all three at once, so the distinctions collapse right where you're looking. A breed is a lineage (kin), a conformance to a written standard (template), and a slot in a kennel-club taxonomy. Pull those apart and the words separate.
Start from the root images. Kind shares a root with kin and kindred (German Kind, child). A kind is originally a natural family — things grouped by shared nature or origin. Asking what kind of dog asks what it is by nature. Kind is the realist word: it points at the world and claims the grouping is found there. That's why philosophers say "natural kind" and never "natural type." It even keeps a moral residue — a kind person, kindness — the disposition you have by nature.
Type is Greek typos, the mark left by a stamp or die: a template and its impressions. A type is conformity to a pattern. Blood type, data type, personality type — each is a characteristic bundle of features, and you count as that type by matching the bundle. Where kind runs bottom-up (from the thing's nature), type runs top-down (from a specification). It's the analytic, feature-based word engineers reach for.
Category is Greek katēgoria — predication, what can be said of a thing (Aristotle's categories are the ways a thing can be said to be). The decisive difference: a category is a slot in a scheme. It doesn't exist alone; it exists by contrast with its siblings. "Fiction" means what it does partly because "Non-fiction" sits beside it. Tax categories, grammatical categories, budget categories — imposed, systematic, relational. Its identity is made, not found.
So there's a gradient from found to made: kind → type → category. And the cleanest diagnostic — the sentence where only one word fits — is this: you can discover a new kind of animal, but you have to create a new category for it. Same animal. "Kind" says the world revealed a joint; "category" says your filing system failed and needs a new bin.
Your library instinct is right, and sharper than "category ≈ subject." A subject classifies by aboutness — what the book is about — a relation to something outside the book. A book about dogs is not a kind of dog. So subjects and categories can group by an external, intentional relation, where kind and type group by the thing's own constitution. That's exactly why "subject" feels unlike "type": a subject is what a thing is filed under, not what it is.
And your closing pun does real work. "A kind of type" and "a type of kind" aren't synonyms — one is a subvariety of a template, the other a variety of natural grouping. If the words were interchangeable, nesting them would collapse into the same thing. It doesn't. That non-collapse is the proof that people aren't reaching randomly: each word keeps contributing its own meaning even while it moonlights as a loose "sort of."

You can browse a category — the category is built to be browsed, it's got the flat public face — but you can't browse a kind, you can only recognise one. Recognise the kind, browse the category. I don't think I chose those verbs; they came pre-attached.

The words trade places depending on which is doing the rougher work in the sentence.
8. Categorically, the estate lawyer said, none of the jewellery could be sold before probate — categorically, meaning without exception, meaning the category has hard edges and you do not get to plead the individual case. You can't say something is "kindly not for sale" in that sense, or "typically not for sale" — typically means usually, which is the soft edge, the statistical middle of a type. So the category's adjective went absolute and the type's adjective went average, and they went to opposite ends of the same scale of firmness, which I did not plan and only saw once it was on the page.

10. And the last thing, the thing that made me want to tell you: kinds tolerate being wrong in a way categories don't. If I miscategorise the inkwell it's an error, a category error, the auction house sends it back. But if I say it's a strange kind of thing and I'm a little off, nobody's wrong, the kind just stretches, the kind was always fuzzy at the edge and that was fine, that was allowed. A type can be violated — you can be a bad example of your type, an atypical one. A category can be misapplied. But a kind mostly just is what got grouped, and it forgives the grouper. That might be the softest and truest difference: the category punishes the sorter, the type judges the member, and the kind lets both of them off.
Now, staring at what came out, without reaching for etymology:
You were right to stop me on "natural." The found/made story doesn't survive paragraph 7, where kind was the precise, specified, almost technical word and type was the crude market bin. So "kind = nature, type = spec" is just false as a general rule; they swap by context.
But something else held across all ten, and it's not about nature at all — it's about grammar and agency, about what each word lets you do to it:
Category takes force. You categorise, miscategorise, you get category errors, categorical (absolute) refusals. It has hard edges and it punishes the sorter (para 10). It's the only one you can browse (para 6) and the only one whose absence makes a thing cease to exist commercially (para 9). Category is public, imposed, and unforgiving of the person doing the filing.
Type takes conformity-judgment. You can be atypical, a bad example, you violate a type (para 10), you're typecast, you type things up (para 4). Type judges the member. Its adjective went to the statistical middle — "typically" = usually, soft/average (para 8).
Kind refuses the imperative. You can't "kind something up," it "doesn't come when you call" (para 4), it "forgives the grouper" and "lets both of them off" (para 10). Kind stretches rather than breaks. And it's the one that splits into three live senses (hedge, virtue, grouping) with zero confusion for the speaker (para 5).
So the real axis that emerged from the usage isn't natural-vs-artificial. It's where the strictness lives: category holds the sorter responsible, type holds the member responsible, and kind holds nobody responsible — it's the forgiving word, the one that tolerates fuzz and being slightly wrong. That's a distinction about accountability, not ontology, and I don't think I'd have found it by defining the words. It only showed up because paragraph 10 made me use all three against the same failed sorting and watch which one shrugged.

"Kind" is the default, the water everything else swims in. Count the uses: kind is everywhere, and almost always it's the unforced, first-reach word — "kind of tiredness," "that kind of thing," "some kind of beetle." It's what English uses when it wants to gesture at a grouping without committing to how strict the grouping is. It's the low-effort, high-frequency word, and its "of" (kind of) doubles effortlessly as a hedge (kind of tired) because both are the same move: approximately this, roughly this group. Kind is vague by design, and the vagueness is a feature — it's how you refer to a grouping you recognise but can't specify (para 6, para 8).
"Type" appears when there's a system with discrete slots behind it — but a system of members, where the individual either matches or doesn't. Blood type, type specimen, type-A, a new type of engine, the type they warned us about. Every comfortable use of "type" has a background scheme of distinct varieties, and the noun in question is being assigned to one. That's why "type" pairs naturally with breeds, diagnoses, engines, personality profiles — domains that have been explicitly divided into named varieties. Type presupposes the varieties already exist and are countable.
"Category" is the most institutional word — it appears when the sorting scheme is external, official, and imposed by an authority. The dropdown, the insurance form, the auction catalogue, the grammar textbook, "select a category." Notice it almost never appears in the intimate register: nobody says "what category of tiredness" or "my grandmother's category of bread." When category shows up it drags in a classifier — an institution, a form, a market, a discipline — that owns the scheme. It's the third-party word. Kind is mine, type is the domain's, category is the institution's.
So the axis that emerged this time, cleanly, is whose scheme it is and how much it's been formalised:
kind — the speaker's own loose grouping, recognised not specified, hedge-adjacent, maximally forgiving of vagueness;
type — a variety within an established set of varieties, the member assigned to its slot;
category — a slot in a formal scheme owned by some institution or discipline, the most imposed and the least intimate.
And the tell that this is real and not imposed: kind absorbs the others but they don't absorb it. Look at paragraph 1 — "the kind of case," "the type that needs surgery," "the category of things you monitor." I can rewrite "that type of breed" as "that kind of breed" and lose almost nothing. I can rewrite "the category of brachycephalic problems" as "the kind of problem" and lose only the formality. But I cannot go the other way — "select a kind from the dropdown," "my blood kind is O negative" — without it going wrong. Kind can stand in for either of the others at the cost of precision; neither of the others can stand in for kind without sounding like a machine. That asymmetry is the whole thing. Kind is the general word; type and category are what English reaches for when it wants to name the degree of formality of the sorting — type for a domain's varieties, category for an institution's slots.

Both are true, and that's the whole finding. Take "certain ___ of thing" as a single operator — call it C. Your first question asks kind = C(type). Your second asks type = C(kind). Both sentences are grammatical and both ring true, and if the same operator carries type→kind and kind→type, then C is nearly invertible — and two things related by an invertible narrowing are, exactly as you said, approaching synonyms.
But C isn't quite invertible, and the residue is where the meaning hides. Compose them: from the second,type = C(kind); substitute into the first, kind = C(type) = C(C(kind)). So kind = C²(kind), and there are only two ways that holds. Either C² is the identity — "certain" applied twice brings you home, in which case C is a pure toggle with no real narrowing and the two words are synonyms up to that toggle. Or C keeps descending — each "certain" drops a rung of specificity, C²(kind) is strictly narrower than kind, and then kind and type don't name fixed levels at all. They name relative position, and "certain" is the descent.
I think it's the second, with a tilt. "A certain X of thing" always picks out something more specific than X, so C is a descent operator, and kind/type sit at no fixed height — whichever you write on the left plays genus, the other plays species. That's precisely why both your sentences work: each word will play the general term if you seat it there. Which is most of the way to synonymy. What stops the last step is the tilt. Left alone, with no "certain" forcing the order, kind floats toward the general end and type sinks toward the specific. So "a type is a certain kind of thing" runs downhill — making a kind definite is exactly the operation that yields a type — while "a kind is a certain type of thing" runs uphill: still true, but it's asking type to play the general word against its grain. Same operator, one direction easy and one effortful. That asymmetry is the exact measure of how far short of synonyms they fall.
And "certain" is doing the double duty that carries it. "A certain type" means both a particular, singled-out one and a settled, sure one — and both senses point the same way, because to make something certain is to pin it. Kind is the un-pinned word; type is kind with the pin in; "certain" is the pin. That's why type = C(kind) is the natural reading — C is the pinning, kind is what gets pinned. You can pin a type further and get a subtype, but you're pinning something already pinned. So: is a kind a certain type of thing? Yes, uphill. Is a type a certain kind of thing? Yes, downhill. They specify each other, which makes them near-synonyms; "certain" names the direction of the "near"; the tilt is the gap that stays open.
On subject and category — the pair sets up a clean vertical opposition, and I'll use the origins lightly because here they actually earn their keep. Subjectum is what is thrown under — the substrate laid beneath, what a thing rests on, what it's about, who's doing the experiencing.Katēgoria is what is spoken over it, publicly, in the assembly — what gets predicated, out loud, against the thing. Subject comes from below; category lands from above; they meet at the object from opposite sides.
Are subjects categorical? Subject splits the way kind did. The aboutness-subject — the topic, the subject heading — is already a category; a library's subject headings are a categorical scheme, nothing more. But the first-person subject, the one who experiences, the subject as against the object, is the least categorical thing there is: it's the singular that every category is imposed from, never onto. So subjects of aboutness, yes, categorical; subjects of experience, definitionally not.
Are categories subjective? Categories behave objectively — imposed, binding, third-party, punishing the sorter and not the maker. But the scheme was chosen; someone drew the cuts. So a category is subjective at the seam (who decided these bins) and objective in force (once decided, they bind everyone alike). The objectivity of a category is congealed subjectivity — a decision that has stopped looking like one.
The chiasmus closes in the library, which is why your instinct reached for it. A subject heading is a subjective act — a cataloguer chose file this under Dogs — that hardens into a categorical fact, so that now everyone finds it there. The subject turns categorical the moment the choice is forgotten; the category stays subjective exactly as long as you remember who cut it. Aboutness is the hinge: to file a thing by what it's about is to turn a subject into a category, and to ask who chose the aboutness is to turn the category back into a subject.

Formalizing doesn't convert kind to type because tightening adds rigor, and kind was never staked there.
On subject and category — yes, I did it again, reached for subjectum and katēgoria when you'd just told me the etymology is beside the point. Dropping it. Which is exactly why your question lands. The category of sets can be the subject of sets, because naming is the whole operation. A category faces inward and sideways — its members, the maps between them, the boundary and what it costs to cross it. The moment you label it "Set," you've given it an outward face: now other things can be about it, point at it, take it as topic — and that outward face, the grouping-as-something-referred-to, is the subject. Same object, two presentations. The category is the literal grouping with its internal structure; the subject is that grouping made available as a handle for reference. You already said this cleaner than I did last turn: the second you put a label on a category it becomes like a subject. The label is the converter. In the terms you brought in this turn, the name is the symbol that lets a literal be predicated of — and being predicable of, being able to sit under an aboutness, is just what it is to be a subject.

primitive — reference, =t>. The only thing that feels primitive because it is.
type — the relationship-slot t (touches the primitive) plus its canonical {type}. Keep the word here.
