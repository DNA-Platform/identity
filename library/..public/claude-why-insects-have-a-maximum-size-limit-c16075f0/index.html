Your intuition is half right, and the interesting part is which half.
The "very obvious cap" is mostly physical, not ecological. The primary limiter is the respiratory system. Insects don't pump oxygen through blood; they pipe air directly to tissues through tracheae that branch into ever finer tracheoles, with the last step being simple diffusion. Diffusion is fine over short distances and terrible over long ones, so as body size grows the tracheal network has to occupy a disproportionately larger fraction of body volume to keep the interior oxygenated. Kaiser and colleagues (2007) imaged beetles with synchrotron X-rays and showed exactly this: tracheal volume scales hypermetrically with body size, and the tracheae squeezing through the leg openings become a bottleneck. Extrapolate that scaling and you hit a wall.
The clean discriminating evidence is deep time. In the Carboniferous and early Permian, atmospheric O2 was around 30 to 35% rather than today's 21%, and that's exactly when you get griffinflies like Meganeura with wingspans near 65 to 70 cm. Rear modern dragonflies or beetles in hyperoxic air and they trend larger over generations. Maximum insect size tracks atmospheric oxygen. A pure predator-prey equilibrium can't explain why the ceiling moves when you change the gas mix.
A secondary physical limit is the exoskeleton under gravity, plus molting. Right after ecdysis the new cuticle is soft, and a very large insect would sag or fail to support itself before it hardened. This is why the biggest arthropods are or were aquatic, where buoyancy offloads the problem: eurypterids around 2.5 m, Japanese spider crabs today. On land, gravity and respiration compound.
Now the part where your instinct is genuinely right. Within that physiological envelope, ecology sets where a lineage actually lands, and predation by agile fliers is a documented tuner. Clapham and Karr (2012) argued that after birds radiated in the Late Jurassic, flying insect size declined even across intervals when oxygen was rising, consistent with maneuverable vertebrate predators selecting against large, less-maneuverable insects. The converse holds too: release from predation produces gigantism. New Zealand's giant wētā reached about 35 g filling a rodent-like ground niche on islands with no mammalian predators. So your agility-and-value equilibrium is real, but it operates below the hard ceiling, not as the ceiling itself.
So I'd reframe the question slightly. There isn't one equilibrium size for a bug. There's a physiological ceiling set mainly by tracheal oxygen delivery and secondarily by exoskeletal support, and then a selective optimum, often driven by predation and flight demands, that sits somewhere under that ceiling and shifts by lineage and habitat. Your model describes the optimum. The cap you noticed is mostly the ceiling.
Primary literature if you want it: Kaiser et al. 2007 (PNAS) on tracheal scaling; Harrison, Kaiser and VandenBrooks for the oxygen-size reviews; Clapham and Karr 2012 (PNAS) on birds and the post-Jurassic size decline.

You're right, and the field has moved to where you're pointing, very recently. Let me concede the core epistemic point first, because it's the important part, and then give you the beetle analysis you asked for.
Your analogy is exactly the right inference structure. "Diffusion caps size" fails the same test as "wingless things can't fly because they lack wings": it names a current architecture and treats it as destiny, when the real question is whether evolution has the raw material and the gradient to build past it. And the discriminating evidence you'd demand, "if it were cheap and advantageous, why hasn't it happened," is now the published argument against the oxygen-limitation story. A March 2026 Nature paper by Snelling, Seymour, and, notably, Harrison himself, the person whose lab built the original tracheal-limitation case, measured tracheole space in flight muscle across 44 species spanning a 10,000-fold mass range. The relative space occupied by tracheoles increases only 1.8-fold across that range and is typically 1% or less of muscle volume, and the same relationship holds when extended to the extinct ~100 g griffinfly Meganeuropsis. Their inference is precisely yours: there is clear evolutionary potential to invest more in tracheoles but insects don't, which is convincing evidence that diffusive oxygen transport through the tracheolar–muscle system does not constrain maximum body size. Their sensitivity analysis makes the point sharper: a threefold increase in tracheole density would substantially raise oxygen supply with only small cost to flight performance. The door was unlocked the whole time and evolution walked past it, which means it was never the lock.
So the honest current answer is that there is probably no single entrenched architectural wall of the kind you're skeptical of. The Meganeura wingspan is the existence proof: an insect body plan can be built at ~100 g, so the ceiling is not a hard engineering limit of the tracheal system. That reframes "why are they capped" into your question: why does the realized optimum sit so low?
Now the beetle analysis, which supports your ecological instinct cleanly. Look at what the actual giants have in common. Goliathus, Titanus, Megasoma, Dynastes: all tropical, and all built almost entirely in the larval stage. Titan beetle larvae feed on rotting wood for up to two years and the adults barely feed at all, living off larval reserves. The heaviest known insect larva is an Actaeon beetle bred to 228 g, about the mass of a brown rat, while the adult is a fraction of that. Goliathus larvae are unusual among their relatives in needing high-protein food, and in captivity they only reach giant size when reared on protein-rich diets. The pattern is that adult size is set by a long larval period sitting on a large, concentrated, stable food resource in a warm climate. Size is a developmental readout of larval nutrition and time, not a fixed species constant. That is why the same lineage is plastic: stag beetle males vary enormously with grub nutrition. So your suspicion that "the same beetle is smaller where conditions are worse" is right at the proximate level, and it's nutritional before it's ever about predators.
On predators specifically, the cleanest natural experiment isn't a beetle, it's the New Zealand giant wētā, and it goes your way. Giant wētā are a textbook case of island gigantism: flightless, they grew large filling a rodent-like ground niche on islands that lacked mammalian predators, and defended themselves by size alone. The mechanism is exactly the equilibrium you proposed, run in reverse: release from predation lowers extrinsic mortality and selects for larger body size and a slower life history. And there's a direct manipulation: on Little Barrier Island, wētāpunga collapsed under introduced cats and rats and rebounded four-fold within six years after rat removal. That's population rather than body size, but it's the same causal arrow, and it's why the giants are relicts of predator-free refugia. The converse, the macroevolutionary version, is Clapham and Karr's finding that after birds radiated in the Late Jurassic, insect size decoupled from atmospheric oxygen, suggesting predation and competition superseded oxygen as the main control on maximum insect size.
Where I'll push back a little, since you asked for something more entrenched than the gill: I think the best surviving candidate for a genuine architectural cost, not a wall, is the molt-plus-gravity interaction, and it's the mirror image of your water-to-land example. To grow, an arthropod must shed and re-expand its entire exoskeleton, and for a window it is soft and mechanically unsupported. In water, buoyancy carries the body through that window, which is why every truly giant arthropod is or was aquatic: eurypterids at ~2.5 m, Japanese spider crabs today. On land, gravity has to be resisted by a freshly soft cuticle, and support scales badly because strength goes with cross-section while load goes with volume. Leaving the water didn't add a constraint so much as remove the buoyant subsidy that made giant arthropods possible in the first place. The largest terrestrial arthropod, the coconut crab at ~4 kg, fits: heavily reinforced, burrow-hiding, and vulnerable for weeks at each molt. But I want to be precise that this is a cost that raises the price of being large on land and shifts the optimum down, not a diffusion-style barrier. It bends the curve, it doesn't cap it.
So the synthesis I'd actually defend: there is no fixed respiratory ceiling. Insects can be built giant and were. What sets realized maximum size is a developmental budget (larval resource and time), a selective optimum tuned heavily by predation and flight demands, and a molt-and-support tax that penalizes terrestrial giants specifically. Your equilibrium model isn't operating below a wall, as I said last time. On the current evidence it may be much closer to the whole story.

You're right, and I want to take the method seriously rather than nod at it, because your reframing of "constraint" is the thing that actually does the work.
Start with your definition, because it's the correct one: a constraint is the probability that some mutation or short sequence of them incrementally moves the trait, per unit selective benefit. That converts "constraint" from a wall into a term in the response to selection. The response of mean size to selection is roughly the standing genetic variance times the selection gradient, G times β. A "limitation" is only ever a claim about one of those two terms. If it's a claim about G, it says the variation to make a bigger bug is not readily produced. If it's a claim about β, it says bigger bugs are made but selected away. Anything I say that isn't cashed out into one of those two is, as you're insisting, not a force account. I was sloppy on exactly this point, calling limitations explanations.
Now use your falsification test as positive evidence, because it settles which term matters. Insects already span about three orders of magnitude in mass. A single species varies severalfold with larval nutrition. Size responds to artificial selection in a handful of generations, and it moves fast in the wild: island lineages shift size on ecological timescales. Titanus is roughly fifteen times the length of your 1 cm bug and the griffinfly was far past that. So the incremental mutational path to "larger" is demonstrably accessible, it is being walked constantly, in every direction, across the clade. That means G for size is large and the supply of variation is not the binding term. By your own criterion the "hard architectural constraint on size" is falsified by the extant variance, cleanly. Whatever holds the modal insect small has to live in β, the net selection gradient, not in the production of variants.
So the real question, and it's Blanckenhorn's framing of the general problem, is not why insects can get big, they can, but what keeps most of them small: what selects against size strongly enough, and specifically for insects, to sit the optimum low. Large size is generally favored: fecundity selection (more eggs in a bigger female), sexual selection (mating and combat success, hence the horned rhinoceros beetles), competitive ability, resistance to starvation. Those are the up-forces, and where they dominate you get the giants. The insect-specific down-forces are what you have to weigh against them:
First, development time under a fast life history. An insect grows only as a juvenile, often on an ephemeral resource, and larger size buys a longer larval period. In a clade whose whole strategy is high fecundity and rapid turnover, delayed reproduction is discounted steeply through the intrinsic rate of increase, and it exposes the juvenile to more cumulative mortality before it ever breeds, and it can cost a whole generation per year by pushing a multivoltine insect to univoltine or semivoltine. That voltinism cliff is a large, discrete fitness drop, and it's an insect-specific gradient that vertebrates, already committed to slow and iteroparous, simply don't pay in the same coin.
Second, flight. This is, I think, the deepest antagonist, because most insect lineages are flying lineages and the flight optimum is what the modal insect sits under. Mass-specific power to fly rises with size and maneuverability falls, and agile vertebrate fliers turn that into a predation gradient, which is exactly Clapham and Karr's post-Jurassic size decline. And it predicts the exceptions: the animals that escape small size are the ones that give up flight or hide. Giant wētā are flightless. The giant beetles are heavy, nocturnal, cryptic. That's the down-force relaxing, not an architecture changing.
Third, resource reliability, which is a selective filter and not a wall. Building a giant requires a large, concentrated, dependable larval substrate: tropical rotting hardwood, a dung ball, a seabird-subsidized island. Those patches are rare and ephemeral, so the probability of a larva monopolizing enough to reach giant size is low, and most attempts at large fail before reproducing. That's variance in the environment feeding straight into viability selection, and it's why the realized giants sit on unusually rich stable resources in warm climates where the developmental-time penalty is also softened.
Now, where does a legitimate constraint survive, in your sense, as a low path-probability rather than a wall? The molt-and-support problem, recast: a size-increasing mutation on land has to also not kill the animal during the soft post-molt window, so the fraction of size-increasing mutations that are viable is lower on land than in water, where buoyancy makes almost any size-increasing variant survivable through the molt. That is not "you can't build it." It's a reduced accessibility of the incremental path, conditioned on environment and pleiotropy, and it feeds viability selection. And it predicts the asymmetry you'd otherwise have to wave off: the truly giant arthropods are aquatic, eurypterids and spider crabs, and the terrestrial max, the coconut crab, is armored, burrow-hiding, and vulnerable for weeks per molt. That's a force account of the water and land difference, not a machine tolerance.
On the fish-to-human demand, I'll concede it directly and then show why it doesn't bind here. No selectionist theory predicts specific key innovations forward. Evolutionary theory is strongly predictive of direction given standing variation and gradients, and weakly predictive of novel architecture. You've set a bar, predict fish to human, that no force account clears, and you're right that a theory pretending to clear it would be fake. But the size question doesn't require clearing it, and that's the whole point: large size is not a needed innovation, it's standing variation sitting right there in Titanus and Meganeura. So this problem lives entirely inside the regime where the theory is predictive, G times β with abundant G. I never needed a "can't," and I shouldn't have reached for one. The griffinfly is the proof that the architecture can go there, and its disappearance precisely when oxygen fell and agile fliers radiated is the proof that what holds size down is a maintained selective balance, not a limit.
The test that separates my account from the one you're rejecting is exactly your 12x objection turned into a requirement: a real theory has to generate the distribution. A single ceiling predicts a spike and can't survive a 15 cm beetle. A gradient-balance account predicts which insects are large (flightless or cryptic, on rich stable larval substrate, in warm lowland climates that cheapen development time) and which are small (flying, exposed, on ephemeral resources), and it predicts the water-land giant asymmetry, and it predicts size tracking oxygen only until birds arrive and then decoupling. That's the deliverable. The size of any given bug is where its lineage's fecundity-and-sexual up-gradient crosses its development-time, flight, and resource-reliability down-gradient, and the clade is small because for most insects, most of the time, that crossing sits low.
Primary literature for the framework, not just the phenomenology: Blanckenhorn 2000, Quarterly Review of Biology 75:385, "The Evolution of Body Size: What Keeps Organisms Small?", which is your exact question posed as a balance of forces; Lande and Arnold 1983, Evolution 37:1210, for the selection-gradient machinery; Maynard Smith et al. 1985, QRB 60:265, for constraints defined as biases in the production of variation, which is your path-probability idea in its original form; and Kingsolver and Pfennig 2004, Evolution 58:1608, on the general tendency of selection to favor large size, which sharpens the puzzle of what opposes it.

The signatures you asked me to marshal are everywhere once you look for size being solved sideways rather than upward. Periodical cicadas are easy prey for birds, reptiles, and mammals, so their defense is not individual but populational: they emerge in overwhelming synchronized numbers that satiate every predator around, with enough left over to breed. The one nuance: predator satiation can't by itself explain why periodicity is so rare, and the long cycle itself was probably driven by glacial cooling, with predation locking in the prime tuning afterward. Here is where I'd push on your friction model, and it's a push that strengthens your headline rather than weakening it. What the molt does is bend the gradient. Each size increment lengthens and worsens the teneral window, the soft unsupported interval after ecdysis, and raises the support cost under gravity, both rising with size. Stack that on the predation gradient and the development-time-under-mortality gradient from before, and the summed selective gradient on size is negative across most of insect ecological space. It flips positive only in the narrow niches where all three relax at once: predation released, resource rich and stable, season long enough to impose no deadline. That is why the giants sit where they sit and look how they look. And the tell that it's selection and not a developmental wall is the water: buoyancy cancels the teneral support cost, the gradient stops bending down, and giant arthropods appear freely, eurypterids, lobsters, spider crabs. Same molt, opposite sign on the gradient, because the environment changed the cost, not the mutation supply.
The one place your innovation intuition keeps real teeth is the coupling. You probably cannot mutate "large" and "no soft window" independently: the vulnerability is developmentally welded to the molt, so "big but not vulnerable while molting" is genuinely hard to assemble on land. That is why the terrestrial giants that exist paid for size with hiding, armor, and long rare life cycles rather than by evolving a slicker molt. So the honest split is mostly selection gating readily available variation, with a genuine developmental-coupling component that makes the specific escape you'd actually want, size without the soft window, hard to reach on land, which is why the bill gets paid in crypsis instead.
So your account is the right shape and I'd only re-weight it. Predation is not a co-factor sitting next to a mutational barrier. It is one of the main selective forces doing the gating, and "evolve in a different direction" wins not because up is blocked but because sideways has a positive local gradient while up has a negative one. The cockroach in the dark and the cicada's prime number are not consolation prizes for insects that couldn't get big. They are the cheaper adaptive peak, and the gradient points straight at them.
