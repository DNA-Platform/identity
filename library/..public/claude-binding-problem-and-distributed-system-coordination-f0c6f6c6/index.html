How does the binding problem in consciousness studies relate to coordination in distributed systems? The binding problem asks how separate neural processes produce unified experience. Distributed systems face an analogous challenge: how do independent nodes with partial state produce coherent behavior? Are there formal frameworks that treat both as instances of the same coordination problem?

Worth separating two things the phrase "binding problem" runs together, because the distributed-systems analogy bites cleanly on one and not the other.
The first is what Revonsuo and Treisman call the segregation/combination problem: features computed in different areas (color in V4, motion in MT, orientation, location) have to be assigned to the right objects, so that "red" and "moving left" attach to the same thing rather than swapping. This is a structural/functional question about producing a consistent global representation from spatially separated partial computations. The second is phenomenal unity — why there is a single field of experience at all rather than a heap of disjoint micro-experiences. The first is the one your distributed-systems framing actually maps onto. Keeping them apart is what lets you say something precise instead of merely suggestive.
At the right level of abstraction the analogy is exact, not loose. Both are local-to-global coherence problems: agents (neural populations / nodes) hold partial state, can only communicate over limited channels with delays, and a coherent global state has to be produced or fail to be produced. The classical distributed-computing results all have recognizable binding analogues. Consensus is "do the parts agree on one interpretation." FLP impossibility (no deterministic asynchronous consensus survives a single fault) is the formal statement that under realistic timing assumptions there is no guaranteed convergence — which is exactly the regime where binding fails and you get illusory conjunctions, rivalry, or the temporal smearing Treisman studied. CAP is the tradeoff between a single consistent percept and responsiveness under partition. Lamport's happens-before partial order is the same object as the temporal-binding hypothesis's reliance on relative timing (the gamma-synchrony proposals are, structurally, clock-synchronization schemes). None of this is metaphor; it's the same class of constraint.
The substantive answer to your last question — is there a formal framework treating both as instances of one coordination problem — is yes, and the framework is sheaf theory. A sheaf assigns local data to the pieces of a cover and specifies when locally-compatible pieces glue into a unique global section; sheaf cohomology measures the obstruction when they don't. That gluing axiom is the literal formalization of "do partial states cohere into one whole," and the obstruction class is the formal content of "coordination failed." What makes this more than a coincidence of vocabulary is that the same machinery has been instantiated in all the relevant domains independently. Abramsky and Brandenburger's 2011 treatment characterizes quantum non-locality and contextuality as obstructions to the existence of global sections — that's the rigorous anchor, because it shows the framework does real work (it produces a strict hierarchy and recovers the Kochen–Specker theorem). On the distributed-computing side there's now an explicit sheaf-theoretic characterization of task solvability: non-trivial sections correspond to valid solutions of a task, obstructions to global sections represent system limitations that make tasks unsolvable, and the cohomology of the task sheaf can be used to compute solving protocols. This sits on top of an older tradition — Herlihy and Shavit's asynchronous computability theorem (Gödel Prize 2004) already characterized wait-free task solvability through combinatorial topology, i.e. whether a certain map of simplicial complexes exists, a local-to-global obstruction. And on sensor fusion specifically, Robinson's claim is that sheaves are the canonical structure for integrating heterogeneous data sources, with the basic fusion question being whether a set of observations forms a global section. The binding-side literature then borrows exactly this: in the sheaf view, local feature assignments — edges, colors, glimpses — must be consistently glued together to yield a global percept, which is presented as the essence of the binding problem, and there are recent papers framing phenomenal unity itself as the existence of a global section over neural state spaces with pathologies read as cohomological obstructions.
So the honest strong claim is: the combination binding problem and distributed coordination are the same formal problem, and sheaf cohomology is the shared language in which "coherence" and "the obstruction to coherence" are both first-class, quantifiable objects. That is a real unification, not an analogy.
Now the part I think matters most, because it's where the unification stops and where someone careful would push. There is a structural tension, not just an open question, between the coordination framing and the phenomenal one, and it surfaces sharpest against IIT. Integrated Information Theory says unified experience requires that the system's cause-effect structure be irreducible to its parts — Φ is essentially the information lost across the minimum-information partition. But distributed systems are engineered toward the opposite property: partition tolerance, modular independence, graceful degradation when nodes split off. A well-designed distributed system is precisely one where partitioning is cheap — which by IIT's measure makes it low-Φ. So "good coordination" and "phenomenal binding," far from being the same thing, may be formally opposed: the better your nodes function independently, the less integrated the whole, and the engineering virtue is the phenomenal vice. (One can take this as a reason to doubt IIT — Aaronson's expander-graph objection and Doerig's unfolding argument both press on whether Φ tracks anything experiential — but either way the tension is informative.)
The sheaf framework is honest about which problem it solves. It captures the segregation/combination problem and the conditions for and obstructions to a consistent global representation. It is silent on phenomenal binding — nothing in the gluing axiom says a glued section is experienced rather than merely consistent, and the consciousness-applied papers are nearly all explicitly conceptual proposals rather than empirically discriminating theories. So I'd resist the cleanest version of the claim you might be tempted toward. What's genuinely established is a common formalism for the coordination problem, with quantum contextuality and distributed-task impossibility as the load-bearing instances and binding-as-gluing as a well-motivated import. What remains a research program — and one with at least one internal reason to suspect the two senses of "binding" diverge — is whether that same formalism reaches the unity of experience, or only its functional shadow.
