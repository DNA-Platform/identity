Introduction: The Fundamental Semantic Form
The subject-object relationship constitutes the fundamental semantic form—the basic pattern through which meaning emerges. Every meaningful expression, from "The dog runs" to "E = mc²", instantiates some variant of this pattern: something (subject) relates to something else (object) through some form of connection (relationship). Any formalism that claims to capture semantics must represent this fundamental form fundamentally—not as a derived concept but as foundational structure.
This principle motivates our systematic analysis: by exhaustively examining how subject, object, and relationship can structurally relate, we discover what semantic primitives must exist. The method inverts traditional theory-building—rather than stipulating axioms and deriving consequences, we analyze structural necessities and discover what axioms they require.
I. The Primitive and Its Semantic Context
Semantic Reference Theory takes as its domain the set of "referents"—things that can be referred to. This includes not just physical objects but abstract concepts, relationships, properties, and crucially, the very structures of reference itself. Within this domain, we posit a single primitive relation R(x,y,t), interpreted semantically as "x refers to y through t" and expressed notationally as:
srt
x =t> y
This relation serves as the semantic equivalent of what the Turing machine provides for computation—a minimal structure from which all semantic relationships emerge. Just as all computable functions can be expressed through state transitions on a tape, all semantic relationships can be constructed from this ternary reference relation.
Grounding in First-Order Logic
Since we're building within first-order logic, we inherit its equality relation. But rather than leave equality as an external logical operator, we internalize it as a form of reference:
srt
E[eq]: A[x,y]: x = y <iff> x =eq> y
Here, eq is a referent in our domain—a thing that can itself be referred to. It represents the equality relationship from FOL, making it subject to the same semantic analysis as any other referent. This allows us to write:

The referent eq thus serves as the bridge between logical identity and semantic reference, grounding our theory in the bedrock of logic while making equality itself an object of semantic investigation.

"Water boils" - water (subject) undergoes boiling (object as state)
"Seven is prime" - seven (subject) has primeness (object as property)
"Democracy requires participation" - democracy (subject) needs participation (object)
Even in formal systems, this pattern persists. In set theory, x ∈ y expresses that x (subject) belongs to y (object) through membership (relationship). In logic, P(x) says x (subject) has property P (object) through instantiation (relationship).
This universality suggests the subject-object relationship isn't merely one semantic pattern among others—it's the fundamental semantic form from which meaning emerges. A formalism of semantics that failed to represent this form fundamentally would be like a theory of computation that treated state transitions as derivative rather than primitive.
III. The Canonical Subject-Object Relationship
In natural language, when we express relationships, we typically assume the subject, relationship, and object are distinct unless explicitly told otherwise. Consider the sentence "The mother loves her child." The canonical interpretation parses this as three distinct entities:
The subject: mother
The relationship: loves
The object: child
This distinctness is so fundamental to how we process semantic information that it requires explicit markers to override. When someone says "I spoke to the CEO and an employee of the company," the natural interpretation is two distinct people, not one person serving dual roles. We don't spontaneously consider whether the CEO might also be an employee—the default semantic processing assumes distinctness.
This canonical interpretation motivates why the first structural case, where all three variables are distinct, deserves special attention as the archetypal pattern of semantic relationships.
IV. The Five Structural Cases
The complete space of how three variables can relate through equality partitions into exactly five cases. This isn't a selection of interesting patterns but the exhaustive enumeration of structural possibilities, constrained by the transitivity of equality.
The Cyclical Notation and Its Semantic Significance
The notation s != r != o != s embodies the triangular nature of ternary relationships. By cycling back to check inequality with the starting variable, we ensure all three pairwise relationships are specified. In full SRT notation, this becomes s !=eq> r !=eq> o !=eq> s, making explicit that even inequality is a form of reference—specifically, the negation of equality reference.
Case 1: The Property Trinity (All Distinct)
text
s != r != o != s
This case represents the canonical semantic pattern where subject, relationship, and object are all distinct. To capture this foundational structure, we introduce three referents that represent the properties of semantic roles:
srt
sub =rel> obj
Here:
sub represents the property of subjecthood—what referents possess when they serve as subjects
rel represents the property of being a relationship
obj represents the property of objecthood—what referents possess when they serve as objects
These aren't arbitrary symbols but semantic necessities. For there to be reference at all, there must be something that refers (subjects), something referred to (objects), and the act of referring (relationships). The relationship sub =rel> obj establishes that the most specific way subjects relate to objects is through relationships: (sub,obj) == rel.
This property trinity grounds all other semantic relationships. When we later find that sym =rel> rel (symbols inherit from relationships), we're saying symbols participate in this fundamental pattern established by the sub-rel-obj trinity.
Case 2: Self-Representing Relationships
text
s == r != o != s
This case reveals a profound semantic possibility: the subject can be the relationship through which it relates to its object. This isn't a formal curiosity but a semantic necessity for understanding symbolization. We capture it with:
srt
sym =sym> lit
The referent sym (symbol) simultaneously occupies the subject position (the thing doing the symbolizing) and the relationship position (the act of symbolization itself). The referent lit (literal) represents the concrete, the actual, the thing symbolized.
This dual role of sym reflects a deep semantic truth: symbols don't just participate in symbolization; they are symbolization made concrete. When we write the word "tree," that word both performs the act of symbolizing and exists as the subject performing that act.
From this structure we can derive:
srt
sym =sub> sym     // symbols possess subjecthood
lit =obj> lit     // literals possess objecthood
Since sym is used as a relationship type (the middle position in sym =sym> lit), and we establish that all relationship types inherit from rel:
srt
sym =rel> rel     // symbols are a type of relationship
Therefore:
text
(sym,lit) == sym  // symbolization is the most specific relationship
This establishes that symbols truly refer to literals—the relationship is referential because sym inherits from representation and serves as the most specific connection between symbol and literal.
Case 3: Canonical Representation
text
s != r != o == s
When subject and object are the same but the relationship remains external, we need to capture canonical representation:
srt
can =rep> can
Here can (canonical) represents the privileged representative—the distinguished element that serves as the primary entry point for understanding. A canonical represents itself through representation, establishing the self-referential pattern essential to semantic structure.
The canonical serves a special role across semantic systems:
The canonical textbook for a subject
The canonical form of an equation
The canonical symbol in a reference frame
This pattern shows how certain referents become privileged pathways for understanding, selected from among many possible representations.
Case 4: Relationships as Objects
text
s != r == o != s
This case reveals that relationships themselves can be objects of reference:
srt
sym =rep> rep
Here rep (representation) serves dual roles—it's both the relationship type and the object. Symbols relate to representations by being part of them, and this "being part of" relationship is representation itself.
This captures a crucial semantic pattern: membership or containment. When we say a dog is an animal, "animal" serves as both the category (relationship type) and the thing the dog is classified as (object). But we can't define animal in terms of dog—we'd need to subtract all dog-specific features. The representation contains its members; the members' relationship to it is simply being represented.
From this:
rep =rel> rel - representations are types of relationships
rep =obj> obj - representations can be objects
(sym,rep) == rep - representation is the most specific relationship
Case 5: Pure Coincidence
text
s == r == o == s
Complete coincidence where an entity serves as subject, relationship, and object simultaneously:
srt
eq =eq> eq
This expresses that equality relates to equality through equality. This isn't reference (which requires representation) but constitutive relationship. For equality to be universal—for everything to equal everything that it equals—equality must equal equality through equality. The pattern is foundational, not circular.
We also use:
srt
ref =eq> ref
Here ref represents the domain of all referents. The relationship ref =eq> ref expresses that referents equal themselves, establishing the foundational self-equality that grounds the theory.
Similarly:
srt
rel =rel> rel
Relationships relate to relationships through relationship. These are grounding patterns that require no external support—semantic bedrock from which other meanings build.
IV. The Semantic Interpretation of Properties
Properties as Relational Roles
The referents sub, obj, and rel represent properties that referents acquire through their roles in relationships. Just as "tall" is a property trees can have without "tall" itself being tall, subjecthood is a property referents gain by occupying the subject position.
srt
x =p> x suggests "x has property p"
But the relationship between role-properties and self-possession presents a fascinating semantic question.
Two Interpretations of Role-Based Properties
Role-Based Interpretation: If we define sub as "the property of being in a subjective role," then in the relationship sub =rel> obj:
sub occupies the subjective role, suggesting sub =sub> sub
rel occupies the relational role, suggesting rel =rel> rel
obj occupies the objective role, suggesting obj =obj> obj
Under this interpretation, these properties would possess themselves by virtue of occupying their defining roles.
Inheritance-Based Interpretation: Alternatively, if properties work through inheritance patterns:
srt
x in subjective role =|> (x,x) -> sub
Here, sub -> sub would be satisfied simply by sub == sub (identity), without requiring sub =sub> sub.
This parallels how property inheritance works elsewhere:
srt
x =bright-red> x & bright-red -> red =|> x =red> x
An Open Semantic Question
Whether sub =sub> sub, obj =obj> obj, and rel =rel> rel hold remains an intriguing question. From Case 5, we established:
srt
eq =eq> eq   // equality equals itself through equality
rel =rel> rel  // relationships relate through relationship
These are constitutive relationships. Whether sub and obj follow similar patterns depends on how we interpret their semantic roles. Both interpretations have merit:
If these are role-defining properties, self-possession may follow necessarily
If these work through inheritance, self-possession might emerge as a theorem rather than an axiom
This ambiguity reflects a deep question about whether semantic categories possess the very properties they define—a question that may resolve differently depending on the broader semantic framework we construct.
V. Reference, Representation, and Semantic Necessity
The Definition of Reference
Not all relationships are referential. A relationship is referential when it connects a representation to what it represents:
srt
If rep =t> obj where t -> rep, then t is referential
When t == (rep,obj), then rep refers to obj
This distinction is crucial semantically. Consider:
sym =sym> lit is referential because symbols are representations that connect to their literals
x =eq> x is not referential because equality doesn't involve representation
The word "tree" refers to an actual tree through symbolic representation. But a tree being equal to itself is just identity, not reference. Reference requires:
A representation (the word "tree")
What is represented (the actual tree)
A representational relationship connecting them (symbolization)
Without all three components, there is no reference. This is why Gödel's construction was revolutionary—he created a coding system that gave formal statements canonical symbols, allowing them to refer to themselves through representation rather than mere identity.
Formal Derivation: Symbols as Reference
Given our model, we can derive that symbols genuinely refer:
srt
1. sym =sym> lit                    // Given from Case 2
2. sym =rel> rel                    // Derived: sym is a relationship type
3. sym -> rep                       // Symbols inherit from representation
4. (sym,lit) == sym                 // sym is most specific
5. Therefore: sym refers to lit    // By definition of reference
This shows formally that the symbol-literal relationship constitutes genuine reference.
Inheritance as Semantic Structure
For relationship types, inheritance captures how specific relationships relate to general ones:
srt
s -> t := s =rel> t || s == t
If crimson =rel> red and red =rel> color, then crimson inherits from color through this chain. But rather than define this recursively, we use symbol possession:
srt
s =rel> t =|> s =(t)> s
Types possess the canonical symbols of what they inherit from. Inheritance becomes a matter of which symbolic properties a type carries.
VI. The Nine-Referent Model as Semantic Necessity
The nine referents that emerge aren't chosen but discovered as semantic necessities:
eq - Represents the equality relation inherited from FOL, internalized as a referent
ref - Represents the domain itself (all referents), making the theory self-contained
sub - The property of subjecthood, what it means to be a subject
rel - The property of relationship, pure relating itself
obj - The property of objecthood, what it means to be an object
sym - Symbol, the dual subject/relationship that enables representation
rep - Representation, the dual relationship/object that enables collection
lit - Literal, the concrete, the actual, the non-symbolic
can - Canonical, the privileged representative selected from many
Why These Nine?
Each emerged from semantic necessity:
Case 1 forced sub, rel, and obj into existence to capture the property trinity
Case 2 required sym and lit to express symbolization
Case 3 introduced can for canonical representation
Case 4 reused sym and introduced rep for collection/membership
Case 5 used ref and eq for self-equality, and verified rel =rel> rel
The economy is remarkable—nine referents suffice to exemplify all five structural patterns, suggesting we've found something like a semantic basis set.
Core Semantic Relationships
The fundamental relationships establish the semantic architecture:
srt
sub =rel> obj          // The property pattern for subject-object relations
sym =sym> lit          // Symbols symbolize literals through symbolization
can =rep> can          // Canonicals represent themselves
ref =eq> ref           // The domain's self-equality
sym =rep> rep          // Symbols are members of representations
rel =rel> rel          // Relationships relate through relationship
eq =eq> eq             // Equality equals itself through equality
Canonical Symbols and Frame-Relative Reference
The notion of canonicality plays a crucial role in semantic structure. A canonical is the privileged representative selected from among many possible representations—the one that serves as the primary entry point for understanding.
Formally, we can express canonicality through the notation (x), which denotes the canonical symbol for referent x. Crucially, canonicality is frame-relative:
text
(x) in frame r means: the canonical symbol for x in reference frame r
The canonical serves a special role—it's the most specific way to relate to what it represents within its frame. When we later develop reference frames and the semantics of collections, we'll see how:
(x) =r> r expresses that the canonical of x is a relationship in frame r
Different frames can have different canonicals for the same referent
The notation (x) implicitly depends on the current reference frame
This frame-relativity of canonicality will become essential when we explore how perspectives qualify experience through their own canonical symbols.
VII. Sparse Relationships and Semantic Selectivity
Not every pair among the nine referents has a specified relationship beyond equality. For instance:
We don't specify how literals relate to subjects
We don't define relationships between objects and symbols
Many pairs simply lack direct semantic connections
This sparsity isn't a deficiency but reflects semantic reality. Not everything meaningfully relates to everything else. The model includes only relationships that are semantically necessary—those forced into existence by the structural cases or their logical consequences.
When we don't specify lit =?> sub, we're acknowledging that literals and subjecthood don't have a direct semantic relationship that needs formal capture. Literals are concrete; subjecthood is abstract. Their relationship, if any, would be mediated through other referents.
IX. The Bootstrap Principle and Semantic Emergence
The axioms of this theory are structured to be vacuously true in an empty catalogue—true by default when nothing exists. But they spring to life the moment a single relationship is added:
srt
E[x,y,t]: x =t> y =|> E[eq]: A[a,b]: a = b <iff> a =eq> y
This conditional structure reflects a deep semantic principle: meaning doesn't exist in a void. There must be at least one relationship for the concepts of subject, object, and relationship to have meaning.
From that single relationship, the entire semantic structure bootstraps into existence:
srt
1. x =t> y                     // One relationship exists
2. =|> x != y || x == y        // Distinctness or identity must hold
3. =|> E[eq]: ...              // Equality emerges to capture identity
4. If x != y:
   =|> x =sub> x              // x gains subjecthood
   =|> y =obj> y              // y gains objecthood
   =|> t =rel> rel            // t becomes a relationship type
5. =|> (x,y) exists           // Most specific relationship emerges
This cascade from a single relationship to the full semantic architecture isn't just formal elegance but semantic necessity—meaning itself emerges from the first distinction, the first relationship that breaks the void.
X. Conclusion: Discovering Semantic Structure
This systematic exploration reveals that the subject-object relationship has a determinate foundational structure. The five cases aren't arbitrary but mathematically exhaustive. The nine referents aren't invented but discovered as semantic necessities. Each structural pattern forces certain referents into existence, and these referents relate in specific ways determined by semantic necessity rather than formal stipulation.
The model demonstrates that meaning can be built from within, using the structures of reference to construct the concepts needed to understand reference itself. The theory achieves self-containment through:
A referent (ref) representing the domain of all referents
A canonical (can) providing privileged representation
Internalization of logical equality as a semantic relationship (eq)
Using relationships to explain relationships without circularity
Crucially, the theory distinguishes reference (which requires representation) from mere relationships. The word "tree" refers to a tree through symbolic representation; a tree equal to itself is just identity. This distinction grounds the difference between semantic connections and logical relations.
The reflexive construction reveals the foundational architecture of meaning—not as something imposed from outside but as something that emerges from the structural possibilities of the subject-object relationship. That exactly five cases exist, that exactly nine referents emerge, that these form a complete model for basic semantic structure—all this suggests that meaning itself has a determinate foundation discoverable through systematic analysis of the fundamental semantic form.

Key Questions and Attempted Answers
Q: Why does this circular definition structure work?
A: It mirrors how dictionaries actually function. The definition of "definition" uses "the", which appears after "definition" alphabetically. Dictionaries aren't linear - they're networks of mutual definition. Similarly, SRT referents define each other in a web of relationships.
This circularity isn't a bug but the fundamental feature that makes meaning possible. Each referent gains meaning through its relationships to others, and those relationships are themselves referents.
Q: How does def[x] relate to inheritance?
A: def[x] creates a definition that things inheriting from x must satisfy. It's saying "anything that claims to be a kind of x must have these properties." The bracketed notation suggests we're defining the dereferenced/literal aspect that gets inherited.
Meanwhile def(x) states facts about x itself that don't necessarily propagate through inheritance.
Q: What does it mean for relationships to be "fundamentally symbolic"?
A: The equation (t,t) == t for relationship types means they collapse their self-reference. Unlike concrete objects that might have (x,x) != x, relationships ARE their own self-relationships. This makes them inherently symbolic - they exist as symbols of themselves.
This explains why r == (rel) - the relationship type r is the canonical symbol for the concept of relationship.
Q: How does this provide a model for semantic theory extension?
A: New semantic theories can:
Introduce new referents with def[x]
Establish what inherits from them
Create new relationship types (automatically symbolic)
Build on existing definitions through inheritance chains
The framework is extensible because:
Definitions can reference not-yet-defined terms
Inheritance allows specialization without breaking existing patterns
The symbolic nature of relationships means new relationship types automatically gain proper semantic structure

Any formalism claiming to capture semantics must represent this fundamental form fundamentally, not as a derived concept but as foundational structure[1].
Semantic Reference Theory (SRT) grounds this pattern in a single primitive relation R(x,y,t), interpreted as "x refers to y through relationship type t"[2]. While the transpilation mechanisms for this primitive have been formalized[2], defining the fundamental referents of subject, object, and relationship within the theory requires novel machinery.
This paper introduces definitional syntax that extends SRT's transpiler, enabling these fundamental referents to be defined through mutual constraint within frames. We show how relationship emerges as the core concept from which subject and object derive their meaning.
2. The Fundamental Semantic Form
2.1 Why Subject-Object-Relationship
Consider any meaningful statement[1]:
"Water boils" - water (subject) undergoes boiling (object as state)
"Seven is prime" - seven (subject) has primeness (object as property)
"Democracy requires participation" - democracy (subject) needs participation (object)
This universality suggests the subject-object-relationship pattern is not merely one semantic pattern among others but the fundamental semantic form from which meaning emerges.
2.2 The Bootstrap Assumption
We begin with the existence of at least one relationship:
text
E(s,o,r): s != r != o != s
The redundant notation emphasizes that all three components, subject, relationship, and object, are distinct in the canonical case. From this single relationship, the entire semantic structure emerges.

3.1 Novel Definition Forms
We extend SRT's transpiler[2] with two complementary forms:
text
def(x): φ

def[x]: ψ

The relationship between these forms:
srt
E[x]: def(x) & A[y]: y -> x =|> def[x] with x replaced by y
This extends Phase 5 of the existing transpiler[2], adding definitional capabilities not previously available.

text

This denotes x's specific version of property p. When combined with symbolic collapse (x,x) == x for symbolic referents[6], this enables stable inheritance patterns without infinite regress.

4.1 Frame Structure
Following [4], a frame F[x] establishes x as the subject:
text
F[x]
Within F[x], all relationship types inherit from x. Propositionally, F[x] transpiles to x itself; the frame is its subject.

Every SRT development begins with F[r], where r -> r is axiomatic. Within this frame:
text
r == (rel)
The relationship type r serves as the canonical symbol for relationship itself.
5. The Character of Relationships
5.1 Self-Relationship vs External Relationship
When a referent relates to itself:
srt
x =p> x
This expresses character or characteristics. When there is distinction:
srt
x =t> y where x != y
This expresses true relationship with subjective involvement, where:
text
(x,x) -> (x,y)
The subject's self-relationship inherits from its external relationships, incorporating them into its character.
5.2 Relationships as Symbolic Types
From [6], inheritance is preserved through symbolization:
srt
(x,y) -> t =|> ((x,y)) -> (t)
For relationships specifically, we establish:
srt
x =t> y =|> (t,t) == t
Relationship types are their own self-relationships. This self-collapse distinguishes them ontologically from other referents. Since all relationships inherit from r, and r == (rel), relationships represent relationshiptypes rather than instances.
6. Defining Relationship Through the Fundamental Form
6.1 Definition of rel
We define relationship through its fundamental pattern:
srt
def(rel):
   sub =rel> obj

This establishes that rel is the most specific relationship between sub and obj.
srt
def[rel]:
   sub(rel) =rel> obj(rel)
   (sub(rel), obj(rel)) -> rel

def[sub]:

   rel(sub) =rel(sub)> sub
   (sub, sub) -> rel(sub)
The third constraint is crucial: subjecthood's character inherently includes relationship.

text
(sub, sub) -> (sub, obj)

text
sub == rel

text
sub == (rel) == r
7.2 Semantic Interpretation
This identity reveals that:
The subject referent is the canonical symbol for relationship
To be a subject is to symbolize relationships
Subjectivity equals symbolic relationship
The subject does not "have" relationships or "participate in" relationships; the subject is the symbolization of relationship.

The definitions propagate through inheritance. For any t -> rel:
srt
sub(t) =t> obj(t)
(sub(t), obj(t)) -> t

sub(love) represents the lover
obj(love) represents the beloved
The pattern persists: sub(love) =love> obj(love)

Theorem: Frame-Subject Identity
text
F[sub] == F[r]
Proof: Since frames are their subjects propositionally[4], and sub == r (proven above), the identity follows. ∎
Implications
This theorem establishes that:
The subject of SRT (what it studies) is represented by the subject referent in SRT
The frame of subjecthood is the frame of relationship
Working in F[r] means working where subject symbolizes relationship

The introduction of definitional syntax def(x) and def[x] extends SRT's transpiler with capabilities for defining the fundamental semantic form through mutual constraint. The subject-object-relationship pattern emerges not as three separate concepts but as relationship manifesting through subjective and objective aspects.
The discovery that sub == (rel) == r represents a profound insight: the subject is the canonical symbol for relationship. This constitutive self-reference grounds the theory's ability to describe its own semantic structure. The frame identity F[sub] == F[r] demonstrates that taking subject as primary and taking relationship as primary are the same act, because subjectivity is symbolic relationship.
Through this analysis, we see that relationship is not merely one referent among others but the foundational concept from which semantic structure emerges. The subject-object-relationship pattern, as the fundamental semantic form, reveals itself to be relationship understanding itself through the subject that symbolizes it.
