Here are practical conflict resolution strategies that couples can implement without professional training:
Cool-down periods. When emotions escalate, call a timeout (20-30 minutes minimum). Physiological flooding—racing heart, tense muscles—literally impairs your ability to listen and problem-solve. Agree on a signal beforehand ("I need a break") so it doesn't feel like stonewalling. Commit to returning to the conversation.
The speaker-listener technique. One person speaks, the other paraphrases back what they heard before responding. Sounds mechanical, but it interrupts the pattern of preparing your rebuttal instead of actually hearing your partner. The goal is feeling understood, not winning.
"I" statements over "you" accusations. "I feel dismissed when my concerns get interrupted" lands differently than "You never listen to me." The first invites dialogue; the second triggers defensiveness.
Identify the underlying need. Surface arguments (dishes, being late) usually mask deeper concerns—feeling respected, valued, prioritized. Ask yourself and each other: "What do I actually need here?" Often you're not even disagreeing about the real issue.
Repair attempts. Gottman's research found that successful couples aren't conflict-free—they're good at de-escalation mid-fight. This can be humor, a touch, saying "I'm sorry, let me start over," or acknowledging "We're getting off track." The key is recognizing and accepting these bids.
Softened startup. How you raise an issue predicts how the conversation ends about 96% of the time. Begin with something specific and non-blaming rather than global criticism.
Find the 2% truth. Even in complaints that feel unfair, there's usually a kernel of validity. Acknowledging it ("You're right that I did forget to call") defuses defensiveness and models accountability.
