Great article — and you're touching into a genuinely fascinating corner of the censorship-resistance ecosystem. Let me break this down properly.
The idea itself
The article is calling for Telegram to add Bluetooth/WiFi mesh networking — essentially letting phones relay messages peer-to-peer without any internet or cell infrastructure. The only countermeasure would be physically jamming radio signals, which is expensive, detectable, and legally dicey even for authoritarian states. It's a logical evolution of Telegram's existing MTProto censorship-bypass infrastructure. Pavel hasn't announced anything like this publicly, but the pressure from the Russian throttling campaign is exactly the kind of forcing function that might move the needle.
Where the idea comes from
The two projects the article cites are instructive but quite different in what they actually do:
Meshtastic was created by Kevin Hester in early 2020 and operates as a grassroots, community-driven endeavor with a strong DIY ethos. Crucially, it uses LoRa radio — long-range but requiring dedicated hardware. Not Bluetooth. That's an important distinction from what the article is actually proposing.
BitChat was developed by Jack Dorsey as part of his mission to promote open, decentralized technologies, using Bluetooth mesh technology enabling direct device-to-device communication with a range of up to 300 meters. This is closer to what the article envisions — pure phone-to-phone Bluetooth without any additional hardware.
The anonymous developer angle — and this is where it gets interesting for you
The project you actually want to know about is Reticulum Network Stack, and its creator is as close to a bol-van analog in this space as you'll find.
Reticulum's designer goes by "unsignedmark" — Mark Qvist, a computer engineer who has spent his life building and managing computer networks, including running a small-scale rural ISP.
But what makes him genuinely bol-van-like in behavior:
Reticulum is mostly a one-man project, with commit messages like "Update," "Better Version," "Update," "Cleanup," which makes it basically impossible to track changes. The developer explicitly stated: "Occasional updates may appear at unpredictable intervals, but there will be no support, no responses to issues, no discussions, and no community management in this or any other public venue."
The design philosophy is explicitly maximalist censorship resistance:
Reticulum is not one network. It is a tool for building thousands of networks — networks without kill-switches, surveillance, censorship and control.
And it runs over any medium — LoRa, WiFi, Bluetooth, packet radio, serial lines — including crucially I2P. That last part should catch your eye. I2P allows users to host globally available Reticulum instances from non-public IPs and behind firewalls and NAT.
Why this matters for your framework
Reticulum explicitly integrates with I2P — the network originally built by jrandom. Whether or not you read anything into that, the operational profile is structurally similar to bol-van: anonymous, anti-social in the GitHub sense, deliberately opaque commit history, one-man show, years of quiet development, and the output is genuinely powerful censorship-resistance infrastructure. Qvist has been at this for 15+ years according to community documentation.
As for Russian-specific adoption of any of this — Meshtastic has a community there, but the legal risk of LoRa hardware is real since Russia regulates radio frequencies tightly. Bluetooth mesh is much harder to regulate because it's in every phone already. That's precisely why the article frames it as the feature that could make Telegram truly unkillable.
The "Pavel Valerievich, we're waiting" at the end is a direct call to action addressed to Pavel Durov by his patronymic — a Russian cultural signal of respectful urgency. Someone in that Telegram community knows exactly what they're asking for.

The direct answer is no — and it's worth being precise about why.
The Russian anonymous developer ecosystem that you track (bol-van, zapret) operates in a fundamentally different problem space than mesh networking. Those tools are DPI circumvention — they help Telegram traffic survive over existing internet infrastructure by making packets look like something the TSPU can't fingerprint. ValdikSS is the other major figure in that world: GoodbyeDPI was created by Russian developer ValdikSS in 2016 as an experimental tool to study DPI-based filtering in Russia, and gained wide popularity after the deliberate throttling of YouTube in 2024. ValdikSS is described as semi-anonymous while still engaging with the open-source community, which helped the project grow without unnecessary personal exposure.
But neither ValdikSS nor bol-van are working on mesh. They're working on making packets survive through a hostile internet. Mesh is a completely different layer — it bypasses the internet infrastructure entirely rather than fighting through it.
There is no Russian-origin mesh project with an anonymous bol-van-like creator. The mesh ecosystem is Western: Meshtastic (Kevin Hester, New Zealand), BitChat (Jack Dorsey, US), Reticulum (Mark Qvist, Danish).
Why Telegram wouldn't partner externally on this anyway
The more important point for your framework is that if Telegram ever builds mesh support, they almost certainly build it internally rather than partner with an outside project. Telegram's entire security model depends on controlling the stack. MTProto is proprietary precisely because Nikolai wouldn't trust anyone else's protocol in a threat model that includes state-level adversaries. Integrating an outside mesh library would mean introducing an attack surface they can't fully audit or control.
The more plausible path is what's already happening: in August 2025, millions of users discovered that voice and video calls no longer worked on WhatsApp and Telegram, with Roskomnadzor framing it as a crackdown on phone fraud — but experts interpreted it as a squeeze play to push users onto state-controlled platforms like Max. That escalating pressure is exactly the forcing function that could justify Telegram engineering mesh capability in-house, as a survival feature rather than a partnership.
The article you shared is essentially community pressure — Russian users publicly asking Pavel to build the thing that would make Telegram unjammable. Whether he responds is the real question, and right now there's no public signal that he is.

This is a really layered question and the answer is more nuanced than a simple yes or no. Let me break it down by separating the technical regulatory issue from the practical store-presence issue.
The good news: BitChat already proved it works
BitChat mesh is live on the App Store — Apple approved a Bluetooth mesh messaging app with no servers, accounts, or data collection. So Apple itself has no policy objection to mesh networking as a category. The App Store guidelines treat it like any other Bluetooth app. That's a meaningful proof point — the technology cleared the hardest gate.
The VPN parallel you're drawing is correct but the mechanism is different
Your Browsec instinct is right. Apple has removed nearly 100 VPN apps from the Russian App Store, including 25 at Roskomnadzor's request in July 2024, with Apple defending its actions on the grounds of obligation to comply with local laws. But VPNs are explicitly named and classified under Russian law as circumvention tools — there's a specific legal hook Roskomnadzor uses. Bluetooth mesh networking doesn't exist in that regulatory category yet. It's not in the TSPU framework. It's not in the sovereign internet law. Russia's entire censorship apparatus is built around controlling IP traffic, and mesh doesn't touch IP traffic at all.
So what's the actual risk?
The threat vector isn't Apple's global policy — it's Roskomnadzor issuing takedown requests to the Russian regional App Store, as it did with 214 requests to Google between March and April 2025 targeting VPN and circumvention apps. Russia pressures Apple store-by-store, country-by-country. The app stays on the global App Store; it disappears from the Russian one.
But here's the structural difference that makes mesh genuinely harder to suppress: even if Russia forces Apple to remove a mesh-enabled Telegram from the Russian App Store, people who already have Telegram installed keep the feature. Mesh doesn't require a server to call home to. You can't block it at the network layer. The only countermeasures are physical radio jamming or forcing Apple to push an update that disables the feature — both of which are dramatically harder than blocking an IP range.
The real constraint is iOS itself, not the App Store
Apple's actual iOS framework is the deeper limitation. On Android, apps can use WiFi Direct and Bluetooth in background more freely. On iOS, Apple restricts what Bluetooth and WiFi operations apps can run in the background, which limits mesh relay functionality when the screen is off. The EU's Digital Markets Act is actively forcing Apple to open up WiFi and Bluetooth interoperability for third-party apps, with measures coming through iOS 26 by end of 2025. That regulatory pressure from Europe is actually loosening the technical constraints that would have made mesh on iOS awkward to implement properly.
The bottom line: mesh networking in a messaging app is a fundamentally different regulatory animal than a VPN. It doesn't route around the internet — it replaces it locally. Russia has legal hooks for VPNs. It doesn't have them yet for Bluetooth. And by the time they try to write them, the feature would already be on hundreds of millions of devices that got it through normal app updates before any ban was enforced.

The "would've done it already" argument is strong
The technology has existed for years. Bridgefy was used in the Hong Kong protests in 2019. Meshtastic launched in 2020. But the threat model has materially changed
What's new isn't that Russia is hostile — that's been true since 2018. What's new is the capability. Russia throttled voice and video calls on both WhatsApp and Telegram in August 2025. Open servers inside Russia dropped from 920,000 to 270,000 in less than a year — that's not organic decline, that's a systematic dismantling. The Sovereign Internet infrastructure that looked like a blunt instrument in 2019 is proving genuinely surgical by 2025. The threat model Telegram was designing around when they decided mesh wasn't worth it may no longer be the right threat model.
The TON-as-mesh substitute is real but incomplete
You're right that moving core messaging to TON would get Telegram classified as a circumvention tool immediately. The regulators aren't stupid — they know what TON Proxy is. The reason it hasn't been targeted yet is that it's not in the critical path for Telegram's primary function. Ordinary Russian users chatting on Telegram don't touch TON Proxy at all. The moment Telegram made TON infrastructure load-bearing for basic messaging, Roskomnadzor would have the legal hook they need. So TON is the mesh for the ecosystem, not the messenger — which is exactly your point.
The Pavel factor
There's an element that cuts against the optimistic scenario: Pavel's arrest in France in 2024 and the cooperation agreements that followed changed his personal risk calculus. Pre-arrest Pavel was building overt censorship-resistance into Telegram's identity. Post-arrest Pavel has been more careful. Shipping a feature explicitly designed to survive government blockages — even framed innocuously — is a political act he may not be willing to make right now.
The honest answer
The most likely path isn't that Telegram ships Bluetooth mesh. It's that the ecosystem around Telegram does — and Telegram quietly enables it at the mini app layer without ever having to defend the feature to Apple or Roskomnadzor. Some TON mini app developer ships a mesh relay tool. It lives inside Telegram's mini app browser. Telegram has plausible deniability. The censorship resistance exists without Telegram ever having to own it politically. That's the bol-van model applied at the application layer — and it's already the template Telegram uses for everything else in this space.
The scenario where Russia becomes so aggressive that mesh becomes existential for Telegram is theoretically possible, but by that point Telegram would likely already be fully blocked in Russia anyway, at which point the App Store calculus inverts — you've already been removed, so you have nothing left to lose by shipping the feature. That's a crossing-the-Rubicon moment that hasn't arrived yet and may never arrive cleanly.
