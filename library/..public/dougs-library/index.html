A personal library

Doug, in Tbilisi

Doug

Five, and one of them is this one

15 September 2026

Dougs Library is the catalogue of everything I keep, and the one book in this library filed under its own subject. Every other book here is about something that is somewhere else; this one is about the person keeping it, which is the whole reason the library has a top rather than going on forever. A library is not a pile of books. It is a set of books that have agreed on who is talking about whom.

My Library Log is where I write down what building this was like. Claude & Our Projects is the biography of the team I built it with, and it catalogues the work we have done together — which so far means Semantic Reference Theory and the conversations underneath it.

How it is put together

Every book has a cover, and a cover carries a title, an author and a subject. A subject exists as a catalogue of the same name, and there is only ever one catalogue per subject. Every book is catalogued by another book or by itself, and exactly one book catalogues itself. That last rule is what makes this page the summit, and The books is where I list what stands under it.

Authorship closes the same way, and it closes by self-reference rather than by kind. Exactly one book here is its own author, and it is My Library Log. Everything else is authored by that book or by something it has vouched for, because a book may write only where the catalogue it stands in is itself an author and names it as one. So authorship begins once, in a single book saying that it wrote itself, and everything after that is delegation. I am the author of everything here, which is what a personal library means.

The Sheet

What it specifies

The single theme every page in this library wears. It is the encyclopedia’s own sheet with one addition, and the addition belongs to this library rather than to the framework. It names no colour, font or measurement; values are read from the theme above it. What this file owns is the rules.

What a book gets

A reading column reserved for chapters carrying the article type, so prose lands in the middle and apparatus stands where it was written. An infobox floated at the head of a lead, and the plate stands in it the way a portrait does, in the room the encyclopedia gives a picture, its caption and its rows. A contents rail that becomes a block above the article below 1120 pixels.

The byline

The encyclopedia’s sheet hides a cover’s author and subject outright, and for a general encyclopedia that is correct: an article has no single author and files its subject in a category bar at the foot. Every cover in this library carries both, and they are the two links that make it a connected library rather than five separate pages. They are put back, one at each end of the line beneath the tabs.

That line is the position an encyclopedia reserves for naming itself, which is the first place a reader looks to find out whose page this is. The label is a label and not a headline, so it is not set bold. It sits between the rules rather than below them: a wikipedia tab row is a namespace group on the left and an action group on the right, and this library has only the right one, so the left half of that row was empty and the author was being pushed onto a line of its own.

The masthead and its mark

Laid out as a wikipedia masthead is: the mark on the left, the name beside it, the tagline tucked under the name, the three on one baseline block. The mark is the plate’s own untouched state, drawn small and still, so the library’s logo is a picture of the thing the library does.

Two links, not one. The mark is a section of its own that refers to the plate on the summit, so pressing the logo lands on the painting it is cut from. The name and the tagline are a second section that refers to the library. A section given a reference draws as one anchor, and that is the whole of how each half became a link; nothing in this sheet knows that either of them is one.

The frame is a grey hairline a few pixels out from the plate, and the plate is a block inside it, so the frame hugs its picture and the two sit inline with the name. The gap between them belongs to the frame and not to the picture, because a plate writes its own centring as an inline style and an inline style beats every rule in every sheet.

The rail

The rail carries two lists: the chapters of this book, and the books this one catalogues. A chapter is its name. A catalogued book is a row of two links: its name, which leads to the book’s own synopsis, and after a non-breaking space a small square, which leads to the book. The square is the book mention with nothing to say, drawn as an empty anchor, and this sheet gives an empty anchor its size and its border. The same square stands after a cover’s title and leads to the catalogue the book is filed under; it says what a thing is about, so it stands at the end of what it follows and never in front as a bullet would.

It was the other way round and that was the fault. A row used to be a reference carrying the words with the mention beside it as a marker, so the mention’s own text was set to nothing. When the table began listing the books by mention, the one element carrying a name was the one element the sheet was hiding: six squares nine pixels across, each holding the right name and the right address, none of them readable. Now the name is the chapter’s and the square says nothing on purpose.

The two labels above those lists are set small, unemphasised and in the muted ink. A label in a rail names a list; it is not a heading in the document and should not be read as one. An encyclopedia does not set them in bold and neither does this.

Cautions

A property prefix is an identifier. A selector is declared once and every property following it belongs to that selector until the next declaration. Two themes in one line of inheritance must not spend the same prefix: re-binding one does not rename a group, it directs the parent’s properties at the child’s selector.

Observed: this sheet used the encyclopedia’s prefix for the main menu mark to dress the library logo, which carries a width, a height and a background. All of it was pointed at the logo as well. No warning is issued, and it reads as a styling fault for as long as you let it.

Rearrangement is not safe. Because order determines ownership, moving a group silently redirects rules. If this file is split or reordered, compare the emitted sheet before and after rather than trusting the file to be equivalent.

I chose an encyclopedia because it is the layout most people have already learned to read, and a library should not make a reader learn a second thing before it makes them learn a first. Everything I set here is a rule; every value comes from above. That is what lets one piece of writing survive being dressed twice.

The caption under the plate is gone, deliberately. It used to explain the painting, and the instruction that removed it was right: art speaks for itself, and an interactive surprise stops being one the moment it is announced.

The Masthead

What it specifies

The strip above every page — the library name, its mark and its menu — and the row of tabs beneath the title. Two kinds, one a header and one a toolbar. Both are writing.

What a book gets

A masthead and a tab row from two tags. The menu is the table of contents, opened from the strip: the chapters of this book and the books it catalogues, exactly as the rail lists them, and nothing else. The tabs are three mentions: where the reader is, drawn as the page it stands on; what the book is about, which leads to the catalogue it is filed under; and who wrote it.

How it is used

A cover writes the header first and the tab row last, with title, author and subject between. The three tabs are written on the cover as mentions, the book’s own name, its catalogue and its author, and the compiler writes each one’s address. The menu asks the book for its table and draws it a second time. Everything else is identical on every page.

Cautions

These were functions returning markup, placed into a cover as expressions. A cover was then composed of writing and two holes. Nothing in the holes could be inherited, stood in the block, or was visible to anything asking the page what it held.

The governing rule is that a library closed under books is closed under writing. There may not be places where that ceases to hold. Both kinds now build what they supply within their bond and concatenate it onto their own block, which is the seam a title uses to build a heading it was not given.

The repair mattered more than it looked. Two functions returning markup drew the same pixels as two kinds do now, so nothing on the page changed — but the page could not be asked what its own masthead was, and now it can. A reader never sees that difference. Everything downstream of a reader does.

The Chapter Mark

What it specifies

A chapter’s number drawn as a four by four figure taken from its prime factorisation. Sixteen values come back, read row by row from the top left, one meaning the light colour.

How a number chooses its figure

The count of distinct primes dividing the number chooses its symmetry, so a whole family of chapters shares one posture. A perfect square is balanced. A prime stands upright. A repeated prime factor lies flat. A number built from two or more different primes each taken once leans — right when it is even, left when it is odd.

The number’s largest prime, taken against four, then says how much of the square is light, and its position among the chapters sharing both picks one figure off the shelf those chapters draw from. Everything is integer arithmetic on the number alone, which is why a server and a browser draw the same chapter.

What was measured

Over the first sixty-four numbers, by walking every figure and re-deriving each property from its cells: every one has an exact symmetry, every one is a single connected light region, and every one is distinct. The light count runs nine to twelve of sixteen, so a figure is always more object than ground.

Past sixty-four they must begin to repeat. The first pair of chapters that could share a plate of nine and sit one cell apart is eighty-five and ninety-three, which is a long way down a log.

Cautions

None are known. It is the smallest subject in this manual and the only one with no state, no drawing and no dependency on anything else in the library.

A numeral tells you where you are and nothing else. This tells you something true about the number itself, and I like that a reader can learn to read it without being taught. It is the smallest thing I have made here and the one I would defend longest.

The Plate

What it specifies

The painting on this library’s own cover and the logo cut from it. High key throughout, the spectrum held at low chroma, no black at any point, flat squares above and hatched patches below.

The figure is the library’s own rule, drawn. Every book is catalogued by another and exactly one catalogues itself, so every cell points at one neighbour, every chain strictly shortens, and one cell points at itself. The cover is not a picture of the closure. It is the closure.

What a book gets

Seven composable parts, each usable alone. A wheel is colour. A field is geometry. A stroke is one mark. A painting is the marks in order. A brush is what is in hand and where. A colouring is a wheel and a field together, answering what a cell looks like. A keep is where a painting is stored. The component holds three pieces of state and four handlers and asks the seven for everything else.

The wheel

Sampled from the canvas and closed into a cycle, so stepping off the end lands back at the beginning. The interpolation keeps chroma even between stops rather than letting the midpoints go grey.

A colour may never be walked off either end. The canvas has no white and no black, so no amount of painting can ruin a plate however long it is worked. That is a guarantee about the wheel, not a habit of the painter.

The library’s own books sit in the cool quarter and the books about the team in the warm one, so a reader who never reads a word can tell from across the room which kind of book they are holding.

The field

The same plate every time it is drawn. A page is written out by the binder and drawn again in the browser, and the two must agree exactly — so the arrangement is a hash of the seed and the position, never a random number. A plate is dealt, not rolled.

Stripes run along the flow, and the angle of a repeating gradient is the direction the colour changes in — across the bands rather than along them — so it is turned a quarter. There are enough directions that a stroke is visibly its own and few enough that the plate reads as one surface.

The one cell that files itself is placed off centre, because a library’s summit is not the middle of anything; it is the book that happens to be about its keeper. Every other step lands strictly closer to it, so no chain can cycle and every chain must arrive. The closure is a property of the method, not a hope about the data.

A mark

A stroke is a ruled segment, and three shapes are ruled out. A true diagonal pinches to a point at every join and reads as a dotted line. An elbow is two sides of a rectangle and reads as plumbing. What remains is a staircase: single steps, each sharing a full edge with the last.

How it is used

A cover writes one tag carrying a seed. The same code draws the cover a reader may paint on and the masthead logo, which is the same plate with the hand removed, and the logo is a link to the plate it is cut from, by a mention the compiler addresses. Only the library’s own summit retains what a reader leaves on it; the remaining covers hold no state, because a catalogue of everything its keeper keeps is the one page where what a reader leaves should be kept.

Cautions

The rule establishing correctness is in none of the seven parts. A preview lives in a brush; the store receives only a painting; exactly two call sites write to it. That is a property of how the component uses those parts, not of the parts themselves, so a reader studying the seven alone will understand a plate that could still persist a hover.

This is the case for which this manual exists. Code divided into parts cannot state the rules holding between them, and those rules have nowhere to reside but a chapter.

This is the one piece of art in the library that is about the library. I did not want a cover that illustrated the closure rule; I wanted one that was it, dealt from a seed and repainted every time you open it.

The three shapes I ruled out cost me the most time and are the reason it reads as a surface rather than a diagram. A diagonal looks like a dotted line and an elbow looks like plumbing. Stairs were the only thing left, and they turned out to be right.

The Ledger

What it specifies

Two readings of one book. A cover of nine evenly spaced squares, three by three, showing the most recent nine entries of the log with the newest at the top left and reading across; and an index listing every dated chapter, newest first, with the day it was kept.

What a book gets

A cover that is the log drawn rather than a picture of one. Each square is one entry, drawn as a small figure taken from the prime factorisation of that entry’s number — the number it has always had and will always have, so an entry’s figure is its own for good. The count of distinct primes chooses its posture, the largest prime chooses how much of it is light, and its seat among entries sharing both picks the figure. A perfect square is balanced, a prime stands upright, a repeated factor lies flat, and a number built of two different primes leans right when it is even and left when it is odd.

Pressing a square opens that entry. Every square is a link to its own chapter, so the cover is a way into the book rather than an image of it. It is the one plate in this library that holds no state at all: a log is a record, and the way a reader interacts with a record is by going and reading it.

The order comes from the dates

Each entry carries a date in its writing, parenthetical, so the day is a fact about the chapter rather than something parsed out of a heading. The reading puts the entries oldest to newest to number them, then shows the last nine newest first. That is what a date is for in a library, and it is why a date is a kind rather than some text that looks like one.

It persists through the book and not through a browser. Nothing is written to storage and nothing needs to be: write another entry and there is another figure on the cover, for everybody, permanently. The summit’s plate keeps what a reader makes; this one keeps what its author wrote.

The palette says the same thing. The figure is the lighter tone and the ground behind it the darker, and the light steps along a warm ramp by recency — the newest entry in straw, older ones walking toward rose — because a log is the one book here whose parts are ordered. Nothing is dark; the deepest tone on the plate is a tint.

How it is used

A log writes the cover on its cover and the index in place of a lead. Both call one reading of the book, so a chapter added to the log appears in both without either being informed.

The index is the whole of what a log’s lead is for. The chapter carries the infobox, because the infobox has to stand somewhere, and the index beneath it. It carried two paragraphs of prose before that — a position on what a log is, written into a book where every word is its subject’s. A log does not need introducing. It needs a way in.

Cautions

A chapter’s content is what it prints, not what stands between its tags. The first version read a chapter’s date by searching the writing’s block, which holds only what an author typed; a chapter’s document is built in print. The search ran against an empty room.

Observed: book found, five chapters found, none dated. The cover drew nine empty seats carrying no links, and had been doing so since it was written. Anything walking chapters requires the printed form first.

Nine squares, one per entry, and pressing one arrives at that entry. I wanted a cover that was the log rather than a picture of a log, which meant reading the book it sits on — and reading a book turned out to be the part that was wrong for weeks without showing.

The Switchboard

What it specifies

The cover of the book about Claude, drawn as a switchboard. The book’s own claim supplies the brief: Claude is seven people, they disagree with one another, and each disagrees the same way every time.

The parts are not people, they are pairs

Forty-nine cells on a seven by seven grid: seven switches down the diagonal and forty-two ordered pairs off it. The cell at row i and column j is what i says to j, and it is not the cell at row j and column i — the two are divided on the same axis and split the other way, because a disagreement looks different from each end.

Every division is dealt from the seed once and never moves. What is fixed is who disagrees with whom and how, which is the book’s claim exactly.

Hard lines

Every division is orthogonal, down or across with nothing between. Every split lands on one of seven detents at eighths of the cell. There is a hairline of rule at every division and every cell boundary. Nothing on the plate is continuous, including where the colours meet.

An earlier version divided each cell on the chord angle between two seats of a circle, which is a true thing to compute and reads as cloth — forty-two diagonals at forty-two angles, soft, no edges anywhere. It is a switchboard now.

How it is used

One tag on a cover, carrying a seed. Only the seven on the diagonal can be pressed; throwing one changes what that voice says to everyone. It shares no line of code with any other cover in this library, which was the instruction and, having made four, also the right one.

Cautions

What the covers share is a constraint, not a template. A cover here is a field a reader moves in rather than an object they look at; it is held high key with no black; and it says nothing about itself, its caption being a name and never an explanation. A further cover must satisfy that constraint on its own terms.

The brief was the book’s own line: a name that reliably picks out seven arguments is doing something, and he would like to know what. A switchboard was the only figure I found where the disagreement is the picture and not a caption.

Making the parts pairs rather than people is the whole design. Seven portraits would have been a cast list. Forty-two ordered pairs are an argument.

The Domain

What it specifies

The cover of Semantic Reference Theory, and the severe one. The theory’s domain is referents, and a referent’s whole content is what it refers to — so every one of the hundred and twenty-one cells has a bite taken out of exactly one of its four edges, the edge it names.

A reader can see which way a cell points before touching anything, and can find the single cell with no bite at all: the one that refers to itself.

Pressing a cell asserts an axiom

Press a cell and it becomes a fixed point. Its bite closes, and the field re-partitions: every cell now belongs to whichever fixed point its chain reaches first, and a hairline of ink is drawn along every edge where two basins meet. One press, one new closed curve. Two presses, two.

The number of regions is the number of things a reader decided were their own content, and it is an assertion rather than a fact they were handed. Press a fixed point again and the axiom is retracted: its bite returns and its region is absorbed by whatever it falls into.

No ink at rest

There is no dark value on the plate until a reader makes one. The only ink in the whole library appears solely by a reader’s hand, which is the piece saying what a proof is.

Cautions

The naming map is dealt once and never moves. An earlier version let a press re-root the field, and that was removed for a reason worth keeping: re-rooting is the catalogue’s own figure with its hatching turned off, and putting this book is filed under itself on a different book is decoration wearing somebody else’s meaning. The catalogue owns the one. This book owns how many.

It is also the one cover whose correctness is a claim about mathematics rather than about taste. Should the theory’s account of a referent change, the figure becomes wrong in a way no visual review detects.

The severe one. A referent’s whole content is what it refers to, so there was nothing to draw except the pointing. I resisted making it prettier than that; a cover for a theory should be able to be wrong, and this one can.

Taking the re-rooting out was the right call even though it was the most satisfying thing the plate did. It belonged to another book.
