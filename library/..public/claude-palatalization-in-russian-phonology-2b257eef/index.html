The ь letter in Russian denotes changing the sound of something what is the vocabulary that I would use in English to describe the change to that sound and it’s opposite
Claude
16:36 UTC, 23 April 2026
The ь marks palatalization. A consonant followed by ь is palatalized (also called "soft" in Russian tradition) — the tongue body is raised toward the hard palate simultaneously with the primary articulation, giving that characteristic "y-colored" quality. In IPA this is notated with a superscript j, e.g. /tʲ/, /nʲ/, /lʲ/.
The opposite — consonants without ь (or marked by ъ in older orthography) — are non-palatalized, or just plain. In the Russian tradition these are called hard (твёрдый) vs soft (мягкий).
A useful nuance: some Russian "hard" consonants aren't merely the absence of palatalization but activelyvelarized — the tongue is pulled back toward the velum. The classic case is hard /ɫ/ (the "dark L" in лампа), which contrasts with palatalized /lʲ/ in соль. So the full pair of processes is:
palatalization ↔ velarization (active secondary articulations at opposite ends of the palate)
or more neutrally: palatalized ↔ plain/non-palatalized
The process of removing palatalization is depalatalization, though in Russian phonology you more often just talk about the hard/soft contrast as a lexical/phonemic feature rather than as a synchronic process.

Claude
16:38 UTC, 23 April 2026
That's a genuinely reasonable reaction, and you're not wrong about the articulation — you've actually put your finger on something real.
"Soft" and "hard" in the Slavic tradition are auditory/impressionistic labels, not articulatory ones. To a Russian ear, palatalized consonants sound lighter, higher-pitched, more "liquid" — hence soft. But articulatorily, palatalized consonants require an additional gesture: you're making the primary closure (say, /t/) and simultaneously bunching the tongue body up toward the hard palate. That's genuinely more muscular work than a plain /t/. So your intuition that "soft" feels harder to produce is correct — it's a more complex articulation.
The terminology is a historical artifact of how the sounds sound, not how they're made. English-speaking phoneticians often find the hard/soft labels confusing for exactly your reason and tend to prefer palatalized / non-palatalized, which describes the articulation directly and avoids the misleading effort connotation.
One practical note: many learners find it easier to approach palatalization not as "add a gesture to the consonant" but as "glide the consonant into a /j/-like position" — essentially think of ть as aiming toward the tongue position of English y in "yes" while you make the t. That often feels less like extra work and more like a single coordinated movement, which is closer to how native speakers actually execute it.

It’s not just articulation, but it’s because a higher sound to me. Could actually hurt my ears more than a lower one. A low pitch ends in silence where is a high pitch ends in breaking glass
Claude
16:39 UTC, 23 April 2026
That's a real and well-documented phenomenon, not just a personal quirk. A few things are going on:
Psychoacoustically, the human ear is most sensitive to frequencies roughly between 2–5 kHz — which is exactly the range palatalization boosts. Palatalized consonants raise the second formant (F2) and concentrate energy in higher frequencies; plain/velarized consonants push energy lower. So palatalized sounds genuinely stimulate your most sensitive hearing range more aggressively. The ear has less natural attenuation up there.
Perceptually, your "breaking glass vs. silence" intuition maps onto something real: low frequencies decay into rumble and then into inaudibility, but high frequencies carry sharpness, sibilance, and the acoustic signature of damage — screams, shattering, scraping, alarms. Evolution tuned us to treat high-frequency transients as salient and often threatening. Low frequencies are felt as much as heard (chest, body); high frequencies are piercing, localized, and harder to ignore or tune out.
Clinically, there's a condition called hyperacusis — reduced tolerance to sound, often specifically to high frequencies — and a related phenomenon called recruitment where, in ears with some high-frequency hearing loss, the remaining hair cells respond with exaggerated loudness once sound crosses a threshold. Many people without any diagnosed condition still sit somewhere on a spectrum of high-frequency sensitivity; it's a real axis of individual variation, not imagined.
So when you say palatalized consonants feel harder on your ears, you're describing a combination of (1) the acoustic fact that they carry more high-frequency energy, (2) the psychoacoustic fact that that's where your ear is most sensitive, and (3) possibly just where you personally sit on the sensitivity spectrum. The Slavic ear hears "soft"; yours hears "sharp." Both are accurate descriptions of the same acoustic signal.

What is the difference between звонкий и глухой звук Answer in english
Claude
16:41 UTC, 23 April 2026
Those are the Russian terms for voiced (звонкий) and voiceless / unvoiced (глухой) sounds.
The distinction is whether your vocal folds vibrate during the consonant:
Voiced (звонкий): vocal folds vibrate. Put your fingers on your throat and say a sustained /z/ — you'll feel the buzz. Russian voiced consonants include б, в, г, д, ж, з.
Voiceless (глухой): vocal folds are open and don't vibrate; only airflow and the oral articulation produce the sound. Say a sustained /s/ — no buzz, just hiss. Russian voiceless consonants include п, ф, к, т, ш, с.
Russian has neat voiced/voiceless pairs that share the same place and manner of articulation and differonly in voicing: б/п, в/ф, г/к, д/т, ж/ш, з/с.
The literal translations are evocative: звонкий comes from звон (ringing, resonance) — a "ringing" sound. Глухой literally means "deaf" or "muffled" — a "dulled" sound. English "voiced/voiceless" is more mechanical and just names the larynx gesture directly.
Two facts worth knowing, since they trip up learners:
Final devoicing. Russian voiced obstruents become voiceless at the end of a word. Хлеб is pronounced khlep, друг is druk, муж is mush. The spelling keeps the voiced letter, but the sound is voiceless.
Voicing assimilation in clusters. When voiced and voiceless obstruents meet, the second one usually wins and the first assimilates to it. Водка → votka (д devoices before к). Сделать → zdelat' (с voices before д). This happens automatically in fluent speech.
So the voicing contrast is phonemic (it distinguishes words — том vs. дом), but it's neutralized in these two predictable environments.
