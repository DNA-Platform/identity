Core Concepts in SRT
Referents
A referent is the fundamental unit in SRT - anything that can be assigned to a constant in the theory. As the documentation states: "a referent is defined to be an object which could be assigned to a constant." Referents are the domain over which the single relation R(x,y,t) operates. They can be subjects, objects, or relationship types themselves within the relational structure.
Reference
Reference is not a primitive but emerges from the relational structure. According to the formal definition in the documents:
srt
z refers to y ⟺ ∃x,t: x =x> y & z =t> y & t -> x
Reference is mediated - you can't refer directly but must go through a representation. The referent z refers to y by relating to y through a relationship type t that inherits from a representation x of y.
Representation
A representation is formally defined as:
srt
x -> representation ⟺ ∃y: x =x> y
This states that x is a representation if and only if there exists some y such that x represents y. The notation x =x> y means x relates to y through relationship type x itself - the relationship is self-named.
Symbol
A symbol is a special kind of representation with additional structure:
srt
x -> symbol ⟺ ∃y: x =x> y & (x,y) == x
A symbol not only represents something but is identical to the most specific relationship between itself and what it represents. This captures the idea that symbols are their relationships - they don't just have them.
The Derivation of x =x> y
Looking through our conversation and the documents, the notation x =x> y appears to have been derived through the concept of representation as self-relationship. Here's the logical progression:
The Most Specific Relationship: For any two distinct referents x and y related by some type, there exists a most specific relationship type between them, denoted (x,y).
Representation Through Relationship: When something represents something else, the relationship type itself becomes significant. The documents state: "A referent x is a representation of y if: x =x> y"
The Self-Named Relationship: The notation x =x> y expresses that x relates to y through a relationship type that is x itself. This captures the essence of representation - when x represents y, the relationship type IS x.
The key insight seems to have emerged when we recognized that representation isn't just any relationship but a special one where the subject names the relationship. As stated in the documents:
"x =x> y & x == (x,y) =|> x =sym> y"
This shows that when x represents y AND x is identical to the most specific relationship between x and y, then x is a symbol for y.
The Canonical Form
The documents also introduce canonical symbols using parentheses notation:

(y) =(y)> y
This states that the canonical symbol for y (denoted with parentheses) represents y through a relationship named after itself. The canonical symbol is the standard, agreed-upon representation.
Why This Notation?
The x =x> y notation elegantly captures several key ideas:
Self-Reference: The representation names itself in the relationship
Directness: It's the most immediate form of representation possible
Identity: For symbols, this relationship is what they are: (x,y) == x
The notation emerged from recognizing that representation requires the representing entity to somehow "point to" what it represents, and the cleanest way to formalize this is to have the relationship type be the representer itself.
Looking at the specific passage about "walking the dog," we see how this plays out:

The pronoun "He" represents the dog through a relationship named after itself. This is representation in its purest form - the word "He" pointing to the dog through the relationship of being "He."
The derivation seems to have crystallized when we realized that representation isn't just a feature some relationships have, but is fundamentally about entities that relate to other entities through relationships named after themselves. This gives representation its special character - it's not just any relationship but one where the subject provides the name for how it relates to its object.
