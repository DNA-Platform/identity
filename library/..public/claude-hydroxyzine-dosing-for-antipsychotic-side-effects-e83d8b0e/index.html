Standard adult dosing from the US prescribing label (hydroxyzine HCl / pamoate) and the EMA 2015 safety review:
Anxiety (labeled indication): 50–100 mg per dose, up to 4 times daily
Sedation / bedtime use (labeled as pre-procedure sedation; insomnia is off-label): 50–100 mg as a single dose. In practice most prescribers start insomnia use at 25 mg at bedtime, moving to 50 mg if needed
Maximum: EMA restricted adults to 100 mg/day total (50 mg/day in the elderly) after a 2015 QT-prolongation review; the US label has no such cap but 100 mg/dose is the top of the range
Onset/duration: roughly 30 min onset, ~4–6 h sedative effect, 20+ h half-life - so next-morning grogginess is common at 50 mg+
Two things worth having in front of you rather than me judging them:
Hydroxyzine is not a standard akathisia treatment in the psychiatric literature. The usual agents are propranolol, benzodiazepines, mirtazapine, and anticholinergics (benztropine). Diphenhydramine is sometimes used, and hydroxyzine is pharmacologically similar (first-gen antihistamine with anticholinergic activity), so it's plausible it helps the restlessness, but I haven't seen trial evidence for it specifically. Its documented benefit here is the sleep side.
QT: hydroxyzine prolongs QT at higher doses; aripiprazole is low-risk on that axis compared with other antipsychotics. Not a known dangerous pair, but it's the interaction prescribers check.

What does propanolol make ypu feel? How does it compare to benztropine
Claude
10:16 UTC, 29 August 2026
Propranolol, subjectively: most people notice very little, which is the point. It blocks adrenaline's peripheral effects, so the physical layer of restlessness and anxiety - racing pulse, tremor, the jittery "motor running" feeling - drops out, and heart rate falls noticeably (a resting pulse of 75 might sit at 60). At 10–30 mg doses people typically report feeling calmer and physically stiller without feeling sedated or mentally altered. The common complaints are tiredness or heaviness, cold hands and feet, lightheadedness on standing, and vivid dreams or disturbed sleep in some. It doesn't help you sleep directly; it may help indirectly by removing the physical agitation. It also blunts the adrenaline response to exercise, so hard cardio feels harder.
Benztropine, subjectively: a different mechanism entirely - it blocks acetylcholine, which rebalances dopamine/acetylcholine signaling in the basal ganglia. That makes it good for the parkinsonian side effects (stiffness, tremor, slowed movement, acute dystonia) but weak for akathisia, where trials show inconsistent benefit. Subjectively it's more noticeable and less pleasant: dry mouth, blurred near vision, constipation, urinary hesitancy, and at higher doses a foggy, memory-impaired feeling. Some people feel mildly euphoric or "loosened up," which is why it has minor misuse potential. Cognitive dulling is the main reason psychiatrists avoid it long-term.
Head-to-head for your case:
Akathisia specifically: propranolol has the better evidence and is the usual first choice; benztropine is what's reached for if there's also stiffness or dystonia mixed in.
Sleep: neither is a sleep aid; hydroxyzine or low-dose mirtazapine covers that side.
Side-effect profile: propranolol's are cardiovascular (pulse, BP, fatigue); benztropine's are anticholinergic (dry mouth, fog, constipation). Hydroxyzine already carries some anticholinergic load, so stacking benztropine on it doubles that.
Reversibility: both wear off within a day when stopped; propranolol at these low doses doesn't need tapering.
One caveat on propranolol you should know before deciding: it's contraindicated with asthma and can mask hypoglycaemia symptoms - relevant if the metformin question from earlier means diabetes is in the picture.

What propanolol dose would you expect the help? I seem to have 10 and 40 mg options.
Claude
10:22 UTC, 29 August 2026
Start with the 10 mg tablets. The akathisia literature (Adler, Lipinski et al., and the reviews that followed) used 10–30 mg two to three times daily, with most responders in the 30–80 mg/day range total; 10 mg three times a day is the standard starting point, and 20 mg three times daily is a typical second step if the first isn't enough after a few days. Effect on akathisia is usually apparent within a day or two, not weeks.
The 40 mg tablet is really a hypertension/migraine size. Taking it whole as a first dose is more than most akathisia protocols start with and is where lightheadedness and marked bradycardia become likely. It's scored, so halves (20 mg) are usable once you know how you respond.
Two practical points from the pharmacology:
Immediate-release propranolol lasts roughly 4–6 hours, which is why it's dosed multiple times daily. A morning-only dose won't cover evening restlessness.
A resting pulse below about 50–55, or feeling faint on standing, is the signal you've gone past your dose.
