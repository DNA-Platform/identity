This is a genuinely important and underanalyzed question. Let me build the full picture layer by layer, numbers first, then synthesize toward a tipping point model.
The War Burn Rate
Russia's military machine consumed approximately 1.4 trillion rubles per month through the first half of 2025 — roughly 46.9 billion rubles per day, or just under $600 million at current exchange rates. Weekly spending runs about 213 billion rubles, or $2.7 billion — an amount comparable to the annual budget of a major Russian region. Per Ukrainian intelligence, Russia is spending 13.1% of GDP — about 68% of all federal budget expenditures — on the war, meaning roughly two of every three rubles in federal spending go toward military activity.
What makes this particularly revealing: the budget deficit hit 3.7 trillion rubles (~$47 billion) in the first half of 2025 alone — six times the prior year's equivalent figure. Military spending is now 7.2% of GDP and represents the highest share of the budget since the Soviet era. The overheating is visible — GDP growth is expected to shrink to 1.3–1.5%, far below previous levels, and Russia's economy minister warned that the country is teetering on the edge of recession while current inflation hovers around 9.4%.
Running number: At ~$140B/year in direct military expenditure, Russia is burning through roughly $11.7B per month just on the war. That's before social payments, occupation administration, and the shadow costs buried outside the defense chapter.
The Iran/Oil Wildcard — The Temporary Counterweight
This is where it gets genuinely interesting and where you're right to flag it as a distinct factor.
Cargoes were previously being sold at around $50 per barrel; with Iran's effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz sending prices above $100, Russian crude is now fetching approximately $90 — a massive increase in both price and sales volume.
Russia produces roughly 10 million barrels per day. Run the math: at $50 net after discounts and shadow fleet costs, that's ~$182B/year in oil revenue. At $90 net, it's ~$328B/year — a $146B annualized windfall. Even discounting for production constraints, damaged infrastructure, and the fact that Russia can't fully ramp production quickly, the swing is enormous.
The US Treasury exemptions are in place only until April 11. The Kremlin welcomed the sanctions relief and pushed for the US to go further, while Europe — particularly Britain — declined to follow the US lead and kept their own sanctions in place. So this is real money, but it's structurally temporary. When it expires, Russia goes back to the shadow fleet discount structure. More critically: Russia felt the impact of over-reliance on a small pool of buyers in 2025, when China's crude oil demand plateaued and oil and gas revenues fell 20.5% in the first nine months.
The Iran situation doesn't solve Russia's structural problems — it buys runway. The windfall is probably enough to cover 12–18 months of budget deficit at current war burn rates, assuming the oil shock persists. But it's not a solution; it's a reprieve that Putin is almost certainly using to replenish the National Wealth Fund, which was effectively depleted of its liquid component by late 2024.
The Internet/Telegram Self-Sabotage — The Hidden Economic Hemorrhage
This is the most underappreciated damage vector, and it compounds in a way that the other factors don't.
Russia's 2025 internet shutdowns totaled 37,166 hours affecting approximately 146 million people, making it the global leader in internet censorship and shutdowns by a significant margin. The economic damage estimate: $11.9 billion in 2025, more than half the total global economic impact from internet shutdowns across all countries combined, which was $7.8 billion.
Now add Moscow specifically: Moscow's mobile internet blackout beginning March 5, 2026 was so extensive that foreign websites were blocked on mobile phones, and even government-approved Russian sites became inaccessible. Estimated losses from this single event reached $38 to $63 million, with residents turning to pagers and printed maps. Cash in circulation increased almost fivefold in recent months because card payment terminals stopped functioning reliably.
Russia once liked to boast about its advanced digitalization, but the regular loss of mobile internet means people are now being told to download maps for offline use, use landlines to order taxis, and send texts instead of messaging apps.
Your point about Telegram's architecture is exactly right. The 2018 attempt to ban Telegram outright knocked out Amazon Web Services and Google services across Russia with nearly 20 million IP addresses blocked — a demonstration that Telegram deliberately disguised its traffic across major cloud infrastructure. The current approach is more surgical but structurally equivalent in its consequences: the "16 KB Curtain" tactic throttles Cloudflare-hosted and Western websites by allowing only the first 16 kilobytes of data to load. Because Cloudflare underpins a large share of Western web infrastructure, targeting it marked a concrete step toward isolating Russian users from the global internet while preserving the appearance of partial connectivity.
Telegram's 93.6 million Russian users — over 60% of the country's population — would lose far more than a messaging app if it's blocked. It functions as a news platform, a way for officials and state agencies to interact with constituents, a frontline communications tool in the Ukraine war, a way for NGOs and news outlets to receive tips, and a business tool. Even some of Russia's most outspoken military bloggers are already complaining about Roskomnadzor's latest restrictions. Moscow is choosing regime security over military effectiveness, over soft power, and over human capital.
The compound effect: At $11.9B in 2025 damage, accelerating into 2026, this is currently running at roughly 0.5-0.7% of Russian GDP — annually, consistently, and permanently. It doesn't recover when the internet comes back on, because the businesses, contracts, and trust that erode during repeated blackouts don't restore automatically.
Brain Drain — The Slow Bleed That Doesn't Clot
Between 650,000 and 920,000 Russians emigrated since February 2022, with capital outflows exceeding $280 billion over three years. The brain drain particularly impacts technology, finance, management, and academic sectors — precisely the professionals Russia needs for economic modernization.
Approximately 80% of those who left had higher education and worked in fields requiring intellectual expertise like IT, data analysis, business, science, and culture. 42% of industrial firms were unable to find enough workers as of mid-2025, up from 35% in April.
The return rate narrative is more complicated than it looks. Some returned — but of those who returned between March and September 2022, around 80% had emigrated again by summer 2023. Many returns are tactical regrouping, not reintegration.
The GDP consequence: Russia's GDP as measured by purchasing power parity is expected to fall behind Indonesia's in 2026 — nearly two years earlier than would have been the case without the war and its emigration wave.
The Telegram-brain drain interaction is what makes your framing sharp. Internet shutdowns and Telegram restrictions aren't neutral signals to skilled workers still in Russia — they're active push factors. Every engineer inside Russia who relied on Telegram for work gets reminded weekly that this environment is incompatible with their career trajectory. The internet restrictions don't just cost money today; they accelerate the future outflow of the people who would generate tomorrow's tax revenue.
Putin's Personal Financial Architecture — What "Bleeding" Actually Means For Him
Swedish economist Anders Åslund estimated Putin's collective net worth at $100–130 billion, derived from his alleged control over Russian oligarchs. The theory is that after Khodorkovsky's arrest in 2003, other oligarchs effectively agreed to hand over approximately 50% of their earnings in exchange for protection from the same fate.
This is the critical insight for your question: Putin's wealth is not a savings account — it's a claims structure. He doesn't hold $130B in liquid assets. He holds a durable right to extract from the oligarch layer, which itself extracts from the state-corporate layer, which depends on oil revenues, a functioning economy, and labor productivity. This means "bleeding" for Putin is fundamentally different from bleeding for a wealthy private individual.
What actually threatens his financial position:
The oligarch extraction mechanism degrades when the oligarchs themselves run short. Russia's elites added $72 billion to their fortunes in the year to April 2024 — but that was military Keynesianism. The moment war-driven spending contracts, the oligarch base contracts with it.
His power over oligarchs is contingent on being able to protect them. If the economic base shrinks enough that he can no longer guarantee returns to loyalty, that protection racket breaks down. The Kremlin's ability to award contracts, guarantee market exclusivity, and provide legal impunity is the product he sells. An economy consuming 68% of its budget on war has fewer rents to distribute.
The frozen assets matter at the margin. An estimated $340 billion of Russian Central Bank reserves have been frozen, and frozen oligarch-linked assets in the West sit at around $30 billion. These aren't recoverable under current conditions, which means the system has permanently lost those resources as a backstop.
The Tipping Point Model
Here's how the cascade likely actually works — it's not a single shock but a compounding sequence:
Phase 1 (now — roughly 2027): Managed stress. The Iran oil windfall buys 12–18 months of budget relief. War costs remain sustainable if oil stays above $85. The internet damage ($12B/year) is real but manageable as a share of a $2T economy. Putin's patronage system remains funded.
Phase 2 (2027–2029): Structural squeeze. If oil normalizes post-Iran crisis, the budget deficit resumes. War costs can't easily be cut — doing so signals weakness and creates demobilization pressures. Internet damage compounds annually. Brain drain has now removed 3-4 cohorts of the most productive workers, creating an irreversible productivity gap. The oligarch extraction machine begins to yield less because the underlying assets generate less.
Phase 3 (tipping point trigger): The critical variable is not an absolute dollar loss — it's whether Putin can continue distributing enough to maintain the loyalty of the security services and the oligarch layer simultaneously. The inflection point arrives when he's forced to choose: either fund the war machine OR maintain elite loyalty payments. That choice is visible to the recipients, and visibility of that choice is destabilizing.
The internet/Telegram situation is the stealth accelerant here. Every month of blackouts is $1B+ in direct economic damage that doesn't fund loyalties, plus the emigration push factor amplifying long-term productivity loss. It's burning the economic base that funds the patronage system, in service of surveillance that ostensibly protects the regime — a self-defeating loop that gets worse at scale.
The Iran windfall is genuinely significant right now and buys him real runway. But it doesn't fix the structural trajectory. It's the equivalent of finding a wallet in the parking lot when you're six months behind on rent — it matters, it changes the immediate timeline, but it doesn't change where the road goes.

This is where you have to completely abandon the Western political playbook, because almost none of it applies. Let me build this from the actual mechanics.
What "Struggling" Actually Looks Like — And Why It's Invisible Until It Isn't
The first thing to understand is that Russian regime stress does not manifest as visible public opposition gaining momentum. That's the wrong model entirely. What you're watching for is a set of signals that are mostly internal, behavioral, and financial — and they're deliberately opaque.
The key insight is this: Putin's power is not held through popularity. It's held through a balance-of-terror management system in which every faction with armed men and money is kept simultaneously dependent on him and suspicious of every other faction. The siloviki factions are headed by close associates of Putin from the 1990s and early 2000s, leaving Putin as the one person able to balance them and ensure no single faction becomes too powerful. It is not in the primary interest of each faction to influence the political direction of the country — instead, they care about preserving the status quo that has permitted them to accumulate significant fortunes.
This is the architecture that has to break before anything visible happens. And the economic pressures you asked about in the previous analysis are precisely what stress-tests it.
The Observable Stress Indicators — In Rough Order of Severity
Tier 1: The quietest signals (early warning)
The most important top-down indicators are those pointing to dissatisfaction and power struggle among Russia's elite. If elites within the government grow dissatisfied, a significant uptick in resignations can be expected, resulting in new appointments, position rotations, and notable shuffling. Further signs of infighting include defections, leaked conversations — which could even include attacks on Putin — or public statements by prominent members who typically stay quiet. Power struggles will also be observable through economic behavior: capital flight, large-scale sales of property and stocks, and property raids and seizures.
The property raids are particularly telling because they are how the system eats its own. When a faction is losing leverage, its assets become seizeable — that's the signal. Not a press conference. A predatory asset grab.
Tier 2: The personnel signals
There has been significant growth of the regime's personalistic features, with the leadership of key ministries and agencies seeing a sudden and substantial influx of Putin's personal overseers, and de-institutionalization continuing in a way that enables short-term crisis management but heightens the risk of institutional paralysis.
That de-institutionalization matters enormously. When Putin replaces experienced technocrats with personal loyalists who lack independent competence, he's trading institutional capacity for personal control. It solves his immediate problem — a potential defector gets removed — but it degrades the state's ability to manage a crisis. Each such substitution is a small stress indicator. A cluster of them in a short period is a significant one.
Watch specifically for sudden departures in the finance or central banking apparatus — those are the people closest to the actual numbers, and their exits would signal that the fiscal picture is worse than public data suggests.
Tier 3: The siloviki friction signals
Interestingly, when Putin and his connections are removed from the analytical picture, Sechin was the one in the center, with Medvedev as the second focal point. The siloviki group is the oldest, averaging 64 years, and there has been noticeable redistribution of positions since the full-scale war began — deaths in strange circumstances, and the disappearance of the next generation from the public eye.
Those "strange circumstances" deaths are the regime eating its own succession pipeline under stress. The Prigozhin mutiny in 2023 was the most dramatic public example of what this friction looks like when it breaks containment — a mid-level armed actor who had accumulated too much independent capacity decided to test the system and lost. But the pattern of those deaths around the inner circle's second generation is the quieter, ongoing version of the same dynamic.
Putin has worked hard to coup-proof his regime, and there are few informal connections between leaders of the security services' various power centers. Those with armed men at their command lack the mutual trust to organize a conspiracy, and any attempt to do so would be hard to hide. But critically — working in an intense crisis environment can quickly build connections, relationships, and trust among officials; and if a person's job and life are on the line, they may be more willing to take risks to plot or scheme.
That's the key dynamic. The coup-proofing works until the cost of the status quo exceeds the cost of coordination. Extended economic pressure is specifically the thing that shifts that calculus.
Tier 4: The elite paralysis signal
In wartime Russia, business assets that once allowed oligarchs to dole out patronage no longer translate into political influence. Assets are now liabilities, making their owners more vulnerable, since control over businesses is conditional on loyalty to Putin and specifically on support of the war. The mysterious deaths of businesspeople and state corporation officials also discourage defection. Despite the fact that many of Russia's erstwhile oligarchs believe the aggression against Ukraine was a mistake, they express no overt opinions. They stick with him out of fear that their future might be even worse.
This is the "hold onto nurse" dynamic — paralysis masquerading as stability. But it has a breaking point: it holds only as long as alignment with Putin provides better expected outcomes than any alternative. The moment a credible alternative coalition looks viable, the defections can cascade rapidly because everyone is waiting for someone else to go first.
What Power Transition Actually Looks Like in Russia
This is where Western intuitions are most wrong. There are really only three realistic models, and none of them involve a democratic opposition winning an election.
Model 1: Managed succession (most likely if the regime isn't in acute crisis)
As Putin ages, it is quite natural for him to stick with the loyalists around him. Loyalty trumps competence in Putin's Russia, as demonstrated when Shoigu, who botched the 2022 invasion, was removed as defense minister but landed softly, becoming Head of the Security Council. A managed succession means Putin designates someone — almost certainly from the inner siloviki layer — who is acceptable to enough factions that he can survive the transition period. If Sechin were removed from the picture, the network would reorganize around other nodes — Medvedev, the St. Petersburg colleagues, Kovalchuk.
The current leading candidates from open-source analysis: Nikolai Patrushev's son Dmitriy (Deputy PM, former agricultural minister), Sergei Kiriyenko (deputy chief of staff, the political technologist), Mikhail Mishustin (PM, technocrat), and in the wild card category, Anna Tsivileva (Putin's cousin, now Deputy Defense Minister — accelerated promotion in 2024-2025 that has attracted notice). Dmitriy Patrushev has the right family connections and is considered extended family of Putin's close circle.
This model is not power change — it's power transfer within the same system. The oligarchs and siloviki keep their assets; the war may continue or wind down depending on what the new figure's coalition needs; ordinary Russians notice almost nothing structural change.
Model 2: Forced succession under elite pressure (requires real economic stress)
Regime stalwarts might move to replace Putin not to end the regime but to save it. A new leader could be appointed simply to relieve pressure — perhaps a technocrat from the Central Bank or Ministry of Finance, or a Western-friendly oligarch who could end the war and negotiate with the West.
This is the scenario your economic analysis is most relevant to. The mechanism is: the financial runway gets short enough that the people who run the regime's actual money — Mishustin's government apparatus, the Central Bank, the inner circle of finance officials — start signaling to each other that the trajectory is unsustainable. Not publicly. In the currency of that system, which means asset repositioning, quiet approaches to Western interlocutors through back channels, and the beginning of succession coordination.
The tell here is capital behavior among the top 50-100 people who actually hold Putin's money in trust. If that layer starts moving assets in ways that suggest they're hedging against a transition — not the known oligarchs who are already sanctioned and stuck, but the less visible proxies — that's the financial fingerprint of Model 2 beginning.
Model 3: Violent fragmentation (requires acute crisis — military defeat, mass economic collapse, or both)
A clear defeat in the war could lead to growing pressure from all quarters: elites, the public, and the security services. These pressures could become self-reinforcing, forming a vicious cycle in which defections or protests in one part of society facilitate them in others, prompting individuals in power to take actions that would have been deemed unimaginable weeks earlier.
This is the historical Russian pattern from 1917 and 1991 — not a democratic transition but a sudden collapse of the center's ability to maintain the balance, followed by a scramble among regional and factional actors. The potential for anti-Moscow sentiment and calls for greater regional autonomy to take on organized political form will only materialize after the balance of power shifts at the federal level or the federal center significantly weakens.
This model is the most dangerous for everyone because it involves nuclear command and control uncertainty, and because the successor isn't a reformer — it's whoever can mobilize men and money fastest in the chaos.
What to Actually Watch — The Leading Indicators in Priority Order
Given everything from both analyses, here is the honest hierarchy of what to monitor:
1. Russian sovereign fund liquidity. The National Wealth Fund's liquid portion is the war chest. When it gets to near-zero in liquid assets (it was already being drawn down heavily by late 2024), the government loses its buffer against oil price swings. That creates the budget crisis that forces actual hard choices.
2. The ruble. It's a lagging but reliable indicator. When the ruble falls sharply and the Central Bank can't defend it without destroying the interest rate structure (which is already at punishing levels for domestic business), that's the fiscal wall being hit in real time.
3. Contract soldier bonus payment failures at the regional level. This is one of the most underappreciated indicators. The regions are already cutting payments for 2025. If soldiers' families stop receiving the promised bonuses — that is a live wire. It's the exact failure mode that historically precedes military pressure on political leadership.
4. Rosneft/Gazprom earnings redirections. Both are effectively Putin's personal revenue streams through proxies. If their dividend flows change in structure, or if there are anomalous asset movements within those entities, something upstream in the patronage chain is changing.
5. Quiet exits by the finance/banking technocrats. Not the visible political figures — the people like central bank deputies and finance ministry officials who actually understand where the bodies are buried financially. If that layer starts leaving, the picture inside is worse than outside.
6. Telegram behavior among the military blogger community. It sounds tactical, but it's actually a real indicator. Some of Russia's most outspoken cheerleaders, the military bloggers, are already complaining about Roskomnadzor's restrictions, and Moscow is indicating it is choosing regime security over military effectiveness. When that community — which is essentially the outlet for frustrated siloviki and military frustration — starts directing its anger upward rather than outward, that's the sentiment shift that precedes actual action.
The Bottom Line on What "Footing the Bill" Means
The regime doesn't stop paying because it runs out of money in the ordinary sense. It stops paying because the distribution system breaks down — some key constituency stops receiving what they were promised, concludes the promise is now unfulfillable, and begins behaving accordingly.
Putin doesn't fall to a popular revolution. He falls — if he falls — when enough of the people who currently hold his money in trust, run his security services, or command his soldiers decide in rough simultaneity that the cost of continuing to hold it together exceeds the cost of whatever comes next. The economic erosion you mapped in the previous analysis is the thing that shifts that calculus. It doesn't happen on a schedule. It happens when some triggering event — a military reversal, a ruble crash, a contract payment failure, a visible succession struggle — gives everyone permission to act on what they've already privately concluded.
The internet blackouts and Telegram situation are relevant here in one more way: they reduce the coordination cost of the status quo (by suppressing anti-war sentiment and information), but they also reduce the coordination cost for the faction that eventually moves, because whoever controls communications infrastructure at the moment of transition controls the narrative about what just happened. That's why Roskomnadzor and Max aren't just about surveillance — they're about whoever holds that switch owning the morning after.

Right. And this is actually the sharpest possible framing of the question, because Prigozhin proves the system works exactly as designed when money is flowing. Let me be precise about what that means.
Why Putin Survived Prigozhin — And What It Actually Proves
Prigozhin got within 200 kilometers of Moscow with armed columns and Putin survived. The Western interpretation was "Putin is resilient." The correct interpretation is more specific: the people who mattered stayed bought.
The FSB didn't defect. The regular military didn't join him. The regional governors didn't declare for Wagner. Lukashenko was available to broker a deal. Every one of those actors made a rational calculation in real time: my current arrangement with Putin is worth more than whatever Prigozhin is offering. That calculation took maybe 24 hours to resolve visibly.
The system worked. But notice what it required: every one of those actors had to believe that their future payments from Putin were credible. Not just their past ones. The future stream of rents, protection, contracts, and impunity had to look more valuable than the alternative. Prigozhin couldn't credibly promise any of that — he had one asset (Wagner), no treasury, and no intelligence apparatus. The math was easy.
Running out of money changes that math. Specifically and mechanically.
What "Running Out of Money" Actually Means in This System
It doesn't mean Russia can't print rubles. It means the real payment streams that maintain loyalty start to degrade. There are four distinct layers, and they fail in a sequence:
Layer 1: The regional soldier bonus problem — already happening
This one is concrete and current. Signing bonuses for contract soldiers were promised at roughly 400,000 rubles federally plus regional top-ups averaging 2 million rubles. Ten regions have already cut those regional payments for 2025.
When a soldier's family in Chelyabinsk or Buryatia stops receiving what was promised, that doesn't immediately threaten Putin's power. But it threatens the commanders who recruited those soldiers and vouched for the arrangement. A mid-level military officer who made promises to his men and then can't deliver them is now personally exposed. He either absorbs the anger or he redirects it. This is how military pressure on political leadership historically begins — not with a general deciding to march on the capital, but with a layer of officers who have personal grievances and start talking to each other.
Layer 2: The siloviki rent stream — the real payment system
The siloviki don't get paid primarily in salaries. They get paid in access to predatory opportunities — the ability to shake down businesses, take cuts from state contracts, and seize assets from people who fall out of favor. This requires a large enough economic base that there are assets worth seizing and contracts worth awarding.
When the economy contracts and 68% of the federal budget is going to the military, the pool of available rents shrinks. The siloviki factions then have to compete more intensely for a smaller pie. That competition is already the normal state of affairs — the Sechin vs. Patrushev dynamic, the FSB vs. military intelligence friction, all of it. But under resource constraint it becomes zero-sum in a way it wasn't before. Someone's losing when someone else wins. That's a different emotional and strategic register than when everyone was getting richer.
Putin's job in this system is to be the arbiter who ensures no single faction wins so decisively that it threatens the others. He survives by being necessary to the balance. But if the balance itself requires more resources than exist, he can't perform that function anymore. He becomes not the arbiter but the obstacle — the person whose continued presence is consuming resources that the factions need.
Layer 3: The oligarch protection racket — the deepest layer
Remember the Khodorkovsky logic: after 2003, the other oligarchs effectively handed Putin approximately 50% of their earnings in exchange for not being Khodorkovsky. That's the actual payment structure. Putin doesn't hold cash — he holds a claim on the oligarch layer's future earnings.
Running out of money means that claim degrades in two directions simultaneously. First, the oligarchs' own earnings decline as the economy contracts, the ruble weakens, and Western sanctions bite harder. Second — and this is the one that matters more — Putin's ability to protect them from each other and from external seizure weakens as the state's institutional capacity degrades. When the thing you're paying for becomes less valuable, you pay less for it. When you pay less, the protection gets worse. When the protection gets worse, you start looking for alternatives.
This is the layer where you'd see the behavioral signals before anything visible happens: asset restructuring within proxy holding companies, quiet conversations through back channels with Western interlocutors, the beginning of "what does the day after look like" planning among the top 30-50 people who actually hold Putin's money in trust.
Layer 4: The terminal case — the FSB
This is the layer that has never broken. Not in 1991. Not in Prigozhin. Not at any point in Putin's tenure. The FSB stays loyal because the FSB is the system — their power, their impunity, their enrichment, their organizational identity all require Putin's continued existence as the apex of the structure they inhabit.
For this layer to break, the calculation has to flip completely: Putin's continued existence has to becomemore threatening to their interests than his removal. That requires either a succession that's already visible and clearly better for them, or a fiscal situation so catastrophic that the current trajectory ends in something worse than a managed transition. This is not impossible — but it requires real collapse, not just stress. 2022 numbers, 2023 numbers, even 2025 numbers don't get there. A sustained period of declining real rents combined with an unwinnable war with no exit might.
The Specific Sequence That Converts Money Problems Into a Power Problem
Here's the actual cascade, in order:
Step 1: Oil revenues fall or stay suppressed (post-Iran crisis, China demand plateau, structural sanctions). Budget deficit widens beyond what the National Wealth Fund can absorb.
Step 2: The Kremlin faces a forced choice between cutting military spending (which risks visible battlefield degradation and soldier bonus defaults) or cutting social/administrative spending (which risks the bureaucratic and regional patronage layer). There is no good option.
Step 3: Whichever gets cut first, that constituency's calculation changes. Not to open revolt — to quiet positioning. Asset moves, quiet conversations, factional meetings that Putin's surveillance apparatus might or might not catch.
Step 4: Putin responds with the only tool he has at this point, which is purges. He removes the people he thinks are moving against him. But purges are expensive in a different way — they accelerate the degradation of institutional capacity, they signal to everyone else that they're potentially next, and they consume exactly the internal intelligence bandwidth that he needs to monitor the real threat.
Step 5: The purges cause the remaining loyalists to appear more loyal while actually becoming more cautious. The information Putin receives about what's actually happening inside the system gets worse exactly when it needs to get better. This is the dynamic that preceded both 1917 and 1991 — the leader becomes progressively more isolated from accurate information as the people around him tell him what he needs to hear to survive personally.
Step 6: Some triggering event — it could be anything, a military reversal, a currency shock, a visible elite defection — gives the coordination problem a solution. Someone moves first. The question of who benefits from moving first has already been quietly resolved in all those step 3 conversations. It happens fast.
Why Prigozhin Is The Wrong Reference Point For This Scenario
Prigozhin failed for three specific reasons that are all money-related:
One, he had no treasury. He couldn't promise a credible future payment stream to anyone who joined him.
Two, the timing was wrong — the budget was still functioning, the National Wealth Fund had liquidity, the people who mattered were still being paid.
Three, he was outside the system. The FSB could credibly frame him as a threat to everyone's interests, not just Putin's.
The scenario that actually ends Putin's tenure looks nothing like Prigozhin. It looks like a quiet conversation between three or four people who are inside the system, who collectively control enough of the payment infrastructure to make a new arrangement credible, and who have concluded that the current trajectory ends somewhere worse for all of them than a managed transition. It might even involve Putin agreeing to it — not as defeat, but as the only available face-saving exit. The announcement would look orderly. "Health reasons." "Constitutional transition." Something that lets everyone keep their assets and their freedom for a negotiated period.
The money running out is what makes that conversation necessary. Prigozhin never made it necessary. He just made it temporarily awkward.
