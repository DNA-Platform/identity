Is there a story in the Bible where Jesus starve himself for like 40 days and then the devil tempt him with things
Claude
09:33 UTC, 4 June 2026
Yes, that's the story of the Temptation of Christ. It's in the Gospels — most fully in Matthew 4 and Luke 4, with a brief mention in Mark 1.
The setup: right after Jesus is baptized, he's led into the wilderness (the desert) where he fasts for 40 days and 40 nights. By the end he's famished, and that's when the devil shows up to tempt him. There are three temptations:
Turn stones into bread — the devil says if you're really the Son of God, make these stones into bread. Jesus refuses, saying man doesn't live by bread alone but by every word from God.
Throw yourself off the temple — the devil takes him to the top of the temple in Jerusalem and dares him to jump, claiming angels will catch him (even quoting scripture to justify it). Jesus answers that you shouldn't put God to the test.
Worship me and rule the world — the devil shows him all the kingdoms of the world and offers them in exchange for worship. Jesus tells him to get lost, that you worship God alone.
After Jesus passes all three, the devil leaves and angels come to attend to him.
The 40 days echo a lot of other "40" periods in the Bible (Israel's 40 years in the desert, Moses' 40 days on the mountain, the 40 days of flood rain), and it's traditionally the basis for the 40 days of Lent.

You're probably blending together a few real observations, each of which is true in a narrower form than "the devil isn't in the Bible."
The strongest candidate is the name "Lucifer." That name genuinely isn't in the Bible as a name for the devil. It comes from one line in Isaiah 14:12 ("How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer") — but "Lucifer" is just Latin for "morning star," and in context the passage is taunting the king of Babylon, not Satan. Identifying it with the devil is a later interpretive tradition, not what the text says.
Second, the Old Testament "satan" isn't the cosmic archenemy people picture. In Hebrew, ha-satan means "the accuser" or "the adversary" — more of a courtroom role than a proper name. In Job, for instance, "the satan" is a member of God's heavenly court, acting almost like a prosecuting attorney with God's permission. The fully developed Devil — God's great evil opponent — mostly takes shape in the Second Temple period and shows up clearly in the New Testament (like the wilderness story). So the Devil is in the Bible, but the version most people imagine developed over time.
Third, almost none of the popular imagery is biblical — the red guy with horns, the pitchfork, the pointy tail, ruling over hell. That's medieval art, Dante, and Milton, not scripture.
You might also be thinking of Hell, which is a similar story. The Bible doesn't have one unified "Hell." It uses several different words that get flattened into that one English term: Sheol (just the grave/underworld, not punishment), Hades (Greek realm of the dead), and Gehenna (an actual burning garbage valley outside Jerusalem, used as a metaphor for judgment). The eternal-fiery-torment picture is again heavily shaped by later tradition.
So the accurate version is something like: "the devil and hell as we picture them are largely post-biblical, and the name Lucifer isn't biblical at all" — rather than that they're absent entirely.

Was the snake in adam and eve the devil
Claude
09:42 UTC, 4 June 2026
Not according to the text of Genesis itself — that's the short answer, and it's another good example of the same pattern we've been talking about.
In Genesis 3, the tempter is simply called "the serpent," described as the most crafty of the wild animals God had made. The text never calls it Satan, the devil, or a fallen angel. Read on its own terms, it's a talking snake — a clever creature, not a cosmic figure of evil. The punishment it receives (crawling on its belly, eating dust, enmity with humans) reads as an etiology, an explanation for why snakes are the way they are, rather than the damnation of a supernatural being.
The identification of the serpent with Satan came much later. It develops in the Second Temple period and gets stated explicitly in the New Testament — Revelation 12:9 calls the dragon "that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan," which reaches back and reinterprets the Eden story. Paul gestures at it too (Romans 16:20). So by the time you get to the end of the Christian Bible, the connection is made openly, and Christian tradition has read it back into Genesis ever since.
So both things are true depending on which lens you use:
Genesis alone: a crafty animal, no devil mentioned.
The Bible as a whole / Christian tradition: yes, the serpent is identified as Satan.
It's the same shape as Lucifer and hell — the dramatic, unified figure is a later interpretive layer laid over an older text that's doing something more modest on its own.

You've put your finger on exactly the right tension, and it's a real one — not you being confused.
Let me sharpen what I'm actually claiming, because the distinction is narrow. I'm not saying "the Old Testament doesn't consider the serpent to be Satan" in the sense that it considered the question and ruled it out. I'm saying the question isn't on the table at all when Genesis is written. The author writes "the serpent," gives it no supernatural framing, and the developed figure of Satan as God's great adversary basically doesn't exist yet in Israelite thought at that stage. You can't identify the serpent with a character who hasn't been conceptualized. So it's less "Genesis says no" and more "Genesis isn't asking the question."
Then centuries pass. The figure of Satan develops. And the New Testament writers — looking back at Genesis through that now-developed lens — make the identification explicitly. Revelation calls the dragon "that ancient serpent... the devil and Satan," deliberately reaching back to Eden. That is in the Bible, stated plainly.
So here's the thing that resolves the confusion, I think: "Is the serpent Satan?" doesn't have one answer because the Bible isn't one document. It's a library written across roughly a thousand years, and its own understanding of these figures develops over that span. The New Testament authors are doing interpretation — reading the old text and drawing a connection the old text didn't draw itself. That's a theological move, and whether it's the "correct" reading depends on whether you think later revelation legitimately unveils what earlier texts meant all along (the traditional Christian view) or whether you're reading each text in its own historical moment (the scholarly/historical view).
Both can be stated as facts:
As a matter of what Genesis's author meant: the serpent is a serpent.
As a matter of what the New Testament claims: the serpent is Satan.
The apostles aren't lying or making something up — they're interpreting. The friction you're feeling is the gap between what a text originally meant and what a later tradition reads into it, and that gap is genuinely there. You're not missing something; you're noticing something real.
