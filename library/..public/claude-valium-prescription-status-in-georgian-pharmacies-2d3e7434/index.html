Short answer: No, they don't appear to share the same status. Alprazolam is explicitly named on Georgia's "subject to special control" list, while diazepam and lorazepam are not.
The Georgian Revenue Service's official customs brochure on prohibited and permitted medications names methadone, methylphenidate, methamphetamine, pregabalin, tianeptine, tramadol, fentanyl, tilidine, alprazolam, [and] flunitraze[pam] as substances subject to special control that cannot be imported without medical documentation. Diazepam (Valium) and lorazepam are not on that named list. The brochure references the underlying instrument: Decree No. 22/n of 22 January 2004 of the Minister of Labor, Health and Social Affairs of Georgia on the approval of the list on medicaments subject to special control.
The legal framework: Georgia's Law on Medicines splits pharmaceutical products into three groups for retail sales — the first group includes pharmaceutical products subject to special control; the second group includes prescription-only products; the third group includes products that may be sold without a prescription. Group 1 drugs in Georgia generally require a special (numbered/registered) prescription form and stricter dispensing controls than ordinary Rx (Group 2).
Confidence and caveats: I'm reasonably confident alprazolam sits in Group 1 based on the customs document and the underlying Decree 22/n citation. I have not directly verified the current full text of Decree 22/n on matsne.gov.ge (the listings have been amended multiple times), so I cannot tell you definitively which prescription form is required at the pharmacy counter or whether diazepam/lorazepam are formally Group 2 versus some other category. Real-world pharmacy practice in Georgia is also notoriously inconsistent — what's legally required and what an Aversi or PSP cashier actually asks for can diverge.
To verify: (1) Pull the current Georgian-language text of Decree 22/n from matsne.gov.ge for the authoritative current list; (2) ask directly at an Aversi or PSP pharmacy — they'll tell you immediately what form of prescription each requires; (3) if it matters for clinical planning, ask at AMC.

Adderall sits one rung higher on Georgia's list than lorazepam — same relative ordering as the US.
From the same Annex №1:
List II (ბრუნვისათვის შეზღუდული ნარკოტიკული საშუალებები / "narcotic drugs restricted for circulation") contains AMFETAMINE (amphetamine) at entry #7 and DEXAMFETAMINE (dexamphetamine) at entry #14 — these are Adderall's two active ingredients. Methylphenidate (Ritalin/Concerta) is also in List II at entry #41.
List III is where lorazepam, diazepam, and alprazolam sit (psychotropic substances).
So under Georgian law, Adderall components are more strictly controlled than lorazepam — List II > List III. List II keeps company with morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, methadone, cocaine, codeine, tramadol, the barbiturates, and flunitrazepam. List III is the benzodiazepine bucket plus zolpidem, ketamine, pregabalin, tianeptine, phenobarbital, and phenazepam.
US comparison: Yes, the relative ordering is the same:
Adderall: Georgia List II ↔ US Schedule II
Lorazepam: Georgia List III ↔ US Schedule IV
The structural difference is that Georgia uses four lists with a narcotic/psychotropic split inherited from the UN conventions (1961 Single Convention → Lists I & II as "narcotic drugs"; 1971 Psychotropic Convention → List III), while the US DEA uses one unified five-tier schedule (I–V) without that narcotic/psychotropic distinction. So Georgia's List I and II together roughly map to US Schedule I+II, and List III roughly maps to US Schedules III–IV.
Practical caveat (not legal advice): Even if your US Adderall prescription is current and documented, importing it requires the apostilled-prescription documentation chain in the rs.ge customs brochure (List II substances trigger the strictest import paperwork). Locally prescribed amphetamine stimulants are reportedly very hard to obtain in Georgia — ADHD stimulant treatment isn't a well-established pathway here. Worth raising directly with AMC if it's a clinical need for anyone in the family.

Roughly, yes — List I is the "no accepted medical use" tier. The Georgian title is "ბრუნვისათვის მკაცრად შეზღუდული ნარკოტიკული საშუალებები" — narcotic drugs strictly restricted for circulation. Maps closely to US Schedule I.
What's in it (sample, not exhaustive):
Classic hallucinogens: LSD/LYSERGIDE, DMT, PSILOCYBINE, PSILOCINE, MESCALINE, SALVIA DIVINORUM
Empathogens / amphetamine-type: MDMA, MDA (tenamfetamine), DOB, DOET, DMA, TMA, methcathinone (ephedrone), cathinone (raw)
Opioid extreme end: HEROIN, DESOMORPHINE (krokodil), 3-methylfentanyl and other fentanyl analogues, etorphine, etonitazene
Plants/naturals: CANNABIS (the plant + resin + oil + extracts), OPIUM + POPPY STRAW, COCA LEAF, KRATOM, KAVA, peyote-type (mescaline-containing)
Synthetic cannabinoids and "new psychoactive substances" — entries marked with an asterisk; the JWH series, CP 47,497 series, designer cathinones, 2C-x phenethylamines, etc.
Mapping to US: Most List I items correspond to US Schedule I. The handful that don't quite map cleanly: kratom (US: not federally scheduled), kava (US: legal supplement), salvia (US: not federally scheduled, varies by state). Georgia is more restrictive on those three than the US is.
Important nuance — your hypothesis isn't perfectly clean: "List I = unprescribed" is mostly right but not absolute. The Georgian law allows licensed research use of List I substances, and a few items (etorphine — used in veterinary practice for large animals, certain fentanyl analogues) have legitimate but extremely narrow uses. The accurate framing is "no general medical/prescribing use; circulation requires special state permit," not "categorically illegal everywhere."
Cannabis is the famous wrinkle. Cannabis is in List I, but Georgia's Constitutional Court decriminalized personal consumption of cannabis in 2018 (rulings #1/3/1282 in 2017 and #1/13/732 in 2018). Possession with intent to distribute, cultivation, and trafficking remain criminal. So the substance is List I on paper, but personal use isn't punished — a structural anomaly worth knowing about if it ever comes up.

Adderall is actually stricter than benzos in terms of prescription validity, not the same. The two sit on different forms entirely.
From the Georgian Pharmacists Association's reproduction of the ministerial decree governing first-group (special-control) prescriptions:
Form №1 (yellow blank) — narcotic drugs, i.e. List I and II. "Narcotic medicinal substances are prescribed on a yellow special prescription blank (Form 1)". Validity: "Prescription blank (Form 1) is valid for 5 days, including the day of issue". Issued in duplicate, kept by pharmacy for 5 years. This is what Adderall (amphetamine/dexamphetamine) requires — also methylphenidate, oxycodone, fentanyl, morphine, tramadol, etc.
Form №2 (green blank) — List III psychotropic. "Psychotropic and pharmaceutical products equated with special-control products are prescribed on a green special prescription blank (Form 2)". Validity: "The issued prescription (Form 2) is valid for 14 days, including the day of issue". Only one drug per blank. Lorazepam, diazepam, alprazolam are here.
Form №3 — Group 2 regular Rx. Per the April 2022 amendment, validity is "for chronic or long-term ongoing diseases, as well as for oral contraceptives, the validity period of the issued prescription is determined to be no more than 6 months".
So the actual ordering is: Form №1 (5 days, Adderall) → Form №2 (14 days, benzos) → Form №3 (up to 6 months, regular chronic Rx). The crowding you're seeing makes sense — anyone on a stimulant has to come back for a fresh script every five days, and benzo patients every two weeks, with only one drug per blank. That generates a lot of pharmacy traffic for a small population of patients.
One caveat: this rules document is from the Pharmacists Association reproducing the underlying decree (originally Min. of Health №150/n of July 21, 2003, with multiple amendments — I saw a 2024 amendment in the search results). The 5-day and 14-day numbers are durable across versions I've checked, but if you want the absolutely-current text, matsne.gov.ge for №150/n is authoritative. Worth confirming with whoever's writing the prescription.
