What you're describing is almost certainly photorejuvenation, also called an IPL photofacial (IPL = intense pulsed light). "Photo epithelia" looks like a voice-to-text artifact — there isn't a real cosmetic treatment by that name. The closest-sounding real term is "photoepilation," but that's laser hair removal, which doesn't match what you described.
Photorejuvenation works by delivering broad-spectrum pulses of light into the skin. The light energy is absorbed by pigments in the skin such as melanin and haemoglobin and converted into heat, which destroys unwanted pigment and stimulates the underlying connective tissue, promoting collagen production. So it does two things at once: it breaks down the pigmented spots and dilated capillaries that make skin look uneven and aged (sun spots, age spots, freckles, broken capillaries, redness from rosacea), and it triggers collagen remodeling, which over time firms the skin and softens fine lines.
The "looking younger" effect comes mostly from evening out the tone rather than any dramatic structural change — after IPL, you may look younger because your skin tone is more even. The pigmented spots typically darken first and then flake off over the following days, revealing more uniform skin underneath.
A few things worth knowing about the actual procedure: it's a course of treatments, not a single session — typically spaced about four weeks apart. It's best suited to lighter skin tones; for darker or tanned skin (Fitzpatrick IV–VI), IPL carries a real risk of causing hyperpigmentation rather than fixing it, so reputable practitioners screen for this. Most clinics also advise against doing it during summer or on recently tanned skin for the same reason.
Two related but distinct treatments people sometimes confuse with this:
Laser resurfacing (ablative or fractional, e.g. Fraxel) — actually removes or microscopically damages the surface layer to force regeneration. More aggressive, more downtime, bigger results.
Photoepilation — laser/IPL hair removal, same underlying tech but tuned for hair follicles.
If a Tbilisi cosmetologist is offering it, the Russian/Georgian term you'll likely see on the menu is фотоомоложение (photo-omolozhenie) — that's the standard local term for exactly this.

Duration. A typical course is three to six sessions spaced two to four weeks apart; after the course is complete, the visible improvements last roughly six to twelve months for most people, and longer with diligent sun protection — with proper sun protection and skincare, IPL results can last 1-2 years or longer. Most clinics recommend maintenance treatments once or twice per year. It's worth understanding why results fade though: the pigmented spots and broken capillaries that were destroyed don't actually come back. What happens is that ongoing sun exposure and aging produce new damage. So the "fade" is really just life continuing to deposit new defects on top of the cleared canvas.
Risk. When performed competently on the right skin type, IPL is one of the safer cosmetic procedures. Common temporary effects (redness, swelling, transient darkening of treated spots before they slough off) resolve in days. The serious risks — burns, scarring, hypo- or hyperpigmentation — are often linked to improper treatment settings, lack of post-care, or pre-existing skin conditions. Two structural risk factors matter most:
The first is skin tone. IPL targets melanin, so darker or recently tanned skin can't easily distinguish "spot to remove" from "skin you want to keep." The standard guidance is that IPL is appropriate for Fitzpatrick I–III (light to medium skin); patients in IV–VI face a meaningful risk of hyperpigmentation or burns. Anyone Mediterranean-pigmented, Central Asian, or recently sun-exposed should be screened carefully, and treatment is typically deferred during summer.
The second is operator skill — settings must be matched to skin type and condition, and Tbilisi cosmetology clinics vary widely in training and equipment quality.
On the long-horizon safety question (is repeated exposure cumulatively bad?): a 2017 review of 25+ years of IPL literature in PMC concluded that there has been no indication that repeated exposure or cumulative adverse events may lead to potential long-term risks. There's no signal connecting IPL to skin cancer in the published literature.
Diminishing returns / dependency. This is the most interesting question, and the answer cuts the opposite direction from what your phrasing suggests. There's no tolerance effect, no rebound, and no mechanism by which the skin becomes "dependent" on treatment in a pharmacological sense. If you stopped tomorrow, your skin would simply resume aging at its normal rate from wherever it currently is — not accelerated, not punished for having been treated.
In fact, the published evidence points the other way: cumulative treatment may produce increasing benefit rather than diminishing. The notable study here is Chang, Bitter et al. 2013 in the Journal of Investigative Dermatology. Skin biopsies from older women were taken before and after a course of BBL (a higher-end form of IPL). Skin aging was associated with altered expression of 2,265 coding and noncoding RNAs, of which 1,293 became more similar to their expression level in youthful skin after BBL treatment. A more recent 2025 real-world study in PMC followed long-term regular IPL therapy and concluded that regular treatment intervals optimize cumulative effects for facial rejuvenation.
Two important caveats on that, both worth keeping in mind given how the IPL/BBL industry markets itself:
First, the original Chang/Bitter study was a pilot — five older women, five younger controls. It's a real, peer-reviewed signal of molecular rejuvenation, but it's not the definitive proof the marketing copy makes it out to be. Med spas tend to quote it as if it settles the matter; it really established a hypothesis worth pursuing.
Second, "more is better" only holds at appropriate spacing. Crowding sessions together inside a single course, without letting skin recover, can damage rather than rejuvenate. The cumulative-benefit finding applies to the 2–3-times-per-year maintenance cadence, not to weekly treatments.
The practical upshot for Ana: treatment frequency is driven by ongoing aging and sun exposure, not by any property of the treatment itself making the skin need more of it. If she goes once and never returns, her skin will be in better shape than it would have been, indefinitely — it'll just continue to age normally from that improved baseline.
