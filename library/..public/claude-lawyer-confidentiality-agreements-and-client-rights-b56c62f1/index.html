You're raising a very valid concern. While I'm not a lawyer, I can share some general information about this issue.
The short answer: A lawyer generally cannot ethically require a client to sign a confidentiality agreement that would restrict the client's ability to:
Seek new legal representation
Report attorney misconduct to bar authorities
Communicate with courts or tribunals
Why this is problematic:
Attorney-client privilege already exists - Communications between attorney and client are already protected by attorney-client privilege. A separate confidentiality agreement isn't needed for this protection.
Ethical rules prohibit it - The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct (which most states have adopted in some form) generally prohibit lawyers from asking clients to agree to terms that would restrict the client's right to:
File complaints about the lawyer's conduct
Cooperate with disciplinary authorities
Seek other legal counsel
Against public policy - Courts have found such agreements void as against public policy when they impede a client's ability to protect their own interests or report misconduct.
Client's right to discharge counsel - Clients have a fundamental right to terminate their attorney at any time, and restrictions that make this difficult are generally unenforceable.
When confidentiality clauses ARE appropriate:
In settlement agreements (where both parties agree not to disclose settlement terms)
When protecting trade secrets or proprietary business information as part of the legal work
If you're being asked to sign such an agreement, this would be a red flag worth discussing with your local bar association or seeking a consultation with another attorney about the ethics of the situation.

Perfect - now I can give you a much better answer. Yes, there's a critical distinction:
1. ETHICS (affects the lawyer): California Business & Professions Code § 6090.5 makes it "cause for suspension, disbarment, or other discipline" for a lawyer to "solicit, agree, or seek agreement" that "misconduct or the terms of a settlement of a claim for misconduct shall not be reported to the State Bar."
2. LAW (affects enforceability of the contract itself):
Even more important - such agreements would likely be void as against public policy, meaning a court would refuse to enforce them regardless of whether they were signed. Here's why:
California courts have held that provisions in attorney-client agreements that interfere with fundamental client rights are "against public policy and void." For example, "provision in contingent fee contract providing that the client may not compromise the suit without the consent of his attorney is against public policy and void."
The key principle: "In all cases where contracts are claimed to be void as against public policy, it matters not that any particular contract is free from any taint of actual fraud, oppression, or corruption. The law looks to the general tendency of such contracts. The vice is in the very nature of the contract."
Contracts that restrict access to justice, legal remedies, or reporting of professional misconduct are generally void as against public policy because they:
Obstruct the administration of justice
Prevent oversight of the legal profession
Interfere with fundamental rights (to change counsel, to report misconduct, to access courts)
So to directly answer your question: A court would almost certainly refuse to enforce such an agreement - not just discipline the lawyer who sought it, but declare the contract itself void and unenforceable from the beginning.
