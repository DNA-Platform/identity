The most likely referent is Floyd Romesberg's semi-synthetic organism (SSO). His group engineered E. coli to carry an unnatural base pair in its genome and showed the cell replicates that expanded-alphabet DNA:
Malyshev et al., "A semi-synthetic organism with an expanded genetic alphabet," Nature 509:385 (2014). This was the first demonstration that DNA containing an unnatural base pair is replicated in vivo without cell growth being significantly affected, using imported hydrophobic triphosphates (d5SICS–dNaM) and an algal nucleotide transporter.
Zhang et al., "A semisynthetic organism engineered for the stable expansion of the genetic alphabet,"PNAS (2017), which fixed the early problem that the SSO grew poorly and lost the unnatural base pair, and reported an optimized organism able to store the increased information indefinitely. A companion 2017 Nature paper showed the cell can transcribe and translate the extra letters into protein bearing noncanonical amino acids.
One precision point worth flagging, since it changes what "not exactly DNA" means. The Romesberg SSO is still chemically DNA: same deoxyribose-phosphate backbone, just two extra letters (the hydrophobic pair, later dNaM–dTPT3). It's an expanded alphabet, not a new polymer.
If what you heard was about a genuinely different chemistry, the other candidate is XNA (xeno nucleic acids) from Philipp Holliger's lab, where the sugar itself is swapped (HNA, TNA, CeNA, etc.). But that's the opposite tradeoff: XNA replication systems with high fidelity were built for six XNAs, yet replacing DNA with XNA inside living cells remains particularly challenging. So XNA is "not DNA" but the replication is in vitro via engineered polymerases, not a living replicating cell.
So the phrase splits two ways: a living, replicating cell but still DNA-based (Romesberg), or truly alternative genetic chemistry but not yet in a living cell (Holliger). Which flavor did the thing you heard emphasize, the cell or the chemistry? That tells me which is the real referent.

You read it right, and the actual factsheet backs your intuition over the press framing.
On the division question: they did not coordinate the two, and they did not invent a new mitosis. Membrane splitting and genome partitioning are decoupled. The 90 kbp genome sits in the cell as seven separate plasmids (their overview says nine, one of a few small inconsistencies in a not-yet-reviewed release), and when the membrane pinches, the plasmids just partition by bulk. It is statistical inheritance, and it is leaky: after five generations only about 30% of daughter cells have the complete set of seven plasmids. They list genome distribution as an open problem and say better inheritance will need more sophisticated division mechanisms. So this is not a primitive stage of mitosis so much as the situation that predates the need for mitosis. Mitosis is the machinery evolution built precisely to make segregation faithful, especially for low-copy chromosomes. SpudCell keeps many copies and hopes each half gets some, which is why fidelity is poor.
The fission itself is purely mechanical: a surface protein (α-hemolysin, the same one used for feeding) crowds on the membrane until the mechanical stress makes it split, and cells that make more of it divide more efficiently. That couples expression level to division rate, not chromosome copies to daughters. So there is no sensor tying "genome is duplicated and apportioned" to "now split." The two processes run independently and the counting just is not there yet.
On the ribosomes, it is worse than cheating a little. The whole translation system is the PURE mix: 36 enzymes purified from E. coli, ribosomes included. Feeder liposomes then deliver more ribosomes, enzymes, and lipids, and division requires streptavidin and linker proteins added from outside. Because it cannot rebuild ribosomes, it runs 5 to 10 generations before the machinery degrades. To their credit the factsheet is candid about all this in a way CNN was not.
Which is your framing complaint, and I think you win it. "Nonliving chemical components" is doing rhetorical work. Nearly every functional part, the ribosomes, the 36 enzymes, the synthetases and tRNAs, is purified from a living organism. "Nonliving" holds only in the trivial sense that no single molecule is alive, which is equally true of your own cells. The claim that survives scrutiny is the one Adamala leads with elsewhere: chemically defined and bottom-up. Every component and its concentration is known, and it is assembled from individually purified parts rather than carved down from a living cell the way earlier minimal-cell work was. That top-down versus bottom-up distinction is the real novelty. "From scratch" and the origin-of-life connotation are the parts the result does not earn.

That axis (XNA, Romesberg's expanded alphabet) is orthogonal to what SpudCell did. I think you're right, and the missing piece is more specific than homeostasis in the narrow sense. What's absent is causal closure. The property "able to reproduce" is not manufactured by the cell, it's injected: the ribosomes, the 36 enzymes, the linker proteins all come from outside, and the code specifying the rest is transmitted with a leak. There's no closed loop in which the reproducer regenerates the thing that makes it a reproducer. That's the exact sense in which the daughter is not reliably the parent's type: the type-conferring machinery is not among the things the division produces.
This is close to von Neumann's criterion. A self-reproducing automaton has to pass on both the description and the constructor that reads it, so the offspring can run the same cycle. SpudCell fails on both arms. The constructor for its most important part, the ribosome, is never in the cell to begin with, it's supplied, and the description is only partly inherited: only about 30% of daughters carry the full plasmid set by generation five, and the system dies at 5 to 10 generations as the borrowed ribosomes degrade. So the complete type is not maintained, it's a decaying cascade. Rosen would call it a failure of closure to efficient causation, Maturana and Varela would say it isn't autopoietic but produced from outside. One observation, three vocabularies.
One place I'd tighten your statement, because it makes the conclusion sturdier, not weaker. "Both daughters having the ability to reproduce" is stronger than biology itself requires: asymmetric division, sterile worker castes, and terminally differentiated somatic cells all yield offspring that aren't reproducers, and we still call the lineage reproducing. The load-bearing criterion isn't "both daughters," it's fidelity high enough that the reproducing subpopulation persists instead of washing out. Stated that way SpudCell still fails, and more cleanly: 0.3 retention by generation five against a hard ceiling near ten generations is a subpopulation headed to zero.
