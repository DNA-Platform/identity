Good timing to ask — X Money went fully live for US Premium subscribers on June 26, just days ago, ending a long beta. A user sent $25 directly to Musk to test the P2P feature and he confirmed it publicly. So this is now a real, shipping product, not vaporware.
What it actually is right now. A conventional fiat banking-and-payments layer bolted onto the social graph. The feature set: peer-to-peer transfers, FDIC-insured deposits, a personalized metal Visa debit card stamped with your handle, 3% cashback, zero foreign transaction fees — plus a much-discussed 6% APY on balances. Notably, the initial rollout is exclusively fiat; crypto functionality is not included at launch. Regulatory footprint: money-transmitter licenses in 41 states plus DC, but not yet New York or Massachusetts, two of the largest markets and the binding constraint on scale.
Who it's competing for. Venmo is the obvious P2P comparison, but that undersells it. The 3% cashback and zero FX fees aim at credit cards; the 6% APY and "you won't need a bank account" framing target traditional banking and money-market funds directly. So the real competitive set is Venmo + Cash App (Block) + PayPal + Apple Payand the deposit base of conventional banks. The structural threat isn't product quality — the argument is that fintechs like Robinhood can't compete because X has ~550 million monthly users, and X doesn't need the best product, just a good-enough one inside the app people already live in. That's the whole thesis.
The social-graph-as-rail logic. This is the WeChat play, explicit and longstanding. Musk has said X could become "the biggest financial institution in the world" and that you should be able to "live your life on the X app." The distribution advantage is genuinely the asset here: payments embedded in the feed, identity already verified for monetization, network effects on who you can pay (anyone with a handle). The skeptic's counter, from payments veteran Richard Crone: "He promised this vision more than two years ago... this may be a day late and a dollar short" — and there's a real open question whether a platform built for conversation can be trusted as a reliable financial intermediary.
The 6% yield is the tell worth watching. With fed funds at 3.5–3.75%, it's unclear what mechanism funds a 6% yield — whether it's subsidized to drive adoption, generated by lending deposits, or something else, and that matters enormously for how regulators treat it. X Money isn't a stablecoin product, but it targets the exact same demand — people wanting better returns than their bank — through a different regulatory path. That arbitrage is the interesting part.
Crypto / stablecoins. No crypto at launch, but the door is deliberately left open. Musk hasn't explicitly said X Money will transact in crypto; he's amplified a third-party post speculating about "crypto integration" — characteristically non-committal. The substantive thread is the stablecoin one. The GENIUS Act (and the STABLE Act) dropped the prohibition on non-banking companies issuing stablecoins that earlier bills had — opening the door for Big Tech like Musk and Zuckerberg to issue their own. Warren has zeroed in on this "suspicious carveout" and pointedly asked whether Musk had a hand in it, given he was a special government employee while the GENIUS Act was being debated.
So to your direct question — is he supporting stablecoins or not? The honest read: not yet operationally, but the regulatory groundwork was laid (possibly with his involvement) for X to issue its own. Chamath's argument is that X Money will accelerate stablecoin adoption and route the profits to users rather than the platform. The fiat-first launch is best understood as a cleaner regulatory on-ramp — establish insured deposits and a licensed-transmitter narrative first, add the stablecoin rail once the legal cover is settled.
One connection to your own terrain: this is the same "everything app" archetype Telegram/TON is building, just inverted. Telegram went crypto-native first (TON as the rail, fiat as the afterthought); X is going fiat-first with a crypto option held in reserve. Two routes to WeChat — one starting from a payments license and a Visa partnership, one starting from a blockchain and a refusal to touch the banking system. Worth tracking which architecture the GENIUS Act regime actually advantages.
(Light flag: the 6% APY and "you won't need a bank account" claims are X's own marketing, and several details here — funding mechanism, NY approval timing, any stablecoin — remain unsettled and Musk-dependent, so I'd weight the present-tense facts above the forward promises.)

APY just means annual percentage yield — the total return on your money over a year, including compounding. Park $1,000 at 6% APY, leave it untouched, and you'd have ~$1,060 a year later. That part is mechanical and boring. (Small note: some coverage sloppily called it "APR" — American Banker did. APR excludes compounding, APY includes it. The part you're right to find confusing is where the 6% comes from, and the honest answer is that it can't come from anywhere safe. Here's the normal machinery: when you deposit cash at a bank, the bank doesn't let it sit there. It lends it out, or parks it at the Federal Reserve, or buys Treasuries — and it earns a return on that. It keeps most of the spread and passes a sliver to you. That's why a normal savings account pays under 1% and even a good high-yield account pays ~3.5–4% — always below the fed funds rate, because the bank pockets the difference as its margin.
X Money is offering 6%. The fed funds rate is around 3.75%. That's the whole anomaly in one line: they're promising to pay you more than the risk-free rate of return on cash. A bank parking your deposit at the Fed earns 3.75% and would lose money paying you 6%. So the 6% is not yield in the ordinary sense — it has to be coming from one of three places, and as a former quant you'll recognize this as the iron law that there's no free lunch above the risk-free rate:
Subsidy. X eats the gap out of its own pocket to buy users. The "yield" is really marketing spend wearing a finance costume — cheaper than the hundreds of millions fintechs normally burn on customer acquisition. Sustainable only as long as Musk chooses to keep paying, which is why nobody knows if 6% is permanent or a teaser that resets later. Even the early coverage flagged it's unclear whether the figure is permanent or promotional.
Risk. Someone — X, or its partner Cross River Bank — is lending or investing those deposits into something yielding well above 3.75%, which by definition means something riskier than cash. Higher return is higher risk; that's not a loophole, it's the definition. This is exactly what Warren pressed on: what risky investments are being used to fund 6% when the risk-free rate is 3.75%, and she noted Cross River had a prior FDIC enforcement action for unsafe practices.
It's not the safe deposit it looks like. If any of the yield runs through a crypto/stablecoin structure rather than an insured bank deposit, then the FDIC has been explicit that stablecoin balances aren't covered by deposit insurance or any government guarantee — so the "6%" could carry depeg or counterparty risk the headline number hides.
The thing to internalize: a deposit-like product paying meaningfully above the risk-free rate is telling you one of two things — we are paying for your presence (subsidy, temporary) or your money is not as safe as you think (risk, undisclosed). The product doesn't need to lock your money in; it needs to make moving it back out mildly annoying relative to leaving it.
