Let me explain the model first, then search for the psychiatrist.
Clinician-led group therapy (the Mental Hub type)
This is a different animal from the DBSA peer model. A licensed therapist — usually a psychologist or psychotherapist, sometimes with a psychiatrist involved — runs a small standing group, typically 6–10 people who meet weekly at a fixed time. It's paid, it's professionally facilitated, and there's a clinician steering the room rather than a peer with lived experience.
Within that, there are a few sub-models, and it matters which one a given group actually is:
Process group. The content is the room itself — how members relate, react, and get triggered by each other in real time. The therapist surfaces those dynamics as they happen. Powerful for interpersonal patterns; less about your specific diagnosis.
Psychoeducational / skills group. Structured curriculum — CBT or DBT skills, mood monitoring, relapse-prevention planning. Closer to a class with homework. For bipolar specifically, a well-run version teaches early-warning-sign tracking and routine stabilization, which is the part of bipolar management that actually moves outcomes.
Support group (clinician-facilitated). Closest to what you knew from DBSA in feel, but a professional holds the frame. Members share, the clinician keeps it safe and on-track.
The tradeoff versus the peer model you're used to: you lose the "everyone in this room has bipolar too" solidarity — a mixed clinical group may have depression, anxiety, trauma, not necessarily other bipolar folks — but you gain professional facilitation and, often, real skills content. It's worth asking Mental Hub two specific questions before committing: is the group diagnosis-mixed or mood-disorder-focused, and is it a process group or a skills group. Those two answers tell you exactly what you'd be walking into.
