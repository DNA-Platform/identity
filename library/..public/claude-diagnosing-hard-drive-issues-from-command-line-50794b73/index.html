Given the warm-restart issue on your machine, there's a real chance automatic repair itself is what's breaking you — each failed attempt reboots, and a warm reboot on your hardware can lose the drive. So the first move is to break the loop and cold-boot, not to repair anything.
1. Find the right drive letters first. In WinRE, C: is often the recovery partition, not Windows. Run:
text
diskpart
list vol
exit
Identify which volume has \Windows on it (usually the largest NTFS one). Call that W: below — substitute whatever letter it actually is. Also note the small FAT32 EFI System Partition; call that S:.
2. Break the auto-repair loop and try a cold boot.
text
bcdedit /store W:\Boot\BCD /set {default} recoveryenabled no
(If that path fails, try bcdedit /set {default} recoveryenabled no.) Then fully shut down — hold power 10 seconds — wait 30 seconds — cold boot. If Windows loads, the update just triggered a warm-restart cycle and your install is probably fine. Re-enable recovery later withrecoveryenabled yes.
3. If it still won't boot, check filesystem and system files (offline).
text
chkdsk W: /f
sfc /scannow /offbootdir=W:\ /offwindir=W:\Windows
dism /image:W:\ /cleanup-image /restorehealth /source:W:\Windows\WinSxS
DISM without internet needs /source: pointing at your own WinSxS.
4. If you suspect a recent update, list and remove it.
text
dism /image:W:\ /get-packages /format:table
Sort by install date in your head — look at packages installed right before this started. Then:
text
dism /image:W:\ /remove-package /packagename:Package_for_KBxxxxxxx~...
Use the full PackageIdentity string from the list.
5. If boot itself is broken (not just Windows), rebuild BCD. On UEFI (which your HP is), don't bother with bootrec /fixboot — it throws access denied. Use:
text
bcdboot W:\Windows /s S: /f UEFI
That rewrites the EFI boot files cleanly.
6. Last resort before reinstall: offline System Restore if you have restore points:
text
rstrui.exe /offline:W:\windows=active
My bet given your hardware history: step 2 alone fixes it. Start there. What does list vol show you?

Genuinely good instinct, and worth taking seriously. Let me give you the honest answer.
The short version: BitLocker didn't cause this, but it absolutely made it worse, and yes — given your hardware, you should probably disable it.
On whether BitLocker causes corruption: It doesn't increase the rate of filesystem corruption. Encryption happens in-line with normal writes — if a write completes, it's encrypted and valid; if a write is interrupted, you get the same metadata damage you'd get on an unencrypted drive. Your warm-restart dropout would have corrupted the filesystem either way. That part is on the T-FORCE drive's PCIe enumeration bug, not BitLocker.
But BitLocker dramatically amplifies the cost of any problem:
Every recovery operation requires the 48-digit key first. You saw this — chkdsk couldn't even read the volume until we unlocked it.
If the TPM loses trust in the boot chain (which happens any time firmware/boot files change), you get locked out and need the key.
If you ever lose the recovery key, the data is gone. Permanently. No recovery tool exists.
Hardware-level data recovery becomes essentially impossible. If the NVMe controller ever dies with data you need, a recovery shop can often pull data off unencrypted flash chips directly. With BitLocker, those chips are ciphertext — the key lives in the TPM which is soldered to a different board.
The boot chain becomes more fragile. Firmware update, BIOS setting change, even some driver updates can invalidate TPM measurements and force recovery-key prompts.
For your specific situation — a drive that physically disconnects on warm restart — BitLocker is a meaningful liability. Every dropout that corrupts metadata is now an event requiring the recovery key to even diagnose. That's the painful loop you've been in today.
My recommendation: once you're booted back into Windows, disable BitLocker on C:.
text
manage-bde -off C:
This starts decryption. Takes hours on 4 TB (runs in background, you can use the machine). When done, the drive is plain NTFS and future recovery events are vastly simpler.
Caveat I want to be straight about: you do lose real security. If the laptop is stolen, a thief with physical access can read everything on the drive — client files, saved passwords, browser sessions, Ana's legal documents, tax info, everything. Full-disk encryption exists for good reasons. Whether that matters depends on what you keep on the machine and your threat model (home office vs. carrying it through airports).
For a laptop you travel with internationally, I'd normally say keep encryption. For your specific hardware situation, the recovery cost outweighs the security benefit — but it's a real tradeoff, not free.
Separately, the real fix is the underlying hardware issue. Even without BitLocker, warm-restart dropouts will keep corrupting files mid-write.
