What is metformin, how does it work, how does it go with quetiapine and lamotrigine. What are effects and side effects? What is dosage
Claude
14:01 UTC, 13 August 2026
Metformin is a first-line drug for type 2 diabetes, from the biguanide class, in use since the 1950s. It's also commonly prescribed off-label to counter weight gain and metabolic effects of antipsychotics, which is likely why it comes up alongside quetiapine.
How it works. It lowers blood glucose without stimulating insulin release, mainly by reducing glucose production in the liver (suppressing gluconeogenesis, partly via AMPK activation and effects on mitochondrial complex I), improving insulin sensitivity in muscle, and modestly reducing intestinal glucose absorption. Because it doesn't push insulin, it essentially never causes hypoglycemia on its own.
With quetiapine. No direct pharmacokinetic interaction — different metabolic pathways (quetiapine is CYP3A4; metformin isn't hepatically metabolized at all, it's excreted unchanged by the kidney). The combination is in fact well studied: metformin is one of the better-evidenced treatments for antipsychotic-induced weight gain and insulin resistance, including with quetiapine specifically.
With lamotrigine. Also no clinically significant interaction. Both are partly cleared by renal organic cation transporters, and there's a theoretical minor overlap there, but it isn't considered meaningful at normal doses and kidney function.
Effects and side effects. Benefits: lower glucose, modest weight loss or weight neutrality, improved lipids. Side effects are mostly GI — nausea, diarrhea, cramping, metallic taste — common at the start and dose-dependent, usually settling within weeks. Long-term use can lower B12 (worth checking annually). The rare serious one is lactic acidosis, essentially confined to people with significant kidney impairment, so kidney function is checked before and during treatment.
Dosage. Typically started at 500 mg once daily with food, increased by 500 mg every 1–2 weeks as tolerated. Common maintenance is 1500–2000 mg/day split into two doses; maximum 2550–3000 mg/day depending on formulation. Extended-release (XR) versions are gentler on the gut and taken once daily. For antipsychotic-related metabolic effects, studies mostly used 750–2000 mg/day.
The starting dose and titration should come from the prescribing doctor, since kidney function and the specific indication matter here.

Will it help with nighttime hunger? Can it be combined with ozempic? What effect on weight gain does it realistically have?
Claude
14:03 UTC, 13 August 2026
Nighttime hunger. Possibly, but modestly. Metformin has a mild appetite-suppressing effect, partly through GLP-1 increases and effects on hypothalamic appetite signaling, and it blunts insulin resistance-driven hunger cycles (the reactive hunger that follows glucose swings). If the nighttime hunger is driven by quetiapine — which is a strong, histamine-mediated appetite stimulant, notoriously at night since it's taken in the evening — metformin helps at the metabolic level but doesn't block that H1-driven craving directly. People on quetiapine plus metformin often report hunger is dampened but not eliminated.
With Ozempic (semaglutide). Yes, this is a standard and common combination — they're routinely co-prescribed in diabetes and increasingly in weight management. No pharmacokinetic interaction. Mechanisms are complementary: metformin works on the liver and insulin sensitivity, semaglutide on appetite, gastric emptying, and insulin secretion. The main practical issue is additive GI side effects (nausea, diarrhea), especially during titration of either drug. Semaglutide is much the stronger appetite suppressant of the two; for nighttime hunger specifically it would do far more than metformin.
Realistic weight effect. Modest. In diabetes trials, metformin alone gives roughly 2–3 kg loss over 6–12 months, and some people just stay weight-neutral. For antipsychotic-induced weight gain the meta-analyses show about 3–4 kg advantage over placebo, somewhat better when started early or preventively rather than after significant gain. Compare semaglutide at ~10–15% of body weight in the weight-loss trials. So: metformin is a brake, semaglutide is a reversal.

Okay how about quetiapine to aripiprazole gor managing prediabetes by controlling metabolic load and hinger?
Claude
14:31 UTC, 13 August 2026
Switching from quetiapine to aripiprazole is one of the standard, guideline-supported strategies for exactly this problem — it's probably the best-evidenced antipsychotic switch for metabolic reasons.
Why it makes sense metabolically. Aripiprazole sits at the favorable end of the antipsychotic metabolic spectrum. It has minimal H1 histamine affinity (that's the main driver of quetiapine's hunger, especially the nighttime cravings), and it's a partial dopamine agonist rather than a full antagonist, with little intrinsic effect on insulin sensitivity, lipids, or glucose. Switch studies (notably the CAMP trial, quetiapine/olanzapine/risperidone → aripiprazole) showed weight loss of roughly 2–4 kg over several months, improved triglycerides and cholesterol, and improvement in glycemic measures — so for prediabetes specifically it addresses the cause rather than just braking it the way metformin does. Appetite reduction is often noticeable within weeks because the H1 blockade simply goes away.
The trade-offs. They're real and worth knowing going in. Aripiprazole is activating rather than sedating — akathisia (inner restlessness) is its signature side effect, in maybe 10–25% early on, and people switching from a sedating drug like quetiapine sometimes experience the loss of sedation as insomnia or agitation. If quetiapine was doing work as a sleep aid or for anxiety, that function disappears and may need replacing. There's also a rare but documented association with impulse-control effects (gambling, compulsive behaviors) worth being aware of. And efficacy isn't interchangeable for every indication: for bipolar depression in particular (relevant given the lamotrigine), quetiapine has strong evidence while aripiprazole's is weak — it's mainly effective on the manic/mixed and maintenance side. So the metabolic gain can come at the cost of the specific thing quetiapine was treating.
How switches are done. Usually a cross-taper — aripiprazole started while quetiapine is gradually reduced over weeks, not an abrupt swap, partly because abrupt quetiapine discontinuation causes rebound insomnia and cholinergic rebound. Aripiprazole's very long half-life (~75 hours) makes the introduction smooth, but it also means side effects like akathisia take a while to fully declare themselves.
The short version: for metabolic load and hunger, the switch is genuinely effective and better-targeted than adding metformin on top of quetiapine. The open question is whether aripiprazole covers the psychiatric indication quetiapine was serving — that's the axis the prescriber will weigh.

Right — that changes the calculus in your favor. Aripiprazole's evidence base is strongest exactly where you need it: acute mania and maintenance against manic relapse. The weak spot (bipolar depression) isn't your problem.
And yes, your description of the mechanism is essentially correct. As a partial agonist at D2, aripiprazole acts as a buffer on dopamine tone rather than a blocker: where dopamine signaling is excessive, it competes with dopamine and caps transmission at its own intrinsic activity (~25-30% of full activation), and where tone is low it provides that same floor. So it clamps the system toward a middle level from both directions — functionally a dopamine stabilizer, which is exactly the property you want against manic dopaminergic surges without the full D2 shutdown that produces the classic antipsychotic deficits. Quetiapine, by contrast, is a plain antagonist doing its antimanic work by blockade plus sedation.
Morning dosing is the standard recommendation for exactly the reason you give — it's activating, and taken in the morning the stimulation works with your circadian rhythm instead of against sleep. The main thing to watch during the cross-taper is the loss of quetiapine's sedation: if it was carrying your sleep, that needs a plan, because sleep disruption is itself a manic trigger. That's the one place where the switch, done carelessly, could interact with the mania risk rather than help it.

The arithmetic works, but the endpoint may be higher than needed. Standard dose-equivalence tables put quetiapine 400 mg at roughly aripiprazole 15 mg, not 20 (both are the WHO defined daily doses, and empirical equivalence studies land in the same place). So a cleaner mapping is 5 mg up / ~130 mg down, ending at 15 mg — with 20 available as headroom if symptoms need it. In practice the taper isn't held to strict equivalence anyway: aripiprazole's 75-hour half-life means it accumulates for two weeks after each step, so many prescribers go up faster than down, letting aripiprazole reach steady state before pulling the last of the quetiapine. The last 50–100 mg of quetiapine is the step people trip on — that's where the rebound insomnia lives.
On newer agents: the interesting one for your case is cariprazine (brand Reagila in Europe, Vraylar in the US). It's the closest thing to a next-generation aripiprazole — also a D2 partial agonist, but with preferential D3 affinity — approved for mania, and metabolically it's in the same favorable tier as aripiprazole: minimal weight gain, negligible H1 affinity, essentially clean on glucose and lipids. Some head-to-head network meta-analyses rank it among the very best antipsychotics for metabolic neutrality. Its drawback profile is also aripiprazole-like: akathisia and activation, possibly a bit more of it. It's made by Gedeon Richter (Hungary), which distributes heavily across Eastern Europe and the CIS, so Reagila is plausibly in Georgian pharmacies — worth checking mdc.ge for რეაგილა/კარიპრაზინი before assuming.
The others you'll read about don't fit your indication: lurasidone (Latuda) and lumateperone (Caplyta) are metabolically clean but approved for bipolar depression, not mania; brexpiprazole isn't approved for bipolar at all. Nothing yet beats the aripiprazole/cariprazine pair for "antimanic + metabolically neutral." So the realistic choice in Tbilisi is aripiprazole (cheap, generic, everywhere) versus cariprazine (newer, pricier, similar profile, D3 angle) — and aripiprazole is the sensible first move given its track record and cost, with cariprazine as the fallback if akathisia or partial response becomes the problem.

Claude
14:49 UTC, 13 August 2026
How you'd choose. There's no test that predicts which will suit you — no genotype or biomarker distinguishes aripiprazole responders from cariprazine responders. The honest answer is that the choice is made on priors and then corrected by experience. The priors favor aripiprazole first: decades of track record in mania and maintenance, cheap generics, every pharmacy stocks it, and its failure modes are well mapped. Cariprazine's distinguishing claim — preferential D3 binding, with some evidence for better effect on motivation/negative-symptom-like features — matters more in schizophrenia than in your picture. Head-to-head they've never been directly compared in mania; network meta-analyses put their antimanic efficacy in the same tier. So the standard logic is: aripiprazole first because it's the known quantity, cariprazine as the switch if you get intolerable akathisia (its profiles differ slightly per person despite the class similarity) or an inadequate response. "How do we know" = we don't, in advance; the first drug is a well-chosen bet and the information comes from your response to it. That's genuinely how this class of decision works, not a gap in your prescriber's knowledge.
Timing. Yes, morning — for both drugs. You're right that with a 75-hour half-life (and cariprazine's active metabolite runs 1–3 weeks) the plasma level barely ripples across a day, so the pharmacokinetics are indifferent to timing. But the subjective activation isn't flat: there's a peak-related alerting effect in the hours after the dose, and taking it in the morning keeps that away from sleep onset. With your insomnia already untreated, that margin is worth having. Consistency matters more than clock time — same time daily.
The insomnia piece is the real flank to watch. Quetiapine, whatever else it wasn't doing, was providing 400 mg of nightly H1/adrenergic sedation, and coming off it produces rebound insomnia on top of a baseline problem — right after losing lorazepam too. Since sleep loss is the classic manic trigger, the taper's sleep plan matters as much as the dose mapping. The habit-based work (CBT-I territory) is the right long-term structure, but it's slow-acting, and the taper will stress it. Worth raising with the prescriber whether you want a bridge for the taper window — the usual candidates are trazodone (widely available in Georgia), mirtazapine at low dose (sedating but H1-mediated, so it carries some appetite cost), or melatonin/ramelteon-type agents which are metabolically clean. That's a decision to make before the last 100 mg of quetiapine comes off, not after the first bad week.

I don’t see how two medication’s that are almost the exact same class have not been compared head-to-head
Claude
14:50 UTC, 13 August 2026
It's a fair reaction, and the explanation is mundane rather than scientific: nobody has an incentive to fund the trial.
Head-to-head antipsychotic trials are almost always industry-funded, and a direct comparison is a bet the sponsor can lose. Gedeon Richter (cariprazine's maker) gains nothing by risking a result of "no better than generic aripiprazole, at 20× the price" — and the makers of aripiprazole have no reason to fund anything, since it's off patent and the generic manufacturers don't run trials at all. So cariprazine's registration trials were run against placebo, sometimes with an active reference arm (to show the trial could detect an effect) but explicitly not powered or designed for comparison. This is the standard structure across the whole drug class: olanzapine vs. risperidone, quetiapine vs. everything — most of what exists came either from a sponsor confident of winning or from rare public funding (CATIE, EUFEST), and there's been no CATIE-scale public trial since the 2000s, before cariprazine existed.
The gap gets papered over with network meta-analysis — inferring A vs. B through their shared placebo comparisons (Cipriani/Leucht-style). That's the source of "same efficacy tier," and it's genuinely weaker evidence than a direct trial: it assumes trial populations are exchangeable, and mania trial populations have drifted over the decades. So the honest status is: no direct comparison exists, the indirect evidence says they're comparable antimanics, and the confidence interval on that is wider than anyone would like. The economics of generic-vs-branded ensures the trial that would settle it will probably never be run.

What about mania induced by the aripiprazole? If it’s stimulating, it must have that effect on some people. It’s supposed to regulate overall dopamine tone, but does it at least have a rebound stimulating effect for some?
Claude
14:54 UTC, 13 August 2026
Yes, this is documented — there's a real case-report literature on aripiprazole-induced or -exacerbated mania, including cases where it emerged shortly after starting the drug or after a dose increase, in both bipolar and schizophrenia patients. It's rare (case reports and small series against millions of exposures, so no reliable incidence number), but it's not theoretical.
The proposed mechanism is exactly the one your question implies. The partial-agonist "buffer" story assumes the drug is competing against high dopamine tone, where its ~25–30% intrinsic activity acts as a cap. But when it arrives in a system where tone is low, or where postsynaptic receptors have been upregulated and sensitized by years of full antagonist blockade — which chronic quetiapine plausibly produces — that same intrinsic activity is a net stimulant. The reported cases cluster suggestively around switches from full antagonists, which is your exact situation: sensitized receptors meet a partial agonist, and 30% intrinsic activity on supersensitive receptors can transiently overshoot. Some authors frame these switch-emergent episodes as a form of supersensitivity or withdrawal-emergent mania, with aripiprazole's agonism as the spark, and it's one of the arguments for slow cross-tapers rather than abrupt swaps — keeping some antagonist on board while the receptor population re-normalizes.
Separate from frank mania, the milder version — activation, restlessness, insomnia, agitation in the first weeks — is common and usually settles. The clinical distinction to hold onto during the switch: akathisia and activation are motor/arousal phenomena, while emergent mania has the cognitive signature (accelerated thought, decreased need for sleep experienced as not needing it, expansive content). You're better positioned than most to tell those apart from the inside, but the taper weeks are the window where monitoring matters most, and it's another reason the cross-taper is slow: the quetiapine still on board during the overlap is doing antimanic work while the aripiprazole finds its level.

Mostly yes, with one mechanistic wrinkle worth knowing.
The wrinkle: at therapeutic doses aripiprazole occupies 90%+ of D2 receptors and binds tightly, while quetiapine binds D2 loosely and dissociates fast. So when both are present, aripiprazole wins the receptor — the added 100 mg of quetiapine can't displace it, and the D2-mediated part of quetiapine's antimanic action is largely blocked. This is a real, documented interaction; it's the same reason adding aripiprazole to other antipsychotics sometimes worsens people — it takes over the receptor and substitutes its 30% intrinsic activity for full blockade.
What still works, though, is everything quetiapine does off D2: the H1/adrenergic sedation, the slowing, the sleep pressure. And in practice that's a large share of what PRN quetiapine does for an escalating night anyway — sleep itself is antimanic, arguably the most reliable circuit-breaker there is. So your existing move retains most of its practical value: it will still sedate you, still get you to bed, still put a brake on the behavioral spiral. What it won't do during the aripiprazole era is add D2 blockade on top — the receptor-level antimanic contribution is spent.
The practical upshot: PRN quetiapine remains a reasonable first response for a mild wobble during stabilization, on the sedation channel. But if something more than a wobble develops — the cognitive signature, sleep need genuinely dropping — the correct lever is the aripiprazole dose itself (15 → 20 → 30 covers considerable antimanic range, since higher doses shift it toward net antagonism) or slowing the taper so more scheduled quetiapine stays on board, rather than stacking PRN doses that are fighting for a receptor they can't win. That's a conversation to have with the prescriber in advance, so the escalation path is agreed before it's needed.

Your logic is sound, and in fact that's exactly what's done when the stakes are high: in acute mania, aripiprazole is started at 15 mg (sometimes pushed to 30 within days) with no slow ramp at all. In your situation — where the concern is precisely coverage during the handoff — the asymmetry flips, and the fast schedule is the defensible one.
One pharmacological detail sharpens this further: D2 occupancy saturates early. Around 10 mg you're already near ~85–90%, and 15 vs. 30 mg barely moves occupancy — the dose-response above 15 is shallow. So "high dose quickly" really means "get to 10–15 mg without dawdling," not "race to 30." The protection you're buying comes from occupancy, and occupancy is nearly maxed at moderate doses; what a fast schedule mainly compresses is the time spent in the vulnerable accumulation window, which is the variable that actually matters.
So the coherent aggressive version of your plan: start at 10, or 5 for a few days then 10–15, reaching a therapeutic dose within the first week or two, while quetiapine holds steady; then taper quetiapine on comfort. The cost is a higher chance of early akathisia and activation — which, per the earlier point, you'd want to be able to distinguish from emergent mania, since the fast schedule makes both more likely in the same window. That's the actual trade the prescriber is weighing when they default to slow: not efficacy, just whose side effects arrive first and how legible they'll be.

Yes — you've drawn it correctly, and it's the sharpest version of the argument for the fast schedule. The low-dose overlap is the worst configuration of the three drugs' properties.
Here's the arithmetic of it. Affinity governs who wins the receptors that get occupied; dose governs how many get occupied. That's the right priority — an endocrinologist who doesn't know the drug class can't tell iatrogenic metabolic dysfunction from ordinary metabolic syndrome, and the management differs (removing the driver versus managing the condition).
Practical routes in Tbilisi, roughly in order of likely payoff:
The international-facing private clinics. These are where English-fluent, Western-trained specialists cluster. Worth calling: Aversi Clinic, New Hospitals (the PSP group's clinic), MediClubGeorgia (long-standing expat reputation, they explicitly market English service), Chachava Clinic, and Todua Clinic. When you call, ask specifically whether the endocrinologist speaks English at clinical level rather than whether "English is available" — the latter often means a receptionist.
The expat channel. The Tbilisi expat Facebook groups and the "Expats in Georgia" communities generate constant doctor recommendations, and English-speaking specialists get named repeatedly. This is genuinely how most foreign residents find their doctors there, and it's faster than cold-calling clinics.
Ask your psychiatrist for the referral. If they treat expats or read English literature, they likely already know which endocrinologist can handle antipsychotic-associated metabolic issues — this is a common enough problem in psychiatry that the referral pathway usually exists. This is probably the highest-yield single move.
Fallback worth considering: telemedicine with an endocrinologist abroad for the interpretation and plan, using local labs for the numbers. Labs in Tbilisi are cheap, fast, and reliable, and HbA1c/fasting glucose/lipids/fasting insulin don't require the ordering physician to be local. That decouples the expertise from the geography, if the search stalls.

That's the better framing: you don't need an English-speaking endocrinologist, you need one who's treated antipsychotic-associated metabolic dysfunction before, and psychiatrists accumulate exactly that list because they keep needing to send people. Your psychiatrist has likely already found the one endocrinologist in Tbilisi who doesn't need the drug class explained.
The intermediary idea is worth asking for directly. A psychiatrist-to-endocrinologist handoff, even just a note saying "decade of quetiapine, switching to aripiprazole for metabolic reasons, please advise on glycemic management during the transition," changes the consultation entirely — it arrives framed as iatrogenic and time-varying rather than as ordinary prediabetes, which is the distinction the first endocrinologist couldn't make. It also means the two of them are working from the same model of what's changing and when.
Worth having ready for that appointment: the timeline of the switch, so the endocrinologist knows the exposure is being removed and can decide whether to treat now or measure again after the taper. That's a real fork — if the driver is coming out, a repeat HbA1c at three months post-switch may look substantially different, and it affects whether metformin gets started now or held.
