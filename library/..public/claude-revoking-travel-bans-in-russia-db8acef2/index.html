Your instinct here is sound, and the thing you're describing isn't a hope — it's a defined legal product. Let me lay out where it's real, and where the wall actually is, because the wall isn't where you'd expect.
The product exists. Under the EU Qualification Directive, a person granted protection in a member state gets both a residence permit and a travel document — and for beneficiaries of subsidiary protection, the travel document is issued precisely when they're unable to obtain a national passport. Refugees get the Geneva Convention travel document outright; subsidiary-protection beneficiaries get a travel document under specific conditions, the key one being inability to get a passport from the home state. That is Dima's exact situation. And courts have leaned protective on this specific point: the Italian Council of State held such travel documents must be granted without even requiring proof that the origin country won't issue a passport, noting that it's often the origin state itself that blocks the passport — and the ECtHR condemned Lithuania for refusing a travel document on formalistic grounds. So a country recognizing the injustice and giving Dima status plus documents to function as a minor is squarely within existing law, not a long shot.
The wall is getting in to ask. Protection is territorial — to apply for asylum you must reach the territory of the country you're asking, and crossing an EU border lawfully is close to impossible for someone in that position; it's a catch-22. And there's no shortcut: the CJEU held in X and X v Belgium that member states are not obliged under EU law to issue a humanitarian visa to let someone enter and claim asylum — a state may issue one at its sovereign discretion, but no court, charter, or convention can compel it. So the binding constraint isn't whether the EU would grant protection. It's that an undocumented child can't board a plane to get there, and no one is required to hand him the document that would let him.
