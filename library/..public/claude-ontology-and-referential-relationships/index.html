Ontology, classically, is the branch of metaphysics that studies being qua being — Aristotle's formulation in Book Γ of the Metaphysics. The point of the qua is to distinguish it from the special sciences: biology studies living beings, physics studies physical beings, but ontology studies what it is to be anything at all. The name is just Greek: ontos (being) + logos (account).
A standard philosophy class structures the subject around three questions, ascending in abstraction:
What exists? — the first-order inventory. Are there numbers, properties, possible worlds, minds, fictional characters?
What categories of being are there? — how existence articulates into kinds. Aristotle's ten categories (substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, affection) is the locus classicus; the modern version is the question whether reality has "natural joints."
What does "to be" mean? — the meta-ontological question. Is existence a property? A quantifier? Univocal or analogical?
Your abstraction — what exists and what type of existence things have — tracks the first two levels cleanly. The third is where traditions divide.
Two traditions are usually contrasted. The logical tradition (Frege, Russell, Quine) denies that existence is a property of things at all; it is the range of the existential quantifier. Quine's slogan — "to be is to be the value of a bound variable" — makes a theory's ontology identical to what it quantifies over, its ontological commitment. The categorial tradition (Aristotle through the scholastics, and in a radicalized form Heidegger) treats ontology as the map of kinds of being. Heidegger sharpens this with the ontological difference: the distinction between Being (the manner or fact of existing) and beings (things that exist), arguing the tradition forgets the former while cataloguing the latter. The CS/AI sense you're familiar with (Gruber: "a specification of a conceptualization") is essentially the Quinean tradition engineered — types, relations, instances, formally specified.
On "expressed": a theory expresses its ontology through what it takes as primitive, what its vocabulary names, what it quantifies over, and what structural axioms those primitives must satisfy. The choice of primitive is itself an ontological commitment — choosing membership, or reference, or proposition, or event as basic is already a claim about the fundamental structure of what is.

There's a tradition behind this — structural approaches to ontology, where entities are what they are in virtue of the relations they stand in rather than in virtue of intrinsic categorial features. SRT's version specializes the relational primitive to reference specifically. Some classroom mappings:
Use/mention is the cleanest case. "Paris" (the word) vs. Paris (the city) — classical philosophy teaches this as a distinction you have to be careful about to avoid errors. SRT would say the distinction is a referential relationship: the word stands in the symbolization relation to the city. Both are referents; what distinguishes them ontologically is that relation, not two different modes of being.
Type/token (Peirce). Three tokens of "dog," one type. Classical accounts strain over what the type is — an abstract object, a set of tokens, a pattern? SRT: the type is a referent, each token is a referent, and the relation between them is representational. The ontological distinction becomes a specific referential structure rather than a difference in categorial status.
Sense and reference (Frege). "Morning star" and "evening star" have the same reference but different senses. Frege posited a separate "third realm" for senses. SRT dissolves that by placing symbols and their referential paths inside the same ontology — no separate realm, just different referential structures ending at the same literal.
Universal/particular. The realism/nominalism debate is about whether redness exists over and above particular red things. SRT doesn't settle the debate; it provides the common referential frame within which different ontologies partition the referents differently. Realist and nominalist positions become different axiomatic commitments about what referential relationships hold.
The pattern worth naming: classical ontology often postulates different modes of being on either side of a distinction — substance vs. accident, universal vs. particular, abstract vs. concrete, symbol vs. thing. The structural move, of which SRT is a specific case, is that the distinctions are carried by relational structure rather than by differences in mode of being. One mode — being a referent — and many relational distinctions.
Where it needs testing: whether referential relationships are rich enough to capture all the classically recognized distinctions. Modal ones — actual vs. possible — might resist, because being possibly-referred-to isn't obviously the same as standing in a referential relation in SRT's sense. That's a real open question worth flagging, not a hedge. But for the symbol/referent family and its relatives, the mapping isn't just a fit — it's what the theory already says.
