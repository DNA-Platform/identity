The shape change:
Leader-based, single-chain: Validators take turns proposing blocks for designated slots, with slots grouped into "leader windows" of size L. The old Catchain was a DAG-ish BFT with multi-proposer dynamics; Simplex collapses that to one leader per slot producing "a single, ever-growing chain of finalized blocks".
Three vote types, ≥2/3 stake quorums: Notarization (block is valid candidate), Finalization (block is committed irrevocably), and Skip (move past a dead/malicious leader). An honest validator never both finalizes and skips the same slot — that constraint is the load-bearing invariant for safety.
Provable safety, probabilistic liveness: Safety holds unconditionally regardless of adversary or network. Liveness only holds with prob=1 under their network model. This is the standard BFT split, but the proofs are spelled out inline in the spec.
Streaming layer, not polling: pushes notarization/finalization votes as they happen rather than waiting on round structure. This is where the 400ms slot / ~600ms finality comes from.
Secondary scope includes QUIC + TwoStep broadcast: network-layer rewrites accompanied the consensus rewrite — the validator transport itself was upgraded.

Quick framing first — the question slightly conflates two layers, and the distinction matters for your N=S thread.
Sybil layer (who gets to vote) vs consensus layer (how they agree). Sunny King's Peercoin (2012) invented Proof-of-Stake as a Sybil-resistance mechanism — it answers "who is allowed to be a validator." Simplex is a Byzantine Agreement protocol — it answers "given a set of validators, how do they agree on block order." These are stacked, not substitutes. TON is still PoS at the Sybil layer (you stake TON to validate). What changed is the BFT protocol running among the staked validators. So Simplex doesn't supersede Sunny King; it supersedes Nikolai's original Catchain.
What Simplex is. "Simplex Consensus: A Simple and Fast Consensus Protocol," Benjamin Y. Chan (Cornell) and Rafael Pass (Tel-Aviv University and Cornell Tech), June 2023, published at TCC 2023. The pitch is in the name: a partial-synchrony BFT protocol tolerating f < n/3 Byzantine faults, designed to be as simple to describe and prove as anything in the literature while matching or beating state-of-the-art latency. Three vote types (Notarize, Skip, Finalize), single chain, leader rotation per slot/window. The contribution wasn't a new cryptographic primitive — it was a radical simplification of the BFT space, with cleaner security proofs than HotStuff, Tendermint, or PBFT.
Important: this is not a TON-specific choice. Simplex is becoming the new gold standard. It's been incorporated by the Commonware library and (with modifications) by Solana. Solana's "Alpenglow" upgrade is a Simplex derivative. So the industry-wide post-HotStuff convergence is on Simplex. TON's adoption of Simplex is the rational engineering move, not the unusual one.
The Nikolai-replaces-Nikolai angle. Original Catchain (2018 TON whitepaper) was Nikolai's bespoke BFT design — a custom protocol with TON-specific shardchain coordination, somewhere in the PBFT/HotStuff family lineage. The new system deprecates that work in favor of an adapted Chan/Pass design. This is interesting because most serial architects defend their own protocols; replacing your own consensus with an academic competitor's is unusually ego-free. Two possible reads:
It's genuinely Nikolai doing the adaptation, and he assessed Simplex as cleaner and faster than his own Catchain — a quiet, high-rigor pragmatism consistent with the pattern you identify.
The TON core team has additional consensus people, possibly under Nikolai but not him personally. The C++ implementation exists in validator/consensus/ — someone wrote it, the spec is anonymous, the contest pseudonymizes everything.

Infinite sharding holds architecturally. The Simplex adoption is at the per-shard consensus layer — what each validator subset runs internally to agree on its own shardchain's blocks.
