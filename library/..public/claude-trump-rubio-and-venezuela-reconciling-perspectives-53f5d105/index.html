Historical Precedents: Three Pathways, All Problematic
The Trump/Rubio Venezuela intervention has three close historical precedents, all from early 20th century US fiscal receiverships. Each followed a similar pattern and led to decades of dysfunction.
PRECEDENT 1: Haiti (1915-1934) → Duvalier Dictatorship
The Intervention: The US invaded Haiti in July 1915, ostensibly to restore stability after President Guillaume Sam's assassination, but actually to protect US assets and prevent German influence. The US took complete control of Haitian finances, removed $500,000 from the National Bank to New York, and created a US-controlled military force called the Haitian Gendarmerie.
US control of customs duties and Haiti's National Bank lasted until 1947, with troops withdrawing in 1934 after sustained protest.
Long-term Outcome (3-40 years out):
The racism and violence during the US occupation, which began in 1915, inspired black nationalism among Haitians and left a powerful impression on the young François Duvalier, born in 1907.
François "Papa Doc" Duvalier came to power in 1957 and set up his own army of thugs—the Tontons Macoutes. The Duvalierist reign of terror—supported and backed by the U.S.—killed roughly 50,000 people. When Papa Doc died in 1971, U.S. warships were stationed off Haiti's coast to oversee transition to his son Jean-Claude "Baby Doc".
The Duvalier dynasty lasted until 1986 (29 years). Chief among the legacies of the occupation was creation of the Haitian Gendarmes, later known as Forces Armees d'Haiti (FADH), which became the tool for maintaining dictatorship.
Why it failed:
Although the US occupation offered some improvements to infrastructure and education, it was ultimately a social and political disaster. The racist beliefs that Haitians were politically inept and unable to govern meant Americans had no incentive to train Haitians adequately
The security forces created to maintain "stability" became instruments of oppression
Anti-American nationalism created by occupation fueled authoritarian populism
PRECEDENT 2: Nicaragua (1912-1933) → Somoza Dynasty
The Intervention: US Marines stayed in Nicaragua from 1912 to 1933 (with a brief gap 1925-1927). Although US policymakers proclaimed desire to bring democracy, military intervention showed order and stability were the primary goals.
In 1927, to end civil war and establish stability so forces could eventually leave, the US formed a pact with Nicaragua to supervise elections and establish a National Guard as the sole military force. They installed Anastasio Somoza as commander.
Long-term Outcome (4-46 years out):
When US troops left in 1933, they did not depart from a democratic nation, but from the emerging dictatorship of Anastasio Somoza, whose dynasty would fall to the Sandinistas in 1979.
Somoza arranged for National Guardsmen to kidnap and kill Sandino (the nationalist resistance leader) in February 1934. Within a month, guardsmen killed the majority of Sandino's followers. Once in command of the country's only military group and without strong political opponent, Somoza appointed himself president in 1937 and began a dictatorial dynasty that ruled Nicaragua until 1980.
The Somoza family ruled for 42 years (1937-1979), eventually overthrown by Sandinista revolutionaries named after the man Somoza had murdered.
Why it failed:
The legacy of US occupation included political instability and general distrust towards American policies. The National Guard, under Anastasio Somoza García, evolved into a significant political force, eventually leading to the Somoza family dictatorship
US-trained security apparatus served dynasty, not democracy
Nationalist guerrilla leader assassinated by US-backed forces became martyred symbol
PRECEDENT 3: Dominican Republic (1905-1924) → Instability & Trujillo
The Intervention: In 1905, facing chronic political instability and concern about European intervention over unpaid debts, the Roosevelt administration took control of Dominican customs. US officials assumed control over key revenue streams, and in some cases exercised authority over internal taxation and budgeting.
A financial accord signed in 1905 gave the US responsibility for all Dominican debt and collection of customs duties. The fiscal stability eroded, culminating in full military occupation from 1916-1924.
Long-term Outcome (5+ years out):
The assassination of President Cáceres in 1911 turned out to be the first act of a frenzied drama that culminated in the republic's occupation by the United States. The US withdrew in 1924, but:
The real origin point of the hard border policy was Trujillo's genocide of 1937, which eliminated an estimated 20,000 ethnically Haitian civilians living in the Dominican Republic.
Rafael Trujillo's dictatorship (1930-1961) followed the US withdrawal and was one of the most brutal in Latin American history.
The Pattern: What Fiscal Receiverships Actually Produce
By the 1930s, these accumulated failures led Washington to abandon the model under FDR's Good Neighbor Policy, recognizing that governing foreign finances from afar was both politically costly and economically ineffective. Coercion alone could not produce stability or legitimacy.
