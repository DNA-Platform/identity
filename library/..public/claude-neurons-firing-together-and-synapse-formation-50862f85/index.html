You're thinking of the Kwon & Sabatini 2011 Nature paper, almost certainly. They used two-photon glutamate uncaging at bare stretches of dendrite on cortical layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons in acute slices and showed that glutamate alone is sufficient to trigger de novo spine growth in a location-specific manner. The requirements were NMDA receptor activation, Ca²⁺ release from internal stores, PKA, and MAPK — and notably no filopodial intermediate was observed; spines appeared within ~10 seconds. The new spines then matured and acquired functional responses to presynaptic input. That's about as close as anyone has gotten to "correlated glutamatergic signal causes a synapse to form right there."
One small correction to your framing: it's not AMPA from the presynaptic axon — AMPA/NMDA are postsynaptic receptors. What comes from the axon is glutamate, which binds both. The Kwon/Sabatini trick is that uncaging glutamate substitutes for presynaptic release, and the NMDAR requirement is the "coincidence detector" piece (needs glutamate + depolarization), which is the synaptogenesis analog of the Hebbian rule you're invoking for LTP. Follow-ups extended this: Oh, Lutzu, Castillo & Kwon showed local GABA uncaging similarly induces gephyrin puncta and inhibitory synapse formation via GABA_A and VGCCs in developing cortex, and Lee et al. (Neuron 2016) found NMDAR-coupled ryanodine-dependent intracellular Ca²⁺ release implements a cooperative plasticity rule between coactive neighboring synapses during synaptogenesis, which is why developing dendrites end up with clustered synaptic weights.
The caveats worth keeping in mind: these results are mostly in young (P8–12) tissue, and "spine forms where glutamate was uncaged" isn't quite "two specific neurons grow a new synapse because they fired together" — the presynaptic partner in Kwon/Sabatini is a laser, not a coactive neuron. A 2024 bioRxiv paper from the Lohmann group pushed closer to the in vivo version, showing NMDAR-driven, minute-long dendritic Ca²⁺ transients immediately precede filopodial emergence in CA1 during a prenatal critical window. But you're right that the clean "two neurons, correlated activity, watch a synapse form between them" experiment is harder to find than you'd expect given how foundational the claim is.

You're not wrong, and this is essentially the selectionist (as opposed to instructionist) account of circuit formation, which goes back to Changeux & Danchin's 1976 Nature paper "Selective stabilization of developing synapses" and Edelman's Neural Darwinism program. The minimal ingredients you listed — exploratory/stochastic synapse formation, activity-dependent stabilization, and elimination of weakly-driven contacts — are sufficient to produce like-to-like connectivity at the population level. You don't need any mechanism that explicitly detects correlation at the moment of synapse formation. This is also baked into classic theoretical work: the BCM rule (Bienenstock, Cooper & Munro 1982), Linsker's emergence of orientation selectivity from Hebbian dynamics, Miller/Keller/Stryker on ocular dominance — all of these predict that population-level "fire together, wire together" structure falls out of local synaptic rules plus turnover, almost trivially. Empirically, the climbing fiber → Purkinje cell pruning (Hashimoto & Kano) and the NMJ work from Lichtman's lab are textbook demonstrations that the selection mechanism is real.
So if the Ding et al. 2025 MICrONS paper is presenting "neurons with similar tuning are more connected" as the headline finding, then yes, it's theoretically expected and the lack of citation to the selectionist tradition is a real gap — though it's also fairly standard for connectomics papers to live in their own citation bubble, separate from the developmental/theoretical neuroscience literature. What I'd say is genuinely interesting in that paper, and harder to get out of vanilla pairwise Hebbian + pruning, is the higher-order rule: the cohort of postsynaptic neurons downstream of a single presynaptic cell is more functionally similar to each other than a pairwise like-to-like rule would predict. That's a statement about third-order correlations in the connectivity, and it's not obvious that simple pair-based STDP plus elimination produces it — you'd probably need triplet rules, heterosynaptic competition, or branch-level signaling to explain it cleanly. That part is worth the empirical effort.
The other thing the paper does that's defensible is quantify the effect at scale and show it holds across layers, areas, and feedback connections, where you could imagine the rule might break down or invert. The theory doesn't guarantee that the brain actually implements the rule everywhere — it's compatible with the brain doing something else. But you're right that the "needs a fancy RNN comparison to be interpretable" framing is overselling. A reader who knows Changeux, BCM, Miller-Stryker would say the pairwise result is a sanity check, not a discovery, and the real novelty is the higher-order cohort finding. The fact that the paper doesn't situate itself that way is a writing/framing issue, not a substance issue — but it's annoying, and your instinct to push back is correct.

Yes — the LTD/death side of the ledger is solid. Nägerl et al. 2004 (Bonhoeffer lab) and Zhou, Homma & Poo 2004 both showed that LTD-inducing low-frequency stimulation produces spine shrinkage requiring NMDA receptor activation and calcineurin, with cofilin-mediated actin depolymerization as the downstream effector. Wiegert & Oertner 2013 (PNAS) extended this with optogenetic LTD and showed that depression is followed days later by elimination of a subset of depressed spines and their neighbors, preferentially at synapses with initially low release probability — unreliable synapses get selectively removed from the circuit. Ramiro-Cortés & Israely 2013 added mGluR-LTD, Hayama et al. 2013 added GABA-gated versions, Shinoda 2010 added the repeated-LTD case. So you have the full circuit: glutamate + post-synaptic Ca²⁺ at the right magnitude → growth/LTP/stabilization; same machinery at lower amplitude → shrinkage, then elimination. Nothing missing from the cell biology.
And yes, theories have been written down. Multiple, actually. Seung & Zung 2017 formalized unsupervised learning as a zero-sum game between Hebbian excitation and anti-Hebbian inhibition with explicit synaptic competition that eliminates all but the most strongly correlated inputs. Knoblauch has a "potential synapses" framework with probabilistic state transitions tied to memory capacity. Zheng, Dimitrakakis & Triesch 2013 combined STDP, homeostasis, and structural plasticity in spiking networks. Fauth & Tetzlaff have a series on the interaction of structural and synaptic plasticity timescales. On the statistics specifically, Loewenstein, Kuras & Rumpel 2011 showed the stationary spine-size distribution is well-fit by a log-normal, which requires that synaptic changes be proportional to current synaptic efficacy (multiplicative dynamics) rather than the additive decay terms used in classical models. The Ziv lab (Statman, Hazan, Minerbi) extended this into a Kesten/Langevin stochastic framework that reproduces synaptic size distributions, turnover statistics, and tenacity timescales from a small number of parameters.
Here's the slightly heretical point that makes your question even sharper: the Ziv-lab-style models are largely activity-independent — stochastic multiplicative shrinkage plus additive growth, or cooperative binding/unbinding of scaffold molecules, already reproduces the lognormal-like distribution of synaptic sizes. Which means a large fraction of the population-level statistics of synapses doesn't actually require Hebbian explanation at all. What requires Hebbian explanation is the correlation structure — which synapses are where, between which neurons — and that's exactly the bit MICrONS measured. So the honest framing of the field is: we have a good stochastic-statistical theory of synapse size distributions (Loewenstein/Ziv), a good cellular theory of how individual synapses are born and die (Kwon/Sabatini + Nägerl/Wiegert), and a good selectionist theory of how correlated activity sculpts populations (Changeux/Edelman/BCM/Seung). They've never been fully unified into a single quantitative model that predicts MICrONS-scale connectivity from molecular rules, but that's not because the pieces are missing — it's because the integration work hasn't been the prestige project. The MICrONS paper could have said "our pairwise like-to-like result is consistent with the predictions of selectionist theory dating back to Changeux 1976, and quantifies the effect at scale; our higher-order cohort result is the genuinely novel observation." That would have been the honest framing.

Chuck's approach is exactly the right reference frame here, and your instinct is sound — but you should know that someone already wrote down that paper, in 2015: Cossell, Iacaruso, Muir, Houlton, Sader, Ko, Hofer & Mrsic-Flogel, "Functional organization of excitatory synaptic strength in primary visual cortex" (Nature 2015), combined two-photon calcium imaging with paired intracellular recordings in mouse V1 L2/3 and showed that strong connections are sparse and occur between neurons with correlated responses, while weak connections link uncorrelated neurons. That is a measurement of P(weight | signal correlation), exactly the function you sketched. They found connection amplitudes spanning two orders of magnitude with a strongly skewed distribution, and the few strong synapses concentrated at high correlation — a one-parameter rule. So the simple theory exists, the data confirming it exists, and it's a decade old. The MICrONS paper is essentially Cossell-at-EM-scale, with structural rather than electrophysiological readout, and the honest framing would be: "Cossell's pairwise rule holds when measured structurally, across layers and areas and feedback, and here is a new higher-order cohort deviation that suggests the rule isn't purely pairwise."
The Stevens-style theory you're gesturing at is also basically free to write down once you accept the Loewenstein/Ziv multiplicative-dynamics framework. Take each potential synapse to follow ds/dt = μ(r)·s + σ·s·dW, where r is the correlation between the two neurons and μ is a drift that's positive above some correlation threshold and negative below. Add an elimination boundary at small s. Then you get, analytically: a lognormal stationary distribution of synaptic sizes conditional on existence, a connection probability P(connected | r) that's a sigmoid in r, an expected weight E[s | r, connected] that's monotonic in r, and a total dyadic connection strength E[total area | r] = P(connected | r) · ⟨#synapses per pair⟩ · E[s | r]. Three or four parameters, fit from the data, and you predict the marginals of the MICrONS observations. You'd then look for residuals — the cohort-level higher-order term being one — to identify what the pairwise theory misses. That's the Stevens move: write the minimal probability model, fit it, and use the deviations to tell you what extra structure you actually need.
The reason the paper doesn't do this is partly cultural — the connectomics + deep-learning axis the MICrONS consortium grew out of frames problems as engineering challenges that need digital twins and RNN comparisons, not as statistical mechanics problems with two or three parameters. Chuck's tradition (along with Bialek, the Ziv lab, Loewenstein, Sompolinsky in a different mode) treats the brain as a place where the right theory is small and the data should overconstrain it. If the answer to "why are functionally similar neurons more strongly connected" requires a deep learning ablation study, you've lost the plot somewhere. Your reading of the paper is correct: it's a confirmation paper dressed up as a discovery paper, and the genuinely interesting finding — the higher-order cohort similarity beyond pairwise prediction — is the part that should have been framed as the residual after fitting the simple Cossell/Stevens-style theory.
