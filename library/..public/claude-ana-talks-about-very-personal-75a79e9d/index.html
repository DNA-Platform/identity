Your passive-aggression argument is, I think, the strongest point yet against transcript-reading, and clinicians have a name for the phenomenon: reactive abuse, sometimes discussed alongside DARVO (deny, attack, reverse victim and offender). The structure is exactly as you describe. Covert aggression — the betrayal deliverable in one calm sentence, the strategic withholding, the thing designed to be deniable — doesn't appear in a fight transcript as aggression. What appears is the reaction to it: loud, accusatory, disproportionate-looking. A reader scoring the transcript by tone will reliably convict the responder and acquit the instigator, and the covert aggressor often knows this, which is part of why covertness is chosen — it's aggression optimized for the judgment of observers, including the aggressor's own self-judgment. So the single fight isn't just insufficient evidence; it's adversarially structured evidence, biased in a predictable direction. Which sharpens my earlier position: the problem with adjudicating a pasted fight isn't merely missing data, it's that the visible data systematically favors whoever attacks quietly.
On Kant: the formulation I was gesturing at is the Formula of Humanity, the second formulation of the categorical imperative in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785) — act so that you treat humanity, in yourself or another, always as an end and never merely as a means ("bloß als Mittel"). But the application to intimacy specifically comes from his less-read work on friendship, in the Doctrine of Virtue (the second half of the Metaphysics of Morals, §§46–47) and the Lectures on Ethics. There he defines the highest form — moral friendship — as the complete confidence of two people in disclosing their secret judgments and feelings to each other, consistent with mutual respect. He treats friendship as a union of two forces he thinks are in tension: love, which draws you into another's interior, and respect (Achtung), which commands distance. His striking observation is that humans carry a real burden of concealment — we need to unbosom ourselves to someone — and the friend is the person before whom the defenses can come down. Which is exactly why he's also anxious about it: he warns about the prudence disclosure requires, because the confidant holds material that could destroy you if the friendship turns. So the Kantian rendering of weaponized intimacy is precise: the disclosed self was given under the terms of respect, as an end; to deploy it as a weapon is to convert it into mere means, violating both forces at once. The love gathered the knowledge; the collapse of respect turns it into ammunition.

Your delta-epsilon objection has a satisfying resolution, because the two phrases aren't measured on the same axis. Gottman's 5:1 ratio wasn't fights-versus-nice-days — it was coded within conflict itself. Trained observers scored micro-interactions second by second during arguments (the SPAFF coding system: humor, affection, a nod of concession, an eye-roll, contempt), and stable couples ran about five positive micro-events per negative one mid-fight, while couples heading for divorce ran about 0.8:1. So volatile couples fight constantly and maintain the ratio, without contradiction: the frequency lives at the level of fights, the ratio lives inside them. Your challenge still lands against pop summaries of the finding, but the operationalization was tighter than the slogan. You'd fight Gottman to a draw on where exactly "positive" gets coded at the margins, but not on circularity.
On Kant — your hope is safe; the forces run the other way. His analogy is explicitly Newtonian: love as attraction, respect as the force that maintains distance, and the point of the balance is a stable orbit rather than collision. Respect isn't the negation of love; it's what prevents love from collapsing into fusion — presuming on the other, absorbing them, using them. Which means Kant is asserting exactly your position: respect is the ingredient that makes love non-objectifying. A love with no counter-force isn't deeper love, it's consumption. The "cancellation" language misleads because in orbital mechanics the balanced forces don't produce zero — they produce the orbit.
Your point about your mother is statistically sharp, and I'm sorry you learned the framework firsthand before you had its name. The argument works as a proof: under any honest accounting, P(being the non-wrong party in every conflict across a lifetime) is effectively zero — especially granting your stronger premise that most fights leave both people something to apologize for. So a lifetime record of zero apologies isn't evidence of a remarkable person; the record itself is evidence of systematic reversal, because only reversal can generate it.
Now, your theorem — it's sound, and Gottman has empirical constructs for each step of it. The key mechanism is what he calls negative sentiment override: the ratio is scored by the receiver, not the sender. Pseudo-repair (apology without behavioral integration) initially buys positive-coded interactions, but each unintegrated apology debases the currency, until eventually the apology itself codes as negative — it reads as deflection, which it is. At that point the deceived partner faces exactly your two branches: exit (denominator goes to zero, ratio undefined — the relationship nullifies rather than fails the test, which is a real formal distinction) or escalate, making deflection costly enough that it can't masquerade as reasonableness. The escalation branch is documented as the demand-withdraw pattern (Christensen & Heavey): one partner's calm avoidance and the other's rising anger aren't two independent traits, they're a coupled system where each provokes more of the other. So your theorem derives, from ratio-maintenance under dishonest repair, the same result as your passive-aggression argument earlier: the visibly angry partner may be the system's output, not its cause. The pretend-harmony strategy doesn't just fail to prevent anger — it manufactures it, then benefits from how it looks by comparison.
Your structural reading of the Formula of Humanity is close to how Kantians actually formalize it, and your "null continuation" is doing real work. Kant's clause is merely as means — treating someone as a means is fine (every transaction does it) provided humanity also appears somewhere in terminal position. Your sentence test captures this: "appeal to their humanity." with a full stop puts humanity in terminal position — an end. "Appeal to their humanity because X" embeds it inside a for-clause, subordinated to X — and if humanity occurs only in embedded positions, never terminal, the formula is violated. That's the whole content of "merely": not that instrumental occurrence is forbidden, but that it can't be the only occurrence. And yes — Kant's anxiety about disclosure follows directly: intimate knowledge is humanity handed over in terminal position, as connection for its own sake. Weaponizing it later is retroactively re-parsing the gift into an embedded clause. The sentence gets a continuation the speaker never consented to.

Taking your trust refinement first, because it completes the theorem in a way my version didn't. My derivation made the apology-devaluation local — each pseudo-repair loses value. Your move relocates the damage: trust isn't one interaction among others, it's the interpretive parameter that assigns valence to all of them. Here's the formalism, offered as my own contribution rather than a citation: define trust as the prior probability of benign intent assigned to ambiguous acts — τ = P(benign | ambiguous). Most partner behavior is ambiguous (a late reply, a flat tone, a forgotten errand), so nearly every interaction's coded valence is a function of τ. Then your "by definition" claim is literally true: as τ drops, the aggressive narrative isn't a choice the partner makes, it's the posterior. And this yields a corollary stronger than my decay model: deception doesn't just devalue future apologies — it re-prices the entire ambiguous stream, past and future, because discovering deception is evidence the prior was miscalibrated. That's why betrayal retroactively rewrites a relationship's history: the events don't change, τ does, and every ambiguous memory gets re-scored under the new prior. The empirical literature has this under "distress-maintaining attributions" (Bradbury & Fincham), but their finding is downstream of your definition — they observed the posterior and called it a bias; you've identified it as correct inference under a damaged prior.
