Here's what I found. Nothing indicates a known network-wide freeze on Monad unstaking — the likely culprits are wallet-side. A few things worth checking, roughly in order of probability:
1. Gas. Monad's native staking requires liquid MON to pay for the unstake transaction itself. Guides explicitly warn to keep a small amount of MON aside for gas so unstaking actions can be processed. If essentially your whole balance is delegated, the unstake button can silently fail or grey out. Sending a small amount of liquid MON to the address is the first thing to try.
2. The two-step exit. Native Monad unstaking isn't a single action. An unstake request exits in the following era — or two eras, if submitted after the boundary block (a boundary block lands every 50,000 blocks) — and after the unbonding period completes, you must separately withdraw the unstaked MON to return it to your available balance. Atomic's own docs say to expect about 2 days after starting unbonding before the MON is liquid again. If an earlier unstake attempt actually went through on-chain, the app may be showing a pending position that needs a withdraw action rather than another unstake.
3. Atomic app sync/state bugs. Atomic has a history of the UI showing stale staking state. Standard remedies: update to the latest version, or reinstall (with your 12-word phrase backed up first).
The clean escape hatch: Atomic is non-custodial, so your stake lives on-chain, not in their app. You can restore the same seed (or export the EVM private key for that address) into another wallet — Rabby or MetaMask configured for Monad — and manage the delegation directly through a validator dashboard (Everstake, Imperator, monadvision.com all read the native staking contract). That takes Atomic's app entirely out of the loop. If you go that route, verify the wallet software carefully before entering the phrase.
One warning: searching this topic surfaces a lot of SEO spam with fake "Atomic Wallet support" phone numbers. Atomic has no phone support — email only ([email protected]). Anything with a call-this-number is a scam.

The research confirms your position, and more thoroughly than I expected. The canonical work here is Fama and French's "Luck versus Skill in the Cross-Section of Mutual Fund Returns," and its conclusion is close to your claim stated formally: even managers with strongly positive alpha estimates over their entire histories can't be distinguished from the mass of unskilled managers — if skilled managers exist, they're statistically hidden. That's professional fund managers with decades of monthly data points — the best-case grading conditions that will ever exist — and the answer is still indeterminate at the individual level. Mauboussin's work lands in the same place: across 1,400+ mutual funds, correlation between successive three-year performance periods was so low that he estimates investing outcomes over that horizon at roughly 85% luck — driven by the paradox of skill, where more skilled participants compress the ability spread until luck dominates outcomes. And he names the exact trap I was in: the narrative fallacy — patterns look clear in hindsight, temporary outperformance reads as enduring skill, and our instinct about which is which is almost always wrong.
So my "track record" standard from last turn was naive twice over. First, as you say, the outcome doesn't cleanly grade the prediction — a position that made money doesn't certify the reasoning, and one that lost doesn't refute it; you can do everything right and lose, everything wrong and win, and a lifetime of results may not contain enough independent bets to separate the cases. Second, even the aggregate statistical machinery that can detect skill existing somewhere in a population — streak analysis across 5,500 funds does find more persistence than a pure-luck null model predicts — can't tell you which individual has it, or whether a specific call was insight or noise.
