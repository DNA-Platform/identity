Searched the web for “how to spot AI generated video tells 2026”
Your instinct used to be a pretty reliable tell — stiff faces, bad lip sync, teeth that smeared when mouths moved. But the first AI videos had obvious tells like out-of-sync voices or distorted objects, and there are fewer signs like this now because the technology has evolved. Veo 3 in 2025 more or less closed the talking-face gap, and stuff generated in 2026 often syncs cleanly at casual-viewing speed.
What still tends to trip current models:
Lip sync drift over time. A clip can start perfect and desync by the end, so watch the whole thing. Mouth movement remains one of the hardest problems — cheeks lift, jaw shifts, chin tightens, and micro-expressions in the eyes subtly react to emphasis, and AI often misses those correlated micro-movements.
Physics and object permanence. Things passing through each other, objects appearing/disappearing between frames, shadows that don't match the light source, reflections that misbehave, straight edges going wavy when the camera moves.
Hands, teeth, hair edges. Still the classic weak spots — teeth that blur or change count, hair that shimmers, fingers that morph.
Audio that's too clean. Unnatural pacing without normal hesitations or breathing, flawless pronunciation lacking the imperfections of natural speech, and missing ambient background noise.
Honest caveat though: controlled tests show people correctly identify high-quality deepfake videos only about 24.5% of the time — worse than chance. So visual tells are a dying game. The more durable signal is context: reverse image search, check whether a real news outlet is reporting it, look at the account posting it, and notice platform labels (that "AI info" tag on the reel you saw is one).
One other note — the Street Fighter thing you saw was a still image, not a video, so it wouldn't have had motion tells either way. For stills the giveaways are overly smooth skin, weirdly perfect symmetry, and background details that don't quite resolve (look at the photographer's cameras at the bottom of that image — they're mushy).

Yeah, you're right on both counts.
Talking video with synced audio is genuinely harder and more expensive to generate than silent motion. The model has to solve lip shapes, jaw movement, and audio timing all at once, and voice cloning a specific real person well enough to fool anyone adds another layer. So a lot of creators default to silent clips with slow pans, subtle idle motion, or just Ken Burns–style movement over a still — because that hides the weak spots and costs way less in compute and time.
There's also a quality tier thing. The top-end models (Veo 3, Sora 2, Kling's latest) can do convincing talking faces, but they're rate-limited, expensive, or behind waitlists. A page pumping out multiple "what if" casting reels a week is almost certainly using cheaper tools, and those still struggle with natural speech. So "stiff faces, no dialogue" is a pretty reliable tell that you're looking at low-budget AI slop specifically — even if it's not a universal tell for AI video in general.
The account pattern you noticed is actually the stronger signal. One stiff-faced clip could be a stylistic choice; a whole feed of them is a workflow.
