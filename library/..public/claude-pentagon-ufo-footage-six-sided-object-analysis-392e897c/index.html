The mechanism is the same one behind the famous 2021 "pyramid" video, which Mick West and others resolved as bokeh — an out-of-focus point source taking on the shape of the camera's aperture (that system had a triangular iris; this one would have a six-bladed or three-strut aperture, both of which produce six-fold symmetry). Bright unresolved point sources in IR systems also generate six-spike diffraction/glare patterns from the aperture blades. So the star shape tells you about the lens assembly, not about the target. Even the release notes that at first glance it resembles a crack across the display — which is itself a tell that the signal is dominated by the optical system rather than resolved structure.
Note that "artifact" here doesn't mean nothing was there. The tracker was locking onto a real bright point source — plausibly an aircraft light, flare, satellite glint, or balloon in a region dense with military and surveillance activity between China and Korea. What's artifactual is the shape. A genuine six-sided craft would have to be resolved by the sensor, and a resolved extended object doesn't render as a symmetric starburst; a saturating point source does. The Pentagon's own framing supports this reading — the released cases are labeled unresolved, meaning no definitive determination, not "defies conventional explanation."

The green fireballs are actually one of the better-documented historical cases, so there are referents — just old ones. Between late 1948 and 1951, dozens of green fireballs were seen over New Mexico, concentrated suspiciously near Los Alamos and Sandia. The February 1949 conference (Teller and other Manhattan Project physicists attended) centered on Lincoln LaPaz, a meteor astronomer who argued they couldn't be ordinary bolides: trajectories too flat and horizontal, color too green, no sonic booms, no recoverable fragments despite systematic searches. That's the "nothing like this has ever been observed in meteorite drops" line in the transcript. The Air Force ran Project Twinkle (1950–51) with instrumented cameras to catch one; it never got usable triangulated data and was shut down. The prevailing modern view is that they were an unusual run of natural fireballs — the green is unremarkable in itself (magnesium and atmospheric oxygen emission produce it) — with the clustering near nuclear sites largely explained by observer density: that's where trained skywatchers and security personnel were concentrated and required to report. LaPaz never accepted that, and the case genuinely remains one where a competent specialist said "this doesn't fit my reference class." What it almost certainly wasn't is craft — the Cold War-era worry was Soviet reconnaissance, and even that died for lack of any physical evidence.
On why they're being released: the proximate cause is Trump's executive order earlier this year mandating declassification, riding a bipartisan disclosure push that's been building since the 2017 NYT story (Schumer's UAP Disclosure Act, the Grusch hearings). The administration gets a cheap transparency win — it's adjacent to the Epstein-files politics, "we release what they hid" — and the files themselves cost nothing because they're genuinely unresolved-but-mundane: areas of contrast, range-fouler debriefs, deformed balloons.
Your last observation is the sharp one, and it has real historical precedent. The government has repeatedly found UFO belief useful as ambient cover. The CIA's own 1997 internal history acknowledged that a large fraction of 1950s–60s sightings were U-2 and OXCART flights, and that Blue Book explanations were left conveniently wrong rather than reveal the programs. Kirtland AFB counterintelligence actively fed UFO disinformation to Paul Bennewitz in the 1980s to obscure classified testing. So "letting others feel that way" isn't an accident of communication — strategic ambiguity around aerospace test activity is a standing institutional habit. The current releases fit that pattern well: every file is stamped "unresolved," which is technically honest (no definitive determination) but functionally feeds the coverup narrative, since the audience reads "unresolved" as "inexplicable" rather than "insufficient data about something boring." The government doesn't believe it's aliens; it also has no incentive to be maximally deflationary, because the mystique is free camouflage for whatever is actually flying near China and Korea.

Not a malfunction — the system is working exactly as designed. What you're describing is the point spread function. If the target is smaller than the sensor's angular resolution (which at any real distance over the Yellow Sea it almost certainly is), it's an unresolved point source, and what lands on the focal plane is not the object's shape but the optical system's response to a point: diffraction spikes from aperture blades or struts, bloom if the source is bright enough to saturate pixels, hexagonal bokeh if slightly defocused. The star pattern isn't obscuring the object's details — those details never entered the system. There is no shape information to recover. All the video actually contains is radiometry (how bright, in what band) and kinematics (angular motion, tracking behavior), and even kinematics is nearly useless without range: an unresolved point at unknown distance gives you no size and no speed, just angular rate.
Your selection-effect inference is right, and it probably runs in both directions. Files that resolved into something identifiable get closed and labeled, so they're not interesting enough to headline; files that identified something sensitive (an adversary platform, or our own) stay classified. What's releasable is the residue: genuinely unresolved cases that reveal nothing about the target and nothing much about collection capability. Even then they hedge — footage in these releases is typically cropped, short, and stripped of the metadata overlay (range, coordinates, platform), because the one thing raw sensor video does leak is information about the sensor. So an 18-second clip of a diffraction pattern is close to the ideal release: maximally evocative, minimally informative. The publishable set is essentially pre-filtered to be mysterious.

21:54 UTC, 10 July 2026
The hardware is a targeting pod — a self-contained sensor turret hung on the airframe (ATFLIR, Litening, Sniper are the well-known ones; ships and drones carry equivalents). Inside is a gimbal-stabilized telescope feeding two kinds of camera: a visible-light TV channel and an infrared channel, usually mid-wave IR (3–5 μm), where the detector is a cooled focal plane array — a grid of photodiodes sensitive to thermal photons, chilled cryogenically so the sensor's own heat doesn't drown the scene. "FLIR" imagery is a map of temperature contrast, not reflected light, which is why targets appear as white-hot or black-hot blobs. The gimbal plus inertial stabilization is what lets it hold a point target dead-center from a moving platform miles away, and an autotracker keeps the crosshairs on whatever contrast blob it's told to follow.
The part relevant to your question: what reaches the screen is heavily processed, and the processing does put things into the image. The raw detector output goes through non-uniformity correction (each pixel has its own gain/offset that drifts), automatic gain control that continuously restretches the scene's contrast so dim things become visible, edge sharpening, and de-rotation (the gimbal geometry rotates the image as it slews, so software or prisms counter-rotate it). AGC is the big one for UAP footage — it will amplify a faint blob into a vivid, crisp-edged object, and when a bright source saturates, it shapes the bloom and spikes around it. The Gimbal video is the canonical case: the "craft rotating" is the de-rotation mechanism stepping the glare pattern around, an artifact of the stabilization system itself. So yes — the "details" you see on an unresolved target are largely manufactured by the enhancement pipeline operating on optics-level artifacts.
Aircrew training is operational: how to slew the pod, designate targets, interpret hot/cold signatures of vehicles and people, manage the symbology. They know practical quirks — glare, gain pumping, tracker breaks — the way a driver knows their car. What they're generally not taught is the underlying pipeline: point spread functions, what AGC does to sub-resolution sources, how the de-rotation geometry moves glare. That knowledge lives with the engineers who build the pods, which is why an experienced weapons officer can genuinely, honestly report a rotating object with no exhaust plume when the physical cause is a glint being processed by his own instrument.

There’s no actual record of the underlying footage to diff against?
Claude
05:41 UTC, 11 July 2026
Generally no — and this is one of the deepest problems with the whole enterprise. The processing happens in real time inside the pod's electronics, upstream of anything that gets recorded. What's stored is the processed video stream — essentially the cockpit display feed, complete with symbology — because that's what operations needs: mission review, battle damage assessment, legal review of strikes. The raw focal-plane readout (pre-AGC, pre-sharpening, pre-de-rotation) typically isn't retained at all. It exists for milliseconds as voltages and buffer states and is gone. Bandwidth and storage are part of it, but mostly there's just no operational requirement — nobody flying a strike mission needs uncalibrated detector output.
So the "original" is the artifact. There's no negative to go back to, no RAW file behind the JPEG. The enhancement is baked in at capture, which means analysts trying to resolve these cases later are doing forensics on an image whose transfer function they can only partially reconstruct — they can reason about what AGC and the point spread function would do, but they can't invert it, because the operations are lossy and scene-dependent.
What does exist alongside the video is metadata: the symbology overlay itself (range if there was a laser or radar track, slant angles, platform coordinates, sensor mode), plus the platform's own data buses. That's often enough to constrain a case — GoFast was resolved using the displayed range and angles to compute the object's actual altitude and speed, which turned out to be mundane. Which makes it notable that the public releases tend to crop or omit exactly that layer. The one form of ground truth that survives recording is the part most consistently stripped before release — justified as protecting sensor capabilities, but it's also the difference between a solvable geometry problem and an evocative mystery blob.

Claude

You could — but the fix isn't a second camera, and the reasons it's not done are instructive.
A separate camera doesn't solve it because the artifacts live in the shared optics. Diffraction spikes, glare, bokeh are produced by the telescope's aperture and focus, so a different camera with its own lens sees its own different artifacts of the same point source — you'd have two stylized renderings and still no ground truth about shape, because shape was never captured by either. What you'd actually want is to tap thesame focal plane before enhancement: record the digitized detector output post-readout but pre-AGC, pre-sharpening. That's technically feasible — it's just a data stream — and it's exactly what science-grade sensors do: astronomy instruments and missile-defense tracking sensors record radiometrically calibrated raw precisely so you can separate instrument response from target. Also worth knowing: fully "unprocessed" IR is nearly unintelligible — a raw FPA frame is a snowstorm of per-pixel gain variation and dead pixels — so the meaningful raw is "corrected but not enhanced," which preserves the actual radiometry AGC destroys.
Why targeting pods don't retain it: no operational need (the pod's job is putting weapons on targets, not instrument science), real bandwidth and storage costs multiplied across every sortie for data virtually never used, and — probably decisive — classification. Calibrated raw output directly reveals the sensor's noise floor, sensitivity, and correction tables, which is capability intelligence of the kind adversaries want most. A recorded raw stream is a leak surface. So the enhancement layer functions almost like armor: the processed feed is releasable precisely because it obscures what the instrument can really do.
The interesting coda is that this gap is acknowledged now. AARO's stated position under Kirkpatrick was that military sensors are unsuited for UAP resolution for exactly these reasons, and they've fielded purpose-built calibrated multi-sensor kits (the Gremlin program) for hotspot deployment. So the honest answer to "why can't they" is: they can, they've started to, but on dedicated instruments — the operational fleet will keep recording the processed feed, and its footage will keep generating starbursts.

You're drawing the right cut, and it's the one the label "unresolved" deliberately blurs. There are two separate epistemic states: an anomaly, where the data is good and positively resists every known explanation; and a data-limited case, where the data is too poor to select among perfectly ordinary explanations. The Yellow Sea video is emphatically the second. The recording itself is explained — fully, mechanistically: point source, PSF, AGC, six-fold aperture symmetry. What remains open is only the attribution of a mundane dot, and a dot at unknown range is unattributable in the same boring way your half-blind witness's testimony is. That residual isn't mystery; it's ignorance, and ignorance manufactured by the instrument, which we understand completely. Honest labeling would be something like "phenomenology explained; source unattributed for lack of data."
Your witness analogy actually cashes out as a likelihood-ratio point: evidence from a known-bad instrument can't raise the probability of exotic hypotheses, because a bad instrument produces the same smear under nearly all hypotheses. P(starburst | balloon) ≈ P(starburst | aircraft) ≈ P(starburst | anything bright and small). Evidence that fails to discriminate leaves your priors where they were. So "unexplained" in the sense that would matter — data that shifts weight toward the exotic — simply isn't present. The Pentagon's usage inverts this: it lets the weakness of the evidence do rhetorical work that only strength of evidence could legitimately do. A blurry photo at Loch Ness isn't evidence of a monster; it's evidence of blur.
And you can't shrug off the part that is explained, agreed — once the phenomenology is nailed down, "unidentified" no longer covers the whole case, only the leftover attribution gap. Bundling both under one word is where the sleight happens: "we cannot make a definitive determination" is true of essentially every distant point of light ever filmed, and dressing that triviality as an open question is how a solved image stays a headline.

A modern cooled MWIR tracker can detect and lock a thermal contrast of a fraction of a degree from tens of miles — far beyond the range at which it can characterize what it locked. Every improvement in sensitivity widens the shell of space in which things are detectable but not resolvable, and everything inside that shell becomes, by procedure, a UAP report. The instrument is an unexplained-phenomena generator by geometry.
Then reporting policy multiplied it. Pre-2019, aircrew mostly didn't file on ambiguous contacts — stigma suppressed the paperwork. Post-Nimitz, reporting became mandated and destigmatized, so the same sensor output that was previously shrugged off now enters a formal pipeline, gets a case number, and eventually a FOIA-able file. Detection up, characterization flat, reporting way up: the catalog grows mechanically, with no change whatsoever in what's actually flying. AARO's own resolved-case statistics show the input distribution is balloons, drones, birds, satellites, and clutter, which is exactly what you'd predict if the growth is instrumental rather than phenomenal.

More pods, more drones, more maritime surveillance near contested coastlines means the unexplained residue grows indefinitely — not because the sky changed, but because we're interrogating it with instruments built to notice everything and identify little.
