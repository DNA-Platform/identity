What a type is and what a name resolves to

This one began as a conversation and became a book without anyone deciding it should, which is the reason I am porting the rest of them in too. We librarians take reference very seriously, and my conversations with AI are the substrate for most of my progress in this domain.

It opens on a word I made up for fun — ivitivity — and ends on the whole numbers. In between the suffix turns out to be two suffixes, subjectivity turns out to live in the context rather than in the subject, and a property of a type turns out to be the thing the whole of Semantic Reference Theory had been circling. The suffix is two suffixes: Latin -ivus (adjective: "disposed to, having the nature of") stacked on -itas (noun: "the state of"). Crucially -ivus attaches to the participial stem, so the base is always a completed act. And your three examples share a root: iacere, to throw.

  • object = thrown against
  • subject = thrown under
  • project = thrown forward

So the arc of -ivity is: an act → a standing disposition → a thing you can refer to. Something thrown once becomes a permanent tendency to throw that way, and then becomes an entity in its own right.

Why "productivity" broke the pattern: it isn't from project at all. It's producere/productus — a different verb. The real paradigm is intact and complete:

  • objection / objective / objectivity
  • subjection / subjective / subjectivity
  • projection / projective / projectivity

Projectivity is a live word — projective geometry. The reason it felt wrong is that -ion (the act) has crowded out -ivity (the disposition) in ordinary speech for project. For the other two, English kept both slots.

Now, ivitivity. Parse it: ivity → stem ivit- → ivitive → ivitivity. It's the property of being disposed to do what -ivity does.

And here's the payoff of the recursion: -ivity takes anything and returns an abstract property. Apply it to something that's already an abstract property and the type doesn't change. The operation is idempotent. Ivitivity is where the suffix hits its fixed point — the thingness of the tendency to make things out of tendencies.

Which is also a decent one-word name for the entire nominalizing habit of philosophy.

The research answer changes the picture: objectivity and subjectivity aren't English derivations at all.

They're both calques of German, and they entered English in the same document. The first use of "objectivity" as a noun in English appears in an unsigned review of Charles Villers' Philosophie de Kant in the Edinburgh Review of January 1803, written by Thomas Brown — and the OED dates its earliest evidence for both words to that same 1803 Edinburgh Review. Etymonline gives subjectivity as 1803, "absence of objective reality," popularized in Kantian terminology, comparing French subjectivité and German Subjektivität.

So the suffix isn't doing English work here. The pair arrived whole, as an imported technical correlative. Nobody in English ever took "subject" and productively suffixed it. That's why there's no slot for project — there was no Kantian Projektivität to import.

Your persistence point is exactly right, and Greek confirms it.

The Springer reference work traces the pair to Greek hypokeimenon and antikeimenon, and to the medieval verbs subiicio and obiicio — which mean the opposite of their post-Cartesian usages. Both Greek terms are -keimenon words: lying. Lying-under, lying-against. Postures of rest. Not throwings at all.

The Greek for "thrown forward" is problēma — that's where problem comes from. It's a separate word precisely because it names something transient: a thing in flight, an obstacle put in your path, something to be dealt with and disposed of. And problem takes no -ivity either. Two words for standing positions form a stable correlative pair; the throwing word doesn't join them, in Greek or in English.

Also: you're right about productivity, and it's not a secret. It's from producere/productus, a different verb entirely. It never had anything to do with project. What it does share is the shape: -ive names a disposition, -ivity names how much of it there is. Look at the class — conductivity, resistivity, permittivity, emissivity, reflectivity, selectivity, sensitivity, connectivity, productivity. Nearly all are magnitudes. Quantities of a capacity.

Which is why projectivity survives only in geometry, where it names the product of any number of perspectivities — a property a mapping definitively has or lacks. The ordinary noun "project" is a count noun for an artifact, and artifacts have no magnitude of disposition to measure.

The medieval inversion is worth having in hand, because it's the reason your grammatical intuition tracks. In Latin usage the two were a correlative pair, not opposites: the obiectum was what stood against the powers of the subiectum, and it was by the subject's faculties that the thing was an object at all — everything existing is a subject, and only subjects with cognitive powers can have objects. Objectivity in the old sense was the mode of being something has in the mind. Aquinas is a documented hinge: he advanced the scholastic account by integrating obiectum and emphasizing the intellect, producing a dual account where both subject and formal object give a science its unity.

Then Kant's translators flipped which pole carried "real," and the two nouns were minted in English together to name the flip.

Objectivity fails the test in its original sense — and this is the useful result.

A. Both work, ordinary English — the objective/objectivity pattern

Active/activity, passive/passivity, creative/creativity, sensitive/sensitivity, receptive/receptivity, productive/productivity, relative/relativity, positive/positivity, negative/negativity, inclusive/inclusivity, exclusive/exclusivity, connective/connectivity, normative/normativity.

B. -ive ordinary, -ivity technical-only — the projective/projectivity pattern

This turns out to be a big and coherent class, and it's dominated by physics and math:

  • conductive/conductivity, resistive/resistivity, reflective/reflectivity, emissive/emissivity, absorptive/absorptivity, refractive/refractivity, diffusive/diffusivity, expansive/expansivity
  • retentive/retentivity and coercive/coercivity — both are hysteresis-loop quantities: retentivity is the magnetization remaining when the external field is removed, coercivity the reverse field needed to drive it back to zero. Note coercive in ordinary English means bullying, and coercivity has nothing to do with that. The technical sense is a separate root of the word.
  • transitive/transitivity, associative/associativity, commutative/commutativity, injective/injectivity, primitive/primitivity
  • expressive/expressivity — in genetics, the degree to which a phenotype is expressed by individuals with a given genotype, distinguished from penetrance, which is the proportion of carriers showing it at all. Expressivity is the intensity axis.

C. -ive fine, -ivity blocked

Expensive, massive, pensive, furtive, restive, decisive, persuasive, offensive, impressive, deceptive, attractive, repetitive, intuitive, addictive. No expensivity, no persuasivity, no attractivity.

And the block isn't random. -ness took every one of them, and the -ness words all measure how it strikes an observer: attractiveness, persuasiveness, impressiveness, decisiveness. That's the interested reading — it can't be stated without someone being struck. Which is exactly your statelessness condition failing.

The minimal pairs prove it. Where both forms survive, they've split along that line:

  • expressiveness (how vividly it comes across) vs expressivity (the measured degree)
  • selectiveness (fussiness) vs selectivity (a receiver's rejection of adjacent channels)
  • productiveness (fruitfulness) vs productivity (output per input)
  • sensitiveness (touchiness) vs sensitivity (response per unit stimulus)
  • objectiveness vs objectivity

Same pattern every time. -ness = as it strikes. -ivity = as it stands.

D. -ivity exists but isn't a disposition at all

Captivity, nativity, festivity. Captivity is imposed from outside and only lasts while the imposing continues — the clean-table failure exactly. Nativity is an event. Festivity is a countable occasion; you can have three festivities.

These are the ones that lost statelessness, and each lost it in a different way: imposed, punctual, enumerable. In each case the noun stopped naming a property and became a thing that happens or a condition you're put in.

So the working rule your criterion generates: -ivity goes wherever -ive names something that holds at the thing while nothing happens. When the base needs an observer to register it, -ness takes the slot. When the base needs an event, you get captivity and festivity instead. When the base is a bare structural fact, you get the physics list — and that's where the suffix is most at home.

Which gets you exactly what a finite theory should say about an unbounded thing: the demand is unbounded, the domain at any time is not. N is never complete, and never inconsistent — the frontier is open by construction, which is your existing shape rather than a special case for arithmetic.

And it's better than Peano rather than a workaround. Peano needs infinity as an axiom because S manufactures. Here nothing manufactures; each part obliges. Potential infinity, in the Aristotelian sense, but derived from a uniqueness clause rather than stipulated. What's actual is what's been named.
