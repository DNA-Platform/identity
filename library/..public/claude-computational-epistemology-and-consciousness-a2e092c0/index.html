MoC7 opens today. It runs October 12–16 at the University of Copenhagen, at the HC Ørsted Institute on North Campus. This year's themes are philosophical foundations, methodologies and measurements, AI/LLMs and consciousness science, and phenomenology. Organizers are also running collaborative sessions aimed at a methodological and conceptual consensus, a nod to the Copenhagen interpretation.
Megan Peters (keynote). She is Associate Professor of Cognitive Sciences at UC Irvine and incoming faculty at UCL. Her core program is how the brain represents and uses uncertainty, how that supports metacognitive judgments of (mostly perceptual) decision quality, and how those processes might relate to phenomenology. Her background is Bayesian modeling of multisensory perception, followed by a postdoc shift into metacognition.
Some anchor papers you may see referenced:
Peters, Thesen, Ko et al. (2017), showing that perceptual confidence neglects decision-incongruent evidence in the brain
Peters (2022), Neurosci Biobehav Rev, on the canonical computations generating phenomenal experience
Her 2026 paper, "How to discover the natural kind of consciousness and test for its presence under population shifts", is the likeliest backbone of an AI-facing talk.

That's Jonathan Mason (AMCS), 11:20–11:40, on the Mathematical Consciousness Hypothesis.
From the abstract:
Three hypotheses.
MCH: consciousness is a mathematical property.
WMCH: a weaker version of the same claim.
NMCH: both are false, so consciousness has fundamentally non-mathematizable properties.
Claim: every theory, model, or measure of consciousness either picks philosophical foundations explicitly or implies them after the fact. Commonly held assumptions can turn out to be philosophically profound.
Method: he uses abstraction layers and substrate independence to clarify what makes a property truly "mathematical." The focus goes beyond functionalism to how specific mathematical structures embed in physical substrates, with Turing-machine universality as the motivating example.
Results: he proves results about whether consistent formal, system-level measures and models of consciousness can exist under each hypothesis, framed within ontic structural realism.
Next at 11:40 is Gaspard Fougea (ENS Paris-Saclay), "A computational framework for the global neuronal workspace hypothesis." He formalizes GNW using timed automata and a refinement relation, and derives new properties that follow logically from the theory's text.

The 12:00–12:20 talks:
Aud 2, Johannes Kleiner (Bamberg): "Towards Direct Tests for Consciousness in Non-Human Animals, Infants, and AI." He argues current tests are indirect, because they rely on a known link between consciousness and the measured property. He proposes direct tests built from covariates that need no such known correlation, grounded in the mathematics of evaluation research.
Aud 3, Marcin Moskalewicz (IDEAS, Warsaw): "AI-induced psychosis? Safety check." He adapts Ackerman's Delegate Game into a metacognitive experiment. The question is whether LLMs recognize a user's prodromal state and the limits of their own competence, and hand off to a human therapist rather than staying "helpful."
Aud 4, Krisztina Jedlovszky (UCL): "Computational Natural Language Analysis Reveals Psilocybin-Induced Changes to Meaning-Making." In 40 participants, inner-speech sampling showed that after psilocybin, self-referential and meaning words increased, semantic-network modularity decreased, and integration rose. These shifts predicted wellbeing.
Aud 5, Marc Ebner (Greifswald): "On Consciousness Thresholds." This is an information-theoretic account. Information lives in a high-dimensional space and must be projected into a low-dimensional one. It stays meaningful only if distances are preserved up to a factor, which yields thresholds for consciousness.
Lunch follows at 12:20, then John O'Keefe at 13:30.

What is the project? What is the discussion?
Claude
11:34 UTC, 12 October 2026
It's the conference's big experiment this year. Instead of the usual free-form small-group discussions, every discussion session works toward a shared roadmap for consciousness science, possibly ending in the field's first large collective paper.
What "consensus" means here: agreement on the map of possibilities, not on a winning theory. Each session takes one aspect of the roadmap and asks:
What research programs exist, and which should exist?
What is each trying to achieve, and what counts as success?
What deliverables, methods, and milestones would it need?
Where do programs conflict, and where do they complement each other?
How it runs: small groups meet in classrooms A101–A112 on HCØ's first floor. There are sessions every day: Monday 15:00, Tuesday 10:00 and 14:00, Wednesday 10:00, and Thursday 10:00 and 14:00. Friday's 15:00 plenary closes it out. Sessions may be recorded and summarized as input to the paper.
Signorelli's 14:30 talk explains the mechanics and probably how groups are assigned. Today's 15:00 session is the first one. The working document is a shared Google Doc linked from the online programme.
