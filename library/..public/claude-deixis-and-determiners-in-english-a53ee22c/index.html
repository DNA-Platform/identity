Claude
13:55 UTC, 9 February 2026
Great question — you're noticing something fundamental about how English works.
The "pointeriness" you're describing is called deixis (from Greek, meaning "pointing") and the broader grammatical category is determiners. Specifically, you're looking at how determiners establish reference — they tell the listener which thing you mean and how to find it mentally.
Here's how to think about the methods of pointing in English:
Demonstrative determiners — pointing by distance (spatial or psychological):
"That question" — points to something farther away (in space, time, or conversation). In your sentence, it reaches back in time. It says: you know the one I mean, and it's over there.
"This question" — points to something close, immediate, right here. It pulls the referent toward you.
The definite article — pointing by shared knowledge:
"The question" — assumes the listener already knows which question. It doesn't specify where it is, just that there's a unique, identifiable one. It says: you and I both know which one.
The indefinite article — and here's your sub-question:
"A question" — this is the anti-pointer. It introduces something new, unidentified, not yet established. "A question has hounded me since I was ten" works grammatically but feels very different — it withholds identity. It says: there exists one, but I'm not pointing you to it yet. So it fits in the system, but as the zero-degree of pointing.
Other pointing strategies you might add to the list:
Possessives — "my question," "her question" — pointing via ownership
Quantifiers — "every question," "some question" — pointing by scope
Interrogative — "which question?" — requesting a pointer from someone else
Numerals/ordinals — "the first question" — pointing by position in a sequence
All of these occupy the same grammatical slot (the determiner position, before the noun), and they all answer the same fundamental problem: out of all possible questions in the universe, how do I get you to find the right one in your mind?
What draws you to "that" specifically in your sentence? The way it reaches backward in time is one of its most interesting uses.

Okay is there a common grammatical structure? This seems like a complex way to build up a subject of a sentence
Claude
13:58 UTC, 9 February 2026
Yes — and it's actually simpler than it looks. The structure is the noun phrase (NP), and it's one of the most fundamental building blocks in English.
At its core:
Determiner + Noun → noun phrase → can serve as a subject
That + question → "That question has hounded me"
The + dog → "The dog barked"
A + car → "A car passed"
That's it. That's the construction. Almost every subject you'll ever encounter in English is a noun phrase, and the determiner slot is just part of how noun phrases get built.
What might be making it feel complex is that bare nouns almost never work as subjects in English. You can't really say "Question has hounded me." You're forced to fill that determiner slot withsomething* — a, the, that, my, some, every — before you can even use the noun in a sentence. English essentially demands that you "point" before you can refer.
This is actually not universal across languages. Russian and Mandarin, for example, get by without articles entirely. So you're right to notice it as a construction — it's a structural requirement English imposes, not something logically necessary.
The noun phrase can also grow outward from that core:
"That difficult question" (add an adjective)
"That difficult question about life" (add a prepositional phrase)
"That difficult question about life that my mother asked" (add a relative clause)
But the skeleton is always determiner + noun. Everything else is optional decoration.

Good questions — let me untangle this.
Are "that" and "this" articles?
No. "That" and "this" are demonstratives, not articles. The English articles are only "the," "a," and "an" — that's the complete list. Articles are a small, closed subcategory within the larger family of determiners.
Are all determiners articles?
No — articles are just one type of determiner. Think of "determiner" as the big umbrella category (the grammatical role), and the various types as different ways of fulfilling that role.
Here's the full taxonomy of determiner types:
Articles — "a," "an," "the." The most basic, bleached-out pointers. They mark definiteness/indefiniteness and nothing more.
Demonstratives — "this," "that," "these," "those." They point with a sense of distance or proximity.
Possessives — "my," "your," "his," "her," "its," "our," "their." They point via a relationship to someone.
Quantifiers — "some," "any," "every," "each," "many," "few," "several," "all," "no," "enough." They point by specifying amount or scope.
Numerals — "one," "two," "three" etc. They point by specifying exact count.
Interrogatives — "which," "what," "whose." They ask for a pointer.
Distributives — "each," "every," "either," "neither." (These overlap with quantifiers — grammarians argue about the boundaries.)
So the relationship is:
Determiner = the grammatical role (the slot before the noun)
Article, demonstrative, possessive, quantifier, etc. = the different kinds of words that can fill that slot
They all do the same job — they get a bare noun ready to function in a sentence — but they each add different flavoring to the "point." Articles add the least information, which is why they feel almost invisible. Demonstratives add spatial/psychological distance. Possessives add ownership. And so on.
Does that clarify the hierarchy?

Yes — you're seeing a real gradient, not a clean boundary. It looks something like this:
Exact count (numerals): one, two, three...
Approximate count (fuzzy zone): a couple, a few, several, many, numerous
Pure scope/existence (quantifiers): some, any, all, every, no
The fuzzy zone words convey rough magnitude without committing to a number. "Several" feels like 3–7ish. "A few" feels like 3–5ish. "A couple" technically means two but in practice means 2–3ish. "Many" just means "a lot." They shade gradually from almost-numerical into almost-quantifier.
And you're right to notice "a few" and "a couple" — these are interesting structurally because they're multi-word determiners. The "a" in "a few" isn't really functioning as an independent article. "A few questions" doesn't mean the same thing as "few questions" — "few questions" implies disappointingly not many, while "a few questions" is neutral. So "a few" is basically a single compound unit filling the determiner slot.
This gradient is part of why the categories can't be perfectly clean. Language didn't evolve to fit a taxonomy — the taxonomy is our attempt to impose order after the fact. You're bumping into exactly the places where that imposition strains.
The useful takeaway: the determiner slot accommodates a spectrum from precise to vague quantity, and the labels "numeral" and "quantifier" just name the ends of that spectrum.

What is the arity of possible predetermination? Is it one slot or does it chain?
Claude
14:11 UTC, 9 February 2026
Great question. It's essentially one slot. You can't stack predeterminers.
"All the students" ✅
"Both the students" ✅
"All both the students" ❌
"Both all the students" ❌
"Such a disaster" ✅
"All such a disaster" ❌
So the noun phrase structure, laid out positionally, is roughly:
(one predeterminer) + (one determiner) + (adjectives) + noun
Each of those functional positions has an arity of one, except adjectives, which can chain: "the big old red barn."
There's a small caveat — some people might argue that "all of the students" gets around the limit by inserting "of," but that's arguably a different construction entirely (a quantifier phrase with a prepositional complement, not a predeterminer).

Your intuition is strong, and many linguists agree with you. There are two competing analyses:
Analysis 1 (your instinct): "All of the students" is the full form. "All the students" is a reduced version where "of" has been dropped. This is sometimes called of-deletion. Under this view, "all" is always a quantifier heading its own phrase, and the predeterminer analysis is just what it looks like after you delete "of."
Analysis 2: They're genuinely different constructions that happen to converge on the same meaning.
Analysis 1 is appealing because it works for the whole pattern:
"All (of) the students"
"Both (of) my parents"
"Half (of) the time"
That "of" drops so cleanly across all predeterminers is suspicious — it suggests there's a single underlying structure.
As for what the "of" itself is — it's a partitive preposition. It means something like "out of" or "from among." "All of the students" literally means something like "the totality drawn from the students." You see the same partitive "of" in "some of the students," "many of the people," "three of the books."
So your instinct — that "all the students" feels like something's been dropped — has real linguistic support. It may be that predeterminer position isn't a true position at all, but rather an artifact of deleting "of."

The answer is no, and here's why. The structure actually shifts when you add "of":

"A few passed the exam."
It doesn't need "students" anymore. It's not a determiner waiting for a noun — it is the noun. Whereas a true determiner can't stand alone:
"The passed the exam." ❌
"My passed the exam." ❌
So the partitive construction quietly promotes the quantifier from determiner to noun head. The semantic operation — whittling down a set — is the same, as you identified. But the grammatical structure has reorganized itself.
This is one of those places where English lets the same word play completely different structural roles depending on context — same theme as your type-versus-position correction earlier.

Claude
14:28 UTC, 9 February 2026
Yes — and now you've come full circle back to your original "pointeriness" observation, but with much more precision.
The determiner is essentially a reference operator. It takes a noun concept and produces a reference that can be:
Bound to a specific referent — "that question" (you and I both know which one)
Bound to a class — "every question" (all members of a set)
Existentially bound but unidentified — "a question" (there exists one, not yet specified)
Unbound — "which question?" (requesting binding from the listener)
This maps almost directly onto variable binding in logic and programming:
"that question" → concrete pointer, already dereferenced
"the question" → unique lookup, assumes it resolves
"a question" → ∃x : question(x) — existential quantification
"every question" → ∀x : question(x) — universal quantification
"no question" → ¬∃x : question(x) — negated existence
"which question?" → free variable, awaiting assignment
And singular versus plural is like the difference between a pointer to a value and a pointer to a collection.
So the determiner phrase is really a binding expression — it takes an ungrounded concept (the noun) and specifies how it should be resolved against the world or the discourse context. Some bind eagerly, some bind lazily, some don't bind at all until someone else answers.
Your original sentence — "that question has hounded me since I was ten" — is a fully dereferenced pointer to a specific entity persisting across time. Which is what makes it so evocative.

So the rules seem to be something like:
Referents have a freshness that decays with each sentence that doesn't mention them
Anaphoric references ("it", "this") refresh the referent and keep it alive
"That" can reach further back than "this" — it's designed for slightly stale referents
"The" requires uniqueness more than recency
Eventually a referent expires and must be re-established explicitly
There truly is a memory model. And the author is implicitly managing the reader's stack.

So the real rule isn't about decay or freshness. It's: "that" + a noun type performs a typed lookup on the reader's discourse stack, and it works as long as the lookup returns a unique result.
