Eugene Hutz, from Gogol bordello the band. He is Ukrainian, but does he have any actual documented connection to gypsy culture or is that just something he has adopted to help US fans relate to his idea of punk
Claude

Searched the web for “Eugene Hutz Gogol Bordello Roma gypsy heritage ancestry”
He has a documented, real connection — it's not adopted for US audiences.
The lineage: Hütz was born Yevhen Nikolaiev-Symonov in Boyarka, Ukraine, in 1972. His father was Ukrainian-Lithuanian, and his mother was half Servitka Roma (Servo Roma), a subgroup of Romani. So he's a quarter Roma by descent — a real bloodline, not a costume.
The wrinkle that makes your question sharp: it was hidden, not lived. Because of discrimination against Roma, his mother's ethnic heritage was kept secret until Hütz was a teenager. He became aware of his extended Roma family in rural Ukraine as a teenager, and embraced it as an identity. The deepening came through circumstance: when the Chernobyl accident happened in 1986, the family fled to the Carpathian Mountains in western Ukraine where his mother's Roma relatives lived, and during that stay in the Gypsy camp he began melding the punk influences of his adolescence with Romani music. He later documented a pilgrimage through Roma camps from the Carpathians toward Siberia in the 2007 film The Pied Piper of Hützovina.
So the honest framing is: genuine ancestry, but an identity recovered and amplified in adulthood rather than one he grew up immersed in. That gap is exactly why he's contested within Romani circles, not by skeptical US fans. He's seen as a polarizing figure among international Romani rights activists because of what they regard as stereotypical displays of Romani culture, and the director of the Kiev Gypsy Theatre objected to his brand of Gypsy-fusion music.
The "gypsy punk" packaging is real branding — the band formed in NYC and the genre label is partly marketing — but it sits on top of actual Servitka Roma descent and a personal reconnection to it. It's adopted in the sense of consciously chosen and theatrically heightened, but not invented.

Where did the name eigene hutz come from? Is it more of a pen and stage name?
Claude
17:47 UTC, 7 June 2026
Searched the web for “Eugene Hütz name origin mother's maiden name real name Nikolaiev-Symonov”
It's a real family name that he adopted as a stage name — so it's both, in a specific way.
His birth name is Yevhen Oleksandrovych Nikolaiev-Symonov. He later moved to New York and took on his mother's maiden name of Hütz — this happened in 1997, when he moved to New York, and "Hütz" is pronounced "Hoodz." "Eugene" is just the Anglicized form of Yevhen/Evgeny.
So it's not invented out of nothing the way a typical stage name is. It's a maternal-line surname he chose to front with instead of his patronymic Nikolaiev name. The catch is the sources disagree on what kind of name "Hütz" actually is — some call it his mother's Ukrainian maiden name, others her German maiden name. Given the umlaut spelling and German pronunciation guide that follows him everywhere, the "German" attribution may be the band-era styling rather than a clean fact, and I wouldn't treat the ethnic label on it as settled.
The pattern is consistent with how he built the whole Gogol Bordello persona: real raw material — actual Roma ancestry, an actual family surname — selected and stylized for the stage. He picked the maternal name that pointed toward the heritage he was reclaiming, rather than keeping the Russian-sounding paternal one. So "Eugene Hütz" functions as a stage name, but every component is genuine; he curated rather than fabricated.

You're right on the etymology, and I should walk back how confidently I gave you "Hoodz."
On the name: Yevhen (Ukrainian) / Yevgeny (Russian) is the direct cognate of Eugene — all from Greek Eugenios, "well-born." That's why Пушкин's Евгений Онегин comes into English as Eugene Onegin. So "Eugene" is the conventional equivalent, and that's almost certainly why he picked it. But your ear isn't wrong: phonetically the first syllable is "Yev-/Ev-," much closer to Evan than to the "Yoo-" of Eugene. The mapping is etymological convention, not sound. (Evan is actually unrelated — it's a Welsh form of John — it just happens to sound nearer.)
On Hütz: hold off on rewriting your brain just yet, because my sources don't agree and I passed one along too cleanly. The "Hoodz" came from a single fan-wiki. Wikipedia gives the German IPA [hʏts], which is closer to "hüts" — the ü as in German über or French tu (a front rounded vowel English doesn't really have), ending in a crisp "ts," not "dz." Those are meaningfully different pronunciations, and I don't have a clip of the man himself saying it to break the tie.
If you want the actual answer rather than two competing transcriptions, the move is to find him say his own name in an interview — that's the only authority that settles it. The "Hoodz" I gave you was not earned.

The umlaut is the official styling — but enforcement is loose, and that inconsistency is itself telling.
Where it's used: songwriting credits carry it. Wikipedia lists "When the Trickster Starts a-Poking" (2002), the band's first single, as written by Eugene Hütz. Databases follow suit — TMDB, Wikidata, and IMDb-derived entries render him "Eugene Hütz." Film coverage keeps it too: he's credited around Everything Is Illuminated (2005), Madonna's Filth and Wisdom (2008), and the documentary whose very title leans on it — The Pied Piper of Hützovina (2006/2007).
Where it drops: press and even his own promo flatten it constantly. The SPIN/Yahoo profile photo caption reads "Eugene Hutz" while the body text writes "Hütz" in the same article — both spellings, same page. NPR's headline says "Hutz," the article body says "Hütz." His own 2025 covers project, the umlaut appears in the official title styling ("Eugene Hütz Unveils...") but the tracklist credits a remix to plain "E. Hutz" and "Eugene Hutz." So even his camp doesn't apply it uniformly.
Why the looseness: it's partly a keyboard-and-ASCII problem (URLs, file metadata, and a lot of CMS systems strip diacritics), and partly that the umlaut is a stylistic flourish rather than a legal-document feature. His birth name has no umlaut — it's a Cyrillic name (Николаев-Симонов), and "Hütz" is the Latin-script stage rendering of his mother's family name. The dots are an aesthetic choice layered onto a transliteration, which is exactly why nobody treats them as load-bearing.
One more wrinkle worth flagging for your mental model: the Russian and Ukrainian Wikipedias file him under Гудзь / Гудзь (romanized roughly "Hudz"), not Хюц. That points back at the pronunciation problem from before — the native-script spelling the Slavic encyclopedias chose is closer to a "Hooj/Hudz" sound than to the German "hüts." So the umlaut may be doing decorative work that pulls the spelling away from how the name actually sounds in his own languages. I still wouldn't lock in a pronunciation without hearing him say it.

The path:
Plug the iPhone in with a USB cable to the data USB port — on a Forester it's the one in the front tray/console marked with a little phone or CarPlay icon, not the charge-only ports. CarPlay over cable is the reliable route. (Newer Foresters, roughly 2020+, can do wireless CarPlay, but get it working wired first.)
First connection, your phone will ask permission and the car may prompt "Use CarPlay" — say yes on both.
On the phone, confirm CarPlay is allowed: Settings → General → CarPlay → your car should appear. Also make sure Siri is enabled (Settings → Siri & Search) — CarPlay won't launch without it.
Once CarPlay is up, the Subaru screen switches to the CarPlay home grid, and Google Maps will be one of the icons there (it's already installed on your phone, so nothing to download).
If you plug in and nothing changes, the usual culprits are a charge-only cable or the wrong USB port — try a known data cable in the marked port.

I found an adapter for the cigarette thing, but it’s not working. Does it need to heat up or something?
Claude
12:31 UTC, 8 June 2026
No — a 12V adapter doesn't heat up. The cigarette-lighter socket in your car is just a power outlet; the adapter plugs in and should work instantly. Nothing needs to warm up. (The actual lighter element heating up is a different, old thing — your adapter isn't that.)
If it's not working, it's almost always one of these:
Ignition is off. On most Foresters that socket only has power when the key is in the ACC or ON position (or the engine running). If the car's fully off, the socket is dead. Turn the key to ACC and check.
Blown fuse for that socket. Very common — a previous high-draw device pops it. There's a fuse box behind a panel near your left knee / lower dash. If the socket is dead even with ignition on, this is the likely cause.
Loose plug. These sockets are deep; the adapter has to seat firmly enough for the center pin and side springs to make contact. Push it in with a slight twist and wiggle to see if a light flickers on.
The adapter itself. Many have a little LED or their own fuse in the tip that unscrews. No LED = either no power reaching it or the adapter's own fuse is blown.
Quick way to isolate it: does the adapter have an indicator light? If the light is off, the problem is the socket (ignition or fuse). If the light is on but your phone/device isn't charging, the problem is the adapter's USB output or your cable.
What's the adapter — a USB charger? And is its light on when plugged in?
