Buy elsewhere → Send to Atomic → Swap
This is a pretty standard approach. If you buy BTC on a KYC exchange (Coinbase, Kraken, whatever), send it to your Atomic wallet address, then swap within Atomic - the swaps themselves don't require additional KYC for most pairs. Your initial BTC purchase is KYC'd at the source, but your subsequent swapping and holding activity in Atomic stays private to you.
The tradeoff is that the exchange where you bought knows you withdrew to that specific address. Depending on your threat model, that may or may not matter.
Device changes and wallet recovery
Your funds aren't actually stored "on" your device - they're on the respective blockchains. Your device just holds the private keys that let you access them. Atomic Wallet is non-custodial, meaning all private keys and the 12-word seed phrase live only on your device, encrypted locally.
When you set up Atomic (or any non-custodial wallet), you get a 12-word seed phrase. That phrase mathematically derives all your private keys. If you get a new phone or computer:
Install Atomic on the new device
Choose "Restore Wallet" instead of "Create Wallet"
Enter your 12 words
All your wallets and balances reappear
Your 12-word backup phrase is the only way to recover your wallet if you lose access. If you lose it, you will permanently lose access to your funds since Atomic Wallet does not store user data.
So the critical thing: write down that seed phrase offline and keep it somewhere secure. It's the master key to everything. Lose the phrase and lose the device = funds gone forever.

This is a common misconception. The US is one of only two countries in the world (Eritrea is the other) that taxes based on citizenship, not residency.
The reality:
As a US citizen, you must file a tax return every year regardless of where you live or how many days you spend in the US. You could live in Georgia for 20 years and never set foot in America - you still file.
What changes when you're abroad:
The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) lets you exclude up to ~$126,000 of earned income (salary, freelance work) from US taxes if you meet either the bona fide residence test or physical presence test (330 days outside the US in a 12-month period).
But here's the thing - investment gains aren't earned income. They're capital gains. The FEIE doesn't apply to them. So if your crypto thesis works out and you sell at a profit in 10 years, the US wants its cut regardless of where you're living.
For your current situation:
If you're just buying and holding with no sales, you're not realizing gains, so there's nothing to tax yet anyway. You'd still technically need to file, but if your only US-connected income is below the filing threshold, it's minimal paperwork.
The real headache:
FBAR - if your foreign accounts (including crypto on foreign exchanges) exceed $10,000 at any point, you must report them. Penalties for not filing are brutal.

You're not wrong about double taxation in general - the US has tax treaties and foreign tax credits specifically to prevent paying twice on the same income.
The problem is the math:
How foreign tax credits work:
You pay taxes to Georgia, then credit that amount against what you owe the US. So if you owed $20,000 to the US and paid $15,000 to Georgia, you'd only owe the US $5,000.
Your situation:
Georgia charges 0%. So you pay $0 to Georgia. You credit $0 against your US liability. You owe the US... the full amount.
The foreign tax credit protects you from paying more than the higher of the two rates. It doesn't let you pick the lower rate. Since Georgia's rate is 0%, the US rate is effectively what you pay.
The options:
Just hold forever and never realize gains (no sale = no taxable event)
Borrow against your crypto instead of selling (this is what rich people do)
Renounce citizenship - but there's an "exit tax" where they treat all your assets as sold on the day you renounce, so you'd owe capital gains then anyway
Move to Puerto Rico under Act 60 (0% on gains accrued after you move, and you stay a US citizen)
For a decade-horizon hold strategy, option 1 is basically what you're doing anyway. The problem only materializes when you want to actually use the money.
