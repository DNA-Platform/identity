This is a great situation to be learning Russian — having native speakers around you is one of the biggest accelerators, and your relationship with Ana gives you real motivation and genuine conversational context, which research consistently identifies as the single most powerful driver of retention.
Let me break this down into what the science actually says, what tools make sense, and how to build a realistic schedule.
What Actually Works (The Science)
The research is pretty clear that there are a few things that genuinely move the needle. The most effective framework combines formal practice (grammar, flashcards, structured drills) as the place to first learn a word or pattern, then genuine communication with real people to repeat those patterns in enough varied contexts that they become automatic and effortless. Apps alone won't get you there — you can diligently spend time every day learning new words and start to feel success, then meet a native speaker and find you struggle stringing together a normal conversation.
The two most evidence-backed mechanics are:
Spaced repetition — you'll remember a word much better if you look at it once a day for five days rather than five times in the same day. Ebbinghaus also found people remember things better when linked to personal experiences, which is where mnemonics help.
Comprehensible input — the idea that you acquire language by understanding messages slightly above your current level, not by brute-force memorization. This is why having Ana around is gold: real conversation where you're trying to understand something that matters is more effective than any app.
The Russian-Specific Challenge
Russian has two major obstacles at the start that aren't an issue with most European languages: the Cyrillic alphabet and the case system (six grammatical cases that change word endings based on their role in a sentence). You need to deal with Cyrillic first — it takes most people only 3–7 days of focused effort and after that it stops being a barrier. Cases are harder and take months, but you don't need to master them before you can speak — you'll internalize them gradually through exposure.
Apps: What to Use and When
No single app covers everything. Here's a practical stack for your situation:
Phase 1 — Cyrillic (first 1-2 weeks): Russian Cyrillic in 3 Hours is a free video-based app that teaches the Russian alphabet through contextual learning by connecting Cyrillic letters to English cognates and familiar patterns. It's strictly an alphabet tool — you watch, you learn, then you delete it after a few days and move on.
Phase 1–2 — Structured foundation: Rocket Russian uses interactive audio lessons where an English-speaking moderator guides you through real conversations between fluent Russian speakers — no other Russian app comes as close to simulating real-world conversation. This type of active, contextual learning is extremely effective for improving both receptive and expressive language skills. It's a one-time purchase (~$150) rather than a subscription. Alternatively, Babbel's 10-minute easily digestible lessons are designed to take you from no knowledge to basic conversations, with a structured approach where each lesson builds on the last, often available for under $10/month.
Ongoing — Vocabulary retention: Anki (free, desktop and mobile) is the gold standard spaced repetition flashcard system. Digital flashcards with spaced algorithms support the combination of related and unrelated vocabulary, enhancing retention by fostering both differentiation and semantic linking. There are pre-built Russian Anki decks covering the 1,000 most common words — that's your fastest path to conversational competency.
Ongoing — Listening immersion: RussianPod101 has an incredible number of video and podcast lessons covering just about every aspect of the language at every level, making it one of the better apps for listening practice, which also covers grammar, vocabulary, and cultural context.
Tutors: Do You Need One, and How to Find Them
Yes, a tutor is worth it — probably 1-2x per week once you have even basic alphabet and 50–100 words. The tutor's job at the beginning is to give you corrective feedback on pronunciation and force you into actual production (speaking), which no app does well.
The two best platforms are italki.com and Preply.com. Both have hundreds of Russian tutors. Pricing on italki ranges from ~$8–$30/hour depending on the tutor's experience.
How to filter when searching:
Look for tutors with a linguistic or pedagogical background, not just native speakers. Not every native speaker is a language teacher — a good teacher should have a linguistic education and professional training in teaching language skills, and should be able to serve as an intermediary language while you're a beginner.
Filter for tutors who explicitly mention the "communicative approach" or "comprehensible input" — these align with the research.
Watch their intro video before booking.
What to say in your first message to a tutor:
"I'm an absolute beginner in Russian. I'm learning because I live with native Russian speakers and need conversational Russian for daily life. I want to focus on speaking and listening, not grammar drills. I'd like to start with survival phrases and work toward holding basic conversations within 3 months. Do you have experience with communicative/conversational approaches for beginners?"
Book a trial lesson with 2-3 tutors and pick the one whose teaching style clicks.
A Realistic Schedule
With consistent daily input, you can reach basic conversational competency in 6–12 months. The key is staying consistent, staying curious, and adjusting methods as you progress. Russian is officially categorized by the US Foreign Service Institute as a Category IV language (hardest tier), so expect ~600 hours to reach professional working proficiency — but you only need a fraction of that to hold daily conversations with Ana's family.
Here's a practical weekly structure you could actually commit to:
Daily (30–45 min total):
15 min Anki flashcard review (mornings, before you look at your phone for anything else)
15 min Rocket Russian or Babbel lesson
5–10 min passive listening (RussianPod101 during a walk or meal)
2x per week:
45–60 min with an italki or Preply tutor
Daily opportunistic:
Use Ana. Ask her to say one thing to you in Russian each day and make you repeat it back. She's from St. Petersburg, which means you'll learn a clean, standard dialect — that's ideal.
The research is emphatic on one point: the most important thing is whether it's fun and you enjoy doing it, because any approach you stick to beats the perfect approach you abandon. So pick the app that feels engaging to you, not the one ranked highest on some list.

Once you can hold even basic conversations, Ana and her network become a free, always-on language lab that accelerates you far faster than any app. You just need enough structured foundation to break into that. The investment framing makes complete sense: spend money early to get past the activation barrier, then immersion does the heavy lifting.
Here are your concrete options, organized by how "anchored" the commitment is:
Tier 1 — Most Accountability: Dedicated Russian Schools with Coordinators
These feel closest to what you're describing — you get assigned a teacher, a curriculum, regular sessions, and actual homework that gets graded.
Russificate (russianschoolrussificate.com) — lesson plans are developed individually for each student based on goals, teachers have linguistic or philological education, and they assign a variety of homework tasks reviewed between sessions. The first lesson is a free trial, and payment comes after lessons, not before.
ExLinguo (exlinguo.com) — your course is supervised by an academic coordinator who designs a structured personal study plan. Your teacher monitors progress through regular tests and homework assessments, and you should expect about one hour of homework for each lesson booked. Homework correction time is already included in the lesson price, so no extra cost. This is close to a traditional school structure but done over Zoom.
Enjoy Russian (enjoyrussian.com) — a group option (2–6 people) that's significantly cheaper: €80/month for 8 lessons (2x per week), each lesson an hour, with a WhatsApp chat room for asking the teacher questions between sessions and homework after each class. Groups start every Monday. The group format also means you're accountable to showing up because others are waiting.
Tier 2 — High Structure via Italki/Verbling with the Right Teacher
This is the middle path — platform-based, but you deliberately set up a structured engagement rather than casual tutoring. The key is how you frame it when you hire the teacher.
Verbling has a built-in homework calendar, flashcard system, and filing system for lesson materials, which makes the accountability loop more concrete than italki alone. You tell a teacher upfront: "I want you to assign homework after every session, quiz me on it at the start of the next session, and give me a structured curriculum to follow — not just conversation practice." Most qualified teachers will do this happily if you ask; they just default to casual conversation if you don't specify.
What to say when hiring:
"I want this to work like a real course. Please give me homework after every session — vocabulary to memorize, sentences to write, audio to listen to. Quiz me at the start of each lesson before we move on. I'm surrounded by native Russian speakers, so I want to build fast enough that I can start practicing with them within 60 days."
Tier 3 — Self-Paced but University-Structured
Ohio State runs actual online Russian courses where students work independently with self-instructional materials, and at the end of each unit meet virtually with a trained instructor for a homework check, conversation practice, and oral exam — earning college credit hour by hour as they pass unit tests at 80% or better. This is probably overkill for your goals and slower than the above options, but it's worth knowing it exists if you ever want something with genuine academic credibility.
What I'd Actually Recommend for Your Situation
Given that you're in Georgia surrounded by Russian speakers and you want rapid progress with commitment anchors, the combination I'd suggest is:
ExLinguo or Russificate for your 2x/week structured sessions with homework — treat it like an appointment you've paid for and can't cancel. The coordinator model means someone is tracking you, which creates external accountability.
Then use the immersion around you as the bonus layer on top. Once you can say 50–100 things reliably, start using a simple rule: one thing per day, you only communicate with Ana in Russian, even if you butcher it. She's the ultimate teacher — she knows what you're trying to say and can correct you naturally in context, which is more valuable than any class.
The investment will compound fast because you're not learning a language in isolation — you're learning it while it's being spoken around you every day. The school gives you the framework; Ana's world is the practice environment. That's genuinely close to ideal conditions.
