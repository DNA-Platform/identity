One framing correction up front since it matters for everything else: Wise didn't have a US ADR before today. What they had was a direct LSE listing (ticker WISE) of the underlying UK plc shares since their 2021 IPO. Today's Nasdaq listing (WSE) is a direct dual listing of those same shares — not a depository receipt overlay. The shares are fully fungible across both venues, and as of last summer's EGM they actually moved their primary listing to Nasdaq, with London now the secondary.
That distinction shapes what can and cannot be different about the US listing.
What actually changes
The shares are the same instrument, so any persistent gap between WSE and WISE gets arbitraged out by market-makers moving shares between depositories. Intraday spreads will be basis points within a week. There is no separate WSE valuation — both lines reprice together. This is closer to Shopify (TSX/NYSE) than to a China A/H situation.
What does change:
Marginal buyer. US institutional flow, US sell-side coverage, US retail brokerage access. The thesis is that the US comp set — Visa, Mastercard, Adyen, Block, PayPal — anchors payments-infrastructure multiples higher than London ever did.
Reporting. Switched to USD / US GAAP for FY26. Net interest income and SBC will look subtly different.
Index eligibility. Russell 1000/3000 inclusion is plausible since Russell takes foreign private issuers if float and size qualify. S&P 500 is essentially off the table — UK domicile disqualifies them.
Options depth. US listed options will build out materially. Real hedging and structured-flow channels open up.
OwnWise. The customer-shareholder loyalty program announced today is a flywheel that converts users into holders. Small in dollar terms but structurally interesting as a retention/CAC mechanism.
What doesn't change
The business itself. Same regulatory footprint, same FX exposures, same take rate dynamics, same competitive set. This is a capital-markets event, not an operational one.
And critically — the supervoting structure didn't just survive the move, it got entrenched by it. The July 2025 EGM bundled the relisting authorization with a 10-year extension of the Class B 9:1 supervoting to 2036. Glass Lewis and PIRC opposed; co-founder Hinrikus publicly called for separate votes and was ignored; Andreessen Horowitz backed it; it passed at 75%+. Käärmann holds ~18% economically but his vote is contractually capped at one share below 50% of total exercisable votes.
The substantive consequence: hostile acquisition is structurally impossible until 2036 absent Käärmann's cooperation. Activist pressure on take rate ("raise fees, expand margin") or capital return ("pay a dividend") has no governance lever. That's a bull case if you believe in the founder's playbook (compounding low-fee infrastructure for a decade) and a bear case if you wanted takeover optionality.
Fundamentals (FY26 ended March 31, 2026)
Cross-border volume $243B, +31% YoY (accelerating)
Cross-border revenue $1.3B at ~0.52% average take rate
Card & other revenue $0.6B on $44B card spend (~1.4% take, mostly interchange)
Transaction revenue $1.9B (+22%)
Net interest on customer balances: $0.6B ($0.8B income − $0.2B expense) on $39B customer holdings
Net revenue $2.5B (+19%)
PBT margin at top of 13–16% guidance, so ~$400M
Structural read: ~76% of net revenue is transactional (volume × take), ~24% is rate-sensitive spread on the $39B float. The float economics are real — they hold customer money for free, deploy it in safe assets at policy rates — but it's a rate beta you don't control. In a sustained cutting cycle the NII line compresses 100–200bps of yield, and that flows straight to PBT because there's no offsetting cost.
At today's $15.96 open and ~1.22B total shares outstanding (1.02B Class A + 204M Class B), market cap is roughly $19.5B → ~7.8x trailing net revenue, ~50x trailing PBT. The comp-set shift is already substantially priced in.
What I'd actually expect different
A higher steady-state multiple, but globally — both lines reprice together. Better liquidity and narrower spreads. Russell passive flows over the next quarter or two. Different framing from US analysts (tech infrastructure rather than UK payments). No change to governance, no change to fundamentals, no change to fee dynamics.
Where I'd push back on the headline framing
The press is calling this a referendum on London as a venue ("London gets the listing, New York gets the logic" — European Business Magazine). That's overstated. The supervoting bundle makes it obvious that Wise specifically wanted to escape LSE governance standards, not LSE valuations. The UK has been tightening listing rules on dual-class shares; the US, post-Meta/Alphabet/Snap, has normalized them. Käärmann moved to the venue where his governance structure is legally and culturally compatible. The "deeper capital pool" framing is the press-release version of that.
The real question isn't "does the US listing change anything." It doesn't, fundamentally. It's whether Wise compounds at 20%+ net revenue growth for another 5–10 years with the founder controlling fee and capital policy, and whether ~50x PBT compensates you for rate-beta on a quarter of net revenue plus concentration in a single founder-controlled fintech. The US listing only changes who's setting that price each morning.

John isn't confused about whether he owned something — he probably did, but he might be confused about what to call it.
Yes, there was a Wise ADR on US markets before today. Wise established a sponsored Level 1 ADR program in September 2021, two months after the LSE IPO. It traded OTC under ticker WIZEY, ratio 1:1 with the underlying Class A ordinary share. So if John bought "Wise" through a US broker before today, that's almost certainly what he held — a real, sponsored, US-dollar-denominated instrument representing one Wise share, just trading on pink sheets rather than a major exchange.
What happens to WIZEY now is the interesting mechanical question. When a Level 1 sponsored ADR gets upgraded to a Level 2 (exchange-listed, no new capital raise) or Level 3 (with public offering) — which is essentially what today represents — the depositary bank typically handles the conversion seamlessly. WIZEY holders' positions get re-tickered to WSE on Nasdaq, same 1:1 ratio. John should see this in his account either already or within the next few settlement cycles. He may not even need to do anything; the broker handles it on the back end. Worth him just checking his Nasdaq positions and seeing if WSE has appeared.
On IBKR specifically — yes, John can just buy WISE.L directly. IBKR is one of the most direct-LSE-access-friendly retail brokers in the US. He can hold GBP cash, place orders on the LSE order book, settle in CREST, all from his US-based account. There's no need to use the US listing if he prefers the London one — they're economically the same exposure with a slight FX cost on entry and exit. The only operational reason to prefer WSE over WISE.L through IBKR is if he wants USD denomination and US tax reporting simplicity (no PFIC concern since Wise isn't a passive foreign investment, but 1099 vs. foreign reporting differs slightly).
Now the structural question — what a "dual listing" actually is.
Dual listings are a longstanding category, not a new thing. But there are several distinct mechanisms that all get lumped under that term, and they work differently:
True fungible cross-listing — same security registered in two clearing systems (Shopify on NYSE+TSX is the cleanest example). Shares move between depositories via standardized cross-border transfers.
Dual-Listed Companies (DLCs) — two separately listed legal entities tied together by contractual equalization agreements so shareholders of each get the same economics from one combined business. Rio Tinto, BHP historically, Unilever and Shell pre-unification. Complex, mostly being unwound by the companies that had them.
ADR-overlay listing — the home country trades ordinary shares, the US exchange trades ADRs that are fungible to the underlying through the depositary's deposit/withdrawal mechanism. This is how virtually every non-Canadian foreign company on a US exchange works: Toyota, Sony, ARM, Alibaba, Diageo, Shell post-unification, etc.
Direct foreign share listing on US exchange — genuinely rare. NYSE's Global Share program. Historically used by Canadian issuers (no ADR needed), Deutsche Bank, UBS, and a few others. Almost never used by UK plcs.
Wise is almost certainly mechanism #3 — an ADR-overlay. WSE on Nasdaq is technically an upgraded sponsored ADR (Level 2 or 3), and WISE on LSE is the underlying Class A ordinary share. They're economically equivalent and arbitraged within basis points by market-makers who can deposit/withdraw at the depositary bank to capture any spread. Worth caveating that I haven't pulled the F-6 filing to confirm whether they went direct-listing-of-ordinaries or ADR — UK plc direct-listing of ordinaries on Nasdaq would be unusual but not impossible. The economic answer is the same either way; the legal-wrapper answer would only matter for tax/voting nuance.
So when I said earlier "the shares are fungible," that's economically true. The operational reality is that there's an ADR wrapper sitting between the WSE ticker and the underlying ordinary share — but creation/redemption keeps prices tied tightly.
Why ADRs still exist if dual listings exist — because they're not separate categories. Most "dual listings" of foreign companies on US exchangesare ADRs. The ADR is the dual-listing mechanism for non-Canadian foreign issuers. The terminology people use is sloppy:
"Dual listing" describes the market structure (security tradable in two venues).
"ADR" describes the legal/mechanical wrapper that enables that structure for foreign-domiciled issuers.
The reason ADRs persist as a wrapper rather than direct foreign-share listings is cross-border clearing friction. US clearing runs through DTC; UK runs through CREST. Linking them directly for fungible ordinary shares requires a Global Share program-style arrangement that most foreign jurisdictions' market infrastructure isn't set up for. The ADR is the bridge — it lets the underlying shares stay in the home clearing system while a US bank issues a parallel certificate that trades in US clearing.
Your last question — do ADRs exist when the proxy provider isn't the issuer?
Yes, exactly that case. Those are unsponsored ADRs. Any US depositary bank (BNY Mellon, Citi, JPMorgan, Deutsche Bank are the four big ones) can establish an ADR facility for a foreign company without the company's permission or involvement, provided the underlying shares are freely tradable in the home market. The bank profits from custody fees and conversion spreads. The issuer has no agreement, no obligations, sometimes no awareness. These trade OTC, are usually thinly liquid, and from the issuer's perspective are slightly annoying — if the issuer later wants to do a sponsored program, SEC staff position is that the unsponsored facilities must be terminated first, which costs the issuer cancellation fees to the depositary banks.
Wise's WIZEY was already sponsored (Level 1) from 2021 onward, so there was no unsponsored mess to clean up before today's upgrade.
The clean way to think about it: ADR = "wrapper that lets a US bank issue a USD-denominated certificate representing foreign shares held in custody abroad." Sponsored vs unsponsored is whether the foreign company is in on the arrangement. Level 1/2/3 is where it trades (OTC / listed / listed-with-capital-raise). Wise has been on this ladder since 2021 and today climbed from Level 1 to Level 2 (or Level 3, depending on filing structure I haven't verified).
So John didn't own nothing. He owned WIZEY, and as of this week or shortly after, he owns WSE. If he wants the cleaner version, he can just buy WISE.L through IBKR and skip the wrapper entirely.

Strictly speaking it's wrong, but it's directionally pointing at something real. Specifically:

So the more accurate framing isn't "they have no plan." It's: they have a back-end-quiet stablecoin posture and no front-end stablecoin product. They're using the tech where useful as a settlement primitive but they haven't built consumer or business products around it, and their CEO continues to publicly position stablecoins as not-yet-better. Which means: at the corporate narrative layer, they're vulnerable to a competitor that leads with stablecoins.
Where the real stablecoin risk actually lives.
Stablecoins are a substitute at the settlement layer of the cross-border stack. They are not (yet) a substitute at the on-ramp, off-ramp, KYC, AML, multi-currency-account, debit card, or local-payout-rail layers — which is exactly what Wise spent 14 years building. So the simple "stablecoins kill Wise" framing is wrong.
The realistic threat decomposition:
Backend settlement substitution — low risk. Wise can adopt stablecoin rails internally without changing what customers see, capturing whatever cost savings exist. Banking rails are already cheap for Wise at scale, so the savings are modest.
B2B treasury — medium-high risk. This is where BVNK ($10B annualized), Bridge (Stripe), Circle's CPN, and Arc are competing directly with Wise Business, which is Wise's fastest-growing segment. Enterprise customers are more sensitive to settlement speed and programmability than consumers. This is the corridor where Wise's regulatory moat counts least and stablecoin programmability counts most.
Vertical-integration distribution attack — highest risk. Stripe+Bridge integrating cross-border payouts as a feature inside the Stripe stack. Meta+Stripe putting USDC creator payouts in Facebook/Instagram. The point isn't that stablecoins are cheaper; it's that Stripe-with-Bridge can offer Wise's product as one line item inside a payment processor that already has distribution Wise doesn't have. Wise has to be the destination; Stripe has to be the default.
The strategic question is whether Wise's licenses + customer relationships + multi-currency accounts are enough of a stack to keep being the destination, or whether they get reduced to a UX wrapper around commoditized rails. Käärmann's bet appears to be the former.

Activist pressure to raise take rate or pay dividends would destroy the thing that makes Wise work (the word-of-mouth-driven 70% organic acquisition). The stablecoin threat is real but I should be precise about its shape. It's not "a Stripe-Bridge-style competitor builds a better Wise faster than Wise can defend." That's the replication scenario and the moat handles it well. It's "stablecoin rails commoditize the settlement layer of cross-border payments, and Wise has to figure out whether it's still the destination customer or just a wrapper around commoditized rails."
