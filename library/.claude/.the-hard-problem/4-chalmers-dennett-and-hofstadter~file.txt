**ChatGPT**

They are fundamentally opposed at the level that matters.

Chalmers: zombies show that all physical/functional facts could be fixed while phenomenal consciousness is absent. So consciousness is not reductively explained by physical structure and function. In The Conscious Mind, he defines a zombie as a physical duplicate lacking conscious experience, and uses this to argue there is no a priori entailment from physical facts to phenomenal facts.

Dennett: the zombie idea is confused. His line is that once you really duplicate all the functional, cognitive, verbal, introspective, reactive machinery, there is no further missing “inner light.” He treats zombies as a bad intuition pump, not a serious metaphysical possibility. His 1995 paper is literally “The Unimagined Preposterousness of Zombies.”

Illusionism, especially in Dennett/Frankish form, says phenomenal consciousness as philosophers imagine it is not real; what is real is the system’s functional self-modeling, reporting, discrimination, attention, memory, etc. Frankish says illusionists may technically count us as “zombies” if zombies only lack phenomenal consciousness, but not as dark, empty beings, because we still have an “inner life” understood functionally.

Chalmers rejects that move. In his work on illusionism and the meta-problem, he treats illusionism as a serious rival but argues that our core beliefs about consciousness are constitutively connected to consciousness itself, not merely caused by independent functional mechanisms. So zombies would not have the same core phenomenal beliefs we have.

The comparative literature usually frames this as a clash between:

phenomenal realism:
There really is something-it-is-like, and zombies expose an explanatory/modal gap.

illusionism/type-A physicalism:
The “something extra” is a misleading concept; explain the reports and the seeming, and nothing remains.

The Stanford Encyclopedia summary puts the zombie debate exactly at the pressure point: if zombies are conceivable and conceivability implies possibility, the anti-physicalist argument succeeds; functionalists and Dennett-style physicalists deny this, because full functional duplication would already include consciousness.

So: they are not consistent unless one equivocates on “consciousness.” If consciousness means functional access, self-monitoring, reportability, attention, and behavioral organization, Dennett/illusionism can accept it and zombies become incoherent. If consciousness means irreducible phenomenal experience, Chalmers accepts it and Dennett/illusionism denies or deflates it. The dispute is partly semantic, but not merely semantic: it is a disagreement over whether the phenomenal datum is explanandum or illusion.
