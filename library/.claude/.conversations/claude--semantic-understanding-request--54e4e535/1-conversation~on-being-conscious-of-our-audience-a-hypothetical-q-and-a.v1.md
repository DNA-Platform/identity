**Q1:** I work with Integrated Information Theory, and I'm puzzled by your dismissal of emergence. IIT shows consciousness emerging from integrated information—phi—which can be measured. Why do we need symbols at all when integration explains the unity of conscious experience?

Your theory describes patterns that accompany consciousness without explaining why there's "something it's like" to be integrated. Our framework shows that symbols aren't optional additions but necessary for experience to exist at all. Integration might organize symbols, but without symbolic reference, you have patterns without meaning.

**Q1:** But we can calculate phi for any system. Your n³ relationships seem similar—both are measurable. What's the fundamental difference?

**A:** The difference is that phi measures structural properties while our synchronization measures semantic coherence. When we check if self-representation matches actual state, we're not measuring information flow but whether the system's claims about itself remain true. A system could have high integration while being completely deluded about its contents. Consciousness requires not just integration but accurate self-reference—knowing what you know.

**Q2:** Speaking of integration, Global Workspace Theory provides a functional account without invoking symbols or self-reference. Consciousness is simply global accessibility of information across processing modules. Your symbolic structures seem unnecessarily complex for what broadcasting achieves simply.

**A:** Global broadcasting explains access consciousness—why some information becomes reportable—but not phenomenal consciousness. Why does globally broadcast information feel like something rather than just being globally accessible? Your workspace processes information, but information isn't inherently meaningful. Our framework shows how information becomes symbolic through self-typed relationships. The workspace might be where symbols interact, but without semantic primitives, you're describing traffic patterns without explaining what makes the traffic meaningful.

When neural patterns represent external reality, they're physical processes about other physical processes—literally meta-physical. Physics can describe the patterns but not their aboutness. When you say "neural patterns," you're already using symbolic language that physics lacks. The patterns are physical; that they mean something requires the semantic framework physics doesn't provide.

**Q3:** But surely meaning reduces to physical facts about connections, thresholds, and signal propagation?

**A:** Then show me the equation in physics for "aboutness." Which physical law captures reference? You can describe every atom in a brain experiencing red, but nothing in physics explains why those patterns are about redness rather than just being patterns. This isn't mysticism—it's recognizing that physics, by design, describes structures without semantics. Metaphysical transduction names the transformation where physical patterns gain symbolic properties that physics cannot express.

**A:** The "feeling" is the first-person perspective of the qualification relationship. **Q8:** How does your framework address the binding problem—how distributed neural processes create unified conscious experience?

**A:** Being wrong about external facts doesn't break consciousness, but being wrong about your own catalogue would. You can mistakenly believe you saw a blue car when it was green—that's error about literals. This matches psychiatric observations—delusions about external reality preserve consciousness, but severe dissociation where self-representation fragments correlates with consciousness disruption.
