# Theoretical Preparation: The Semantic Construction Method

The construction of a first-order theory requires choices about what to take as primitive and what to derive. Just as Turing chose the minimal machinery of states, symbols, and transitions to capture computation, Semantic Reference Theory takes R(x,y,t) as its sole primitive relation, interpreting it as "x refers to y through t" and notationally expressing it as x =t> y. This relation serves as the semantic equivalent of the Turing machine—a minimal structure from which all semantic relationships can be constructed. The choice isn't arbitrary but reflects a conviction that reference is the atomic operation of meaning, much as state transition is the atomic operation of computation.

The historical development of formal theories reveals a pattern obscured by their final presentations. When Zermelo and Fraenkel developed set theory, when Peano formalized arithmetic, when Russell and Whitehead attempted to ground mathematics in logic, they didn't simply write down axioms and derive consequences. They engaged in iterative cycles of formalization, testing their axioms against known mathematical truths, adjusting their primitive notions when paradoxes emerged, refining their language to capture precisely what needed expressing. The polished axiom systems we inherit disguise this messy, creative process of discovering which formal structures best capture semantic intuitions. What appears here as systematic case analysis represents this same iterative discovery process made visible.

The notation s != r != o != s embodies more than convenient abbreviation. In standard first-order logic, equality between two variables is expressed simply: x = y or x ≠ y. But among three variables, the complete specification requires checking all three pairwise relationships. The cyclical notation captures this elegantly—starting with s, checking its relationship to r, then r to o, then cycling back to check o against s. This creates a closed loop that fully specifies the triangular relationship. When we write s =eq> r !=eq> o =eq> s in full SRT notation, we see that equality and inequality are themselves relationships that can be examined systematically. For four or more variables, such notation would require redundant checks, but for three, it provides exactly the coverage needed.

The five cases—s != r != o != s, s == r != o != s, s != r != o == s, s != r == o != s, and s == r == o == s—partition the space of possible equality relationships among three variables exhaustively. This isn't a selection of interesting cases but the complete enumeration of structurally distinct possibilities. The reduction from eight logical possibilities to five semantic cases reflects the transitivity of equality: if any two equalities hold among three variables, the third must follow. This mathematical necessity grounds the semantic exploration—we're not choosing which patterns to examine but discovering what patterns exist to be examined.

The principle of most specific relationships provides the organizing structure for the model. When multiple relationships hold between two referents—as when x =eq> y and x =color> y and x =red> y might all be true—one relationship must be foundational, the one from which others inherit. The notation (x,y) denotes this most specific relationship, satisfying x =(x,y)> y and the inheritance condition that for any t where x =t> y, either (x,y) == t or (x,y) =rel> t. This principle transforms a potential chaos of overlapping relationships into a structured hierarchy. The discovery that (sym,lit) == sym isn't a stipulation but a recognition that symbolization IS the foundational relationship between symbols and literals.

The first structural case, where all three variables are distinct, establishes what might be called the canonical form of the subject-object relationship in natural language. When we say "The mother loves her child," we naturally parse this as three distinct entities: the subject (mother), the relationship (loves), and the object (child). That English defaults to assuming distinctness unless explicitly told otherwise—"I spoke to the CEO and an employee" suggests two people rather than one—supports taking sub =rel> obj with all three distinct as the archetypal pattern. The referents sub, rel, and obj thus serve as types representing the abstract categories of subjecthood, relationship, and objecthood.

The second case reveals a profound semantic possibility: a referent can simultaneously serve as subject and as the relationship through which it relates to its object. The expression sym =sym> lit captures this perfectly—symbols symbolize literals through the act of symbolization itself. This isn't word play but formal precision. The referent sym occupies two roles in the ternary relation, much as a verb might be conjugated differently to reflect its grammatical function while remaining the same lexical item. This dual role explains why sym must inherit from rel (symbols are a type of relationship) while also maintaining its identity as a subject type.

The distinction between reference and relationship requires careful articulation. A relationship is referential when it expresses a connection between a representation and what it represents. Formally, if rep =t> obj and t inherits from rep, then t is referential. When t == (rep,obj), making it the most specific relationship, then rep =t> obj expresses reference—the representation refers to its object. The relationship x =eq> x, while expressing that x equals itself, isn't referential because equality doesn't involve representation. This distinction matters: Gödel sentences are self-referential precisely because Gödel constructed a coding that allows sentences to have canonical symbols through which they can refer to themselves. The mere reflexivity of equality doesn't constitute reference.

The fourth case, where the relationship equals the object, captures how representations contain their members. When we write sym =rep> rep, we're expressing that symbols are part of representations, but representation itself serves as both the relationship type and the object. This is like saying a dog is an animal, where "animal" is both the category (relationship type) and the thing the dog is (object). But we can't define animal in terms of dog—we'd have to subtract away dog-specific features. The representation contains the dog; the dog's relationship to it is simply being represented. This asymmetry is fundamental to how collections and their members relate.

The fifth case doesn't express self-reference in the semantic sense but rather complete coincidence of roles. When we write eq =eq> eq, we're not saying equality refers to itself but that it serves simultaneously as subject, relationship, and object in this configuration. This is constitutive rather than referential—equality must equal itself for the concept of equality to be coherent. Similarly, rel =rel> rel establishes relationships as self-grounding. These aren't circular definitions but semantic foundations, the bedrock from which other relationships build.

The distinction between types and instances runs throughout the construction. The referent sub represents the abstract type of subjects, not any particular subject. This explains why sub =sub> sub (the type having its own property) isn't automatically derivable—types don't necessarily instantiate their own type properties. The type "Number" in programming doesn't have arithmetic operations; the type "Subject" needn't have subjecthood. Yet if sub turns out to be (rel)—the canonical symbol for relationships—then sub =sub> rel would mean subjecthood symbolizes relationships, suggesting that being a subject fundamentally means being in a relationship.

Properties emerge as self-referential relationships where x =p> x indicates x has property p. These aren't the same as types—a property is something a referent has, while a type is something a referent instantiates. The pattern x =sub> x suggests x has the property of subjecthood, likely because x serves as subject in some relationship. The pattern x =color> x would mean x has the property of color. Properties form their own semantic category, possibly inheriting from equality (all properties being specializations of self-identity) but remaining distinct from the type hierarchy rooted in rel.

The inheritance structure avoids the recursive definitions that plague traditional formulations. Instead of defining x -> y recursively as "x inherits from y if x equals y, or x directly inherits from y, or there exists z such that x inherits from z and z inherits from y," we can use the symbol possession principle. If relationship types possess the canonical symbols of what they inherit from—if x =rel> y implies x =(y)> x—then inheritance becomes a matter of which symbols a type possesses. The apparent transitivity emerges from the structure rather than being axiomatized: if crimson possesses (red) and red possesses (color), then the chain of possession creates the inheritance hierarchy.

The eight referents that emerge from the five cases—eq, ref, sub, rel, obj, sym, rep, lit—aren't chosen but discovered. Each case forces certain referents into existence. The economy is striking: eight referents suffice to exemplify all five structural patterns, with some referents serving multiple roles across cases. This suggests we've found something like a basis set for semantic reference, a minimal collection from which richer semantic structures can be built. The model's validity depends not on aesthetic appeal but on whether these eight referents and their relationships form a consistent system that captures the semantic intuitions motivating the construction.

The sparseness of relationships in the model reflects semantic reality. Not every pair among the eight referents has a specified relationship beyond equality. Literals don't directly relate to subjects; objects don't symbolize; many pairs simply don't have semantic connections that need formal capture. This isn't incompleteness but selectivity—including only relationships that are semantically necessary rather than logically possible. When a relationship isn't specified, its absence is as meaningful as the presence of others.

The conjecture that sub == (rel) would unify several threads in the model. If subjecthood is the canonical symbol for relationships, then being a subject means fundamentally being in a relationship. This would explain why sub =sub> sub might hold (symbols self-refer), why subjects are essential to relationships (you can't have a relationship without a subject), and why SRT itself, being in the reference frame of rel, might have sub as its canonical subject. This isn't proven but represents the kind of semantic discovery the construction method enables.

The conditional nature of SRT's axiomatization reflects a sophisticated understanding of semantic foundations. Rather than asserting truths about an assumed domain, SRT's axioms are structured to be vacuously true when no relationships exist—the antecedent of conditionals like "if any relationship exists, then..." being false when the catalogue is empty. But the moment a single relationship is added, the entire structure springs into existence: equality emerges, subjects and objects differentiate, relationship types form hierarchies. This bootstrapping from emptiness to semantic richness captures how meaning itself might emerge from void.

The distinction between SRT as epistemology and its extensions as semantic theories is crucial. SRT with no constants isn't a theory of meaning but a framework for constructing theories of meaning. It's a generator, a schema, a pattern that becomes substantive only when constants are added. The choice to work first with sub, rel, obj, sym, lit, rep, ref, eq isn't arbitrary—these are the minimal constants needed to exemplify the five structural patterns. But these could be replaced with other constants that fit the same patterns, generating different semantic theories. SRT provides the form; particular constants provide the content.

The reflexive nature of the project—using reference to build a theory of reference—creates both challenges and insights. We're not defining reference initially because we're using referential relationships to build up to the point where reference itself can be defined. Reference emerges as the most specific form of representation relationship, but we need representation, symbols, relationships, and specificity in place before reference can be articulated. This isn't circular but foundational—we're discovering the semantic prerequisites for reference itself.

The larger vision sees successful semantic primitives being absorbed into language itself. Just as == in programming languages makes equality a grammatical feature rather than a function, truly fundamental semantic relationships deserve to become parts of linguistic syntax rather than remaining as theoretical constants. The notation (x) for canonical symbols, (x,y) for most specific relationships, and x => y for general reference all point toward a language where meaning-making operations are built into the grammar. The goal isn't just to theorize about meaning but to create languages adequate to their own semantic foundations.
