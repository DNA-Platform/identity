The First Transductive Consistency Theorem
Con(T) ↔ Con(T ∪ {G})
Definition: Transduction
Let T be a theory and let φ be a sentence in the language of T. Consider an algorithm A which takes theories as inputs and extends them based on some criteria. If algorithm A takes in T and outputs T ∪ {φ}, we say that A has transduced φ into T.

Key Relationships to Consider
G ↔ ¬Prov_T(⌜G⌝) (the Gödel sentence)
Con(T) ↔ ¬Prov_T(⌜0=1⌝) (consistency statement)
G is not provable in T if T is consistent (by the first incompleteness theorem)
There is a provable relationship between G and Con(T)
Proof Sketch
First, let's prove that if T ∪ {G} is consistent, then T is consistent:
Assume Con(T ∪ {G})
This means T ∪ {G} ⊬ 0=1
Since T ⊂ (T ∪ {G}), anything provable in T is also provable in T ∪ {G}
Therefore, if T ⊢ 0=1, then T ∪ {G} ⊢ 0=1
But we assumed T ∪ {G} ⊬ 0=1
Therefore, T ⊬ 0=1, which means Con(T)
Now, let's prove the converse: if T is consistent, then T ∪ {G} is consistent:
Assume Con(T)
By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, T ⊬ G and T ⊬ ¬G
We need to show that T ∪ {G} is consistent, i.e., T ∪ {G} ⊬ 0=1
From the second incompleteness theorem, we know T ⊢ G ↔ Con(T)
Since we assumed Con(T), and T can prove G ↔ Con(T), adding G doesn't introduce a contradiction
Therefore, Con(T ∪ {G})
Combining these results:
Con(T) ↔ Con(T ∪ {G})
Explanation
The First Transductive Consistency Theorem demonstrates that transducing the Gödel sentence G into a consistent theory T preserves consistency. This is in stark contrast to the Second Transductive Consistency Theorem, which shows that transducing ¬G leads to inconsistency.

This result is profound because it reveals an asymmetry in how undecidable sentences behave when transduced. While the Gödel sentence itself cannot be proved or disproved within T, transducing G is "safe" in the sense that it won't collapse the theory into inconsistency, provided T was consistent to begin with.

Moreover, this theorem establishes that extending a theory with statements that align with its consistency (which G does) preserves the theory's integrity, while extending it with statements that contradict its consistency (which ¬G does) leads to collapse.
