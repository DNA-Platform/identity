# The Semantics of Dichotomy: The (Dichotomy) Dichotomy

## Definitions

We begin with three fundamental concepts:
- `d := dichotomy` - the property of distinction or separation  
- `m := monotony` - the property of uniformity or non-distinction
- `im := impossibility` - the property of contradiction

## The Core Structure of Dichotomy and Monotony

### The Fundamental Identities

The relationship between dichotomy and monotony rests on two essential identities. First, monotony IS the negation of dichotomy:

`m == (!d)`

This means monotony literally equals "not-dichotomy" - it's defined by the absence of distinction. Second, dichotomy IS the most specific relationship between dichotomy and monotony:

`d == (d,m)`

This remarkable self-reference means that dichotomy equals the very relationship that distinguishes dichotomy from monotony. The concept exemplifies itself.

### The Canonical Relationship

In SRT, canonical symbols make abstract concepts graspable. For dichotomy, we have:

`(d) == m`

The canonical symbol for dichotomy IS monotony. This captures a deep semantic truth: we understand distinction only through its absence, just as we understand signal through noise or being through non-being.

But there's something even deeper here. Since `(d) == m`, we can derive:

`m =m> d` where `(m,d) == m`

This means monotony represents dichotomy through itself, and monotony is the most specific relationship between monotony and dichotomy. Notice that in this symbolic representation, m is the subject - monotony is what does the representing.

This reveals why the canonical must be monotony: when we talk about "dichotomy" as a subject matter, we're talking about ONE thing - a single concept, a unified topic of discussion. To discuss dichotomy as a subject requires symbolizing it as a unity, as monotony. The moment we actually perform dichotomy (splitting into two), we're no longer talking ABOUT it but DOING it.

This is the key insight: to reference dichotomy as a concept requires treating it monotonously. The subject "dichotomy" is singular - it's only when we apply or perform dichotomy that duality emerges.

### Membership in the Dichotomy

Both dichotomy and monotony are members of their own dichotomy. For dichotomy, we have `d =(d)> d`, which through the identity `d == (d,m)` means dichotomy relates to itself through its own relationship with monotony. For monotony, we have `m =(d)> m`, expressing that monotony is part of the dichotomy.

Since `(d) == m`, these membership relations imply deeper properties. We get `d =m> d`, meaning dichotomy has the property of monotony before distinction occurs. Similarly, `m =m> m` means monotony is monotonous - it has its own property.

### The Property of Distinction

While both d and m start in monotony, the act of distinction creates different properties. Dichotomy becomes dichotomous through `d =d> d`, which given `d == (d,m)` means dichotomy relates to itself through the very distinction it creates.

Monotony, however, lacks this property of distinction: `m !=d> m`. Since `m == (!d)`, monotony represents itself as not-dichotomy through `m =(!d)> m`.

### Structural Relationships

The union of dichotomy and monotony inherits from monotony:

`(d|m) -> m`

This makes sense - the undifferentiated union of distinction and non-distinction is itself undifferentiated. Their intersection creates impossibility:

`(d&m) -> im`

One cannot simultaneously have and lack distinction. This contradiction defines impossibility.

### Negation Rules

With d as the canonical pole of the dichotomy, double negation returns to the canonical:

`!!d == d`

However, negating the non-canonical pole would be non-canonical. We don't write `!m` but use `d` instead.

## The Nature of Impossibility

Impossibility has its own semantic structure. It represents a canonical impossible object:

`im =im> [im]`

This canonical has a type that nothing can instantiate. If `[im] =[im]> t` for some type t, then universally `A[x,y]: x !=t> y`. Nothing has type t - it's an empty type that exists semantically but has no instances.

When applied to our dichotomy, `d&m -> im` means being both dichotomous and monotonous is impossible. The property itself exists - we can talk about it - but nothing can have it: `A[x]: x !=im> x`.

## Application to Temperature

Let's apply this abstract structure to the concrete dichotomy of temperature, using:
- `h := hot`
- `c := cold`
- `temp := temperature`

### Temperature's Fundamental Structure

Temperature is the monotony - the undifferentiated property:

`temp == (h|c)`

Cold IS the negation of hot when hot is taken as canonical:

`c == (!h)`

The pair of hot and cold forms a dichotomy that inherits from our abstract dichotomy type:

`(h,c) -> d`

### How Objects Have Temperature

At the monotonic level, an object has temperature through `obj =temp> obj`, which is equivalent to `obj =(h|c)> obj` - the object is in the undifferentiated temperature state.

At the dichotomous level, the object must be either hot or cold. We have `obj =h> obj` if and only if `obj !=c> obj`. Similarly, `obj =c> obj` if and only if `obj !=h> obj`. The equivalence `obj =(!h)> obj` if and only if `obj =c> obj` shows how negation creates the other pole.

### The Complete Temperature Equivalence

These three expressions are equivalent:
- `obj =temp> obj` - the object has temperature
- `obj =h> obj || obj =c> obj` - the object is hot or cold  
- `obj =(h|c)> obj` - the object is in the temperature state

### Inheritance in Temperature

The specific values inherit from the general property:

`h -> temp` and `c -> temp`

This inheritance enables the reduction we want. If `obj =h> obj`, then by inheritance `obj =temp> obj`. The specific implies the general.

### Representation Relationships

The dichotomy represents temperature through itself:

`(h,c) =(h,c)> temp`

Temperature in turn represents the literal state of being hot or cold:

`temp =temp> [h|c]`

Here `[h|c]` is the literal, undifferentiated state that temperature points to.

## The Self-Describing Nature of the Dichotomy Dichotomy

What makes the d/m dichotomy unique is its complete self-reference. Since `d == (d,m)`, dichotomy IS its own most specific relationship with monotony. Since `m == (!d)`, monotony IS precisely not-dichotomy. This creates a structure that completely describes itself.

When dichotomy represents itself through dichotomy (given that `d == (d,m)`), it's saying "dichotomy is the relationship that creates distinction." That relationship is precisely what relates dichotomy to monotony. The concept and its instance coincide.

This differs fundamentally from concrete dichotomies like hot/cold. There, the values (hot, cold) are distinct from their dichotomy `(h,c)`. The dichotomy represents something else (temperature). The structure instantiates rather than exemplifies the d/m pattern.

## Implications for Semantic Theory

The dichotomy/monotony structure reveals how meaning emerges from distinction itself. Before distinction, there is only monotony - undifferentiated potential. The act of distinction creates both dichotomy (the principle of separation) and makes monotony visible as the ground from which distinction emerged.

This pattern appears throughout semantics:
- Signal/noise
- Figure/ground  
- Being/non-being
- Presence/absence

In each case, we understand one through the other, and the distinction itself becomes meaningful. The dichotomy dichotomy shows this isn't just a pattern but the foundational semantic operation from which all meaning emerges.
