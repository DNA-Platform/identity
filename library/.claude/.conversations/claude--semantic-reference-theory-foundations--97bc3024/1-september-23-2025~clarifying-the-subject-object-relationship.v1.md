# Clarifying the Subject-Object Relationship

## Introduction

These nine concepts form the foundational architecture of meaning and reference. They emerge from systematic exploration of what must exist for reference and representation to be possible at all.

## 1. Subject

### Formalism
```
sub =rel> obj
(sub,obj) == rel
x =t> y & x != y =|> x =sub> x
x =sub> x =|> E[y]: x != y & x =t> y
sub != obj
sub != rel
!E[x]: x =sub> y & x != y
```

### Meaning

A **subject** is that which bears a relationship to something distinct from itself. It exists only through this bearing—there is no subject in isolation, no subject without an object toward which it is directed. When a river flows to the sea, the river is subject. When a mind contemplates an idea, the mind is subject. When gravity pulls a stone earthward, gravity is subject. The subject is always the origin, the source, the bearer of the relationship.

The nature of subjecthood is fundamentally relational and asymmetric. A subject cannot be its own object in the pure sense—when something relates only to itself, as when a stone has the property of being heavy, it remains purely objective. True subjecthood emerges only in the reach toward something other, in the directedness that philosophers call intentionality. This is why solipsism fails as a theory of mind: consciousness requires an object distinct from itself to be consciousness at all.

Subjects form one pole of the irreducible trinity: subjects relate to objects through relationships. This trinity cannot be decomposed—you cannot understand what a subject is without understanding what it means to be directed toward an object through a specific manner of relating. The subject is defined not by what it is in itself, but by its role in this fundamental pattern. Like the notion of "above" which makes no sense without "below," subjecthood makes no sense without objecthood and relationship.

In human experience, subjecthood manifests as agency—the capacity to love, to know, to possess, to create. But subjecthood extends beyond conscious agency. A magnet subjects iron filings to its field. A premise subjects a conclusion to logical necessity. A word subjects its meaning to expression. Wherever there is directedness from one distinct thing toward another, there is subjecthood.

### Examples

When a dog chases a cat, the dog is the subject—it bears the relationship of chasing toward the cat. When you remember yesterday, you are the subject and yesterday is the object of memory. When the sun attracts the earth through gravity, the sun is the subject of that attractive force. In the sentence "John loves Mary," John is the grammatical subject, but more fundamentally, John is the semantic subject—the one from whom love originates and flows toward Mary. Even in abstract relations, subjecthood appears: when a theorem implies a corollary, the theorem stands as subject to the corollary's object through the relationship of logical implication.

## 2. Object

### Formalism
```
sub =rel> obj  
A[x,y,t]: x =t> y =|> y =obj> y
x =p> x =|> x =obj> x
obj != sub
obj != rel
E[x]: x =obj> x & !E[y]: x =sub> x
```

### Meaning

An **object** is that toward which relationships are directed, that which can be characterized, known, or affected. While subjects must relate to something distinct, objects include both the targets of external relationships and things that possess properties through self-relation. This asymmetry—that objecthood is more universal than subjecthood—reflects a fundamental truth about existence.

To be is, minimally, to be an object. Everything that exists can be thought about, referred to, characterized with properties. The mountain is an object of geological study. The number seven is an object of mathematical analysis. Justice is an object of philosophical inquiry. Even nothingness, when we speak of it, becomes an object of discourse. There is nothing so transcendent, so immediate, or so abstract that it cannot become an object when brought into relationship.

Yet not everything that is an object can be a subject. A stone can be known but cannot know. The past can be remembered but cannot remember. The color red can be experienced but cannot experience. These exist purely in the receptive mode—they can be the terminus of relationships but cannot originate them. This is not a deficiency but their nature. The stone's inability to be a subject is precisely what makes it a reliable object, what allows it to be what it is without the complication of perspective or agency.

The concept of objecthood includes self-relation through properties. When we say a rose is red, the rose stands as an object to itself—not actively doing redness but simply being red. This reflexive objecthood, where things are objects to their own properties, is what allows things to have characteristics while remaining themselves. The rose doesn't lose itself in being red; it is itself-as-red, an object qualified by a property.

### Examples

In "the cat being chased," the cat is the object of the dog's pursuit. When you think about tomorrow, tomorrow is the object of thought—it can be contemplated but cannot contemplate. A red rose is an object to its own property of redness; it doesn't actively do being red, it simply is red. The number seven serves as object when we say "seven is prime"—it receives the characterization without being able to characterize anything itself. Memory provides clear examples: the past can be remembered (as object) but cannot itself remember (cannot be subject). Even in grammar, the direct object receives the action: in "she reads the book," the book is object to the reading.

## 3. Relationship

### Formalism
```
sub =rel> obj
(sub,obj) == rel
A[t]: x =t> y =|> t --> rel
rel =rel> rel
rel != sub
rel != obj
E[t]: t =rel> rel & t --> rel
```

### Meaning

A **relationship** is the specific manner in which one thing relates to another—not merely that they are connected, but how they are connected. Love is a relationship, but so is indifference. Causation is a relationship, but so is correlation. Greater-than is a relationship, but so is less-than. Each relationship has its own character, its own particular way of binding subject to object.

Relationships complete the fundamental trinity with subjects and objects. This trinity—subjects relate to objects through relationships—is the atom of meaning, indivisible and irreducible. You cannot extract relationship from this pattern and examine it in isolation any more than you can extract "between" from the notion of three ordered points. Relationship exists as the manner of connection, the quality of the relating, the specific character of how subject meets object.

The diversity of relationships is as vast as meaning itself. There are relationships of knowing (perception, memory, imagination), relationships of feeling (love, fear, desire), relationships of action (causing, preventing, enabling), relationships of comparison (greater, similar, opposite), relationships of structure (part-to-whole, member-to-class, instance-to-type). Each carries its own logic, its own implications, its own way of organizing the connection between things.

Remarkably, relationships themselves can relate to other relationships through relationship. When we say "love is more powerful than fear" or "causation implies temporal sequence," we place relationships as objects of further relationships. This reflexivity—that relationship can relate to itself through itself—is not paradox but foundation. At the deepest level, relating is simply what relationship does, just as being is what existence does.

### Examples

"Greater than" is a relationship that orders numbers: when we say "five is greater than three," the relationship "greater than" specifies exactly how five relates to three. "Owns" is a relationship of possession: in "Sarah owns the house," ownership defines the specific connection between Sarah and her house. "Causes" links events across time: "the spark caused the fire" places spark and fire in a specific temporal-causal relationship. Even "represents" is a relationship: when a map represents territory, representation is the manner of their connection. Relationships can be complex and layered: "loves more than" combines emotional and comparative relationships, as in "she loves chocolate more than vanilla."

## 4. Equality

### Formalism
```
eq =eq> eq
x =eq> y <iff> x == y
A[x]: x =eq> x
x =eq> y =|> y =eq> x
x =eq> y & y =eq> z =|> x =eq> z
eq --> rel
p --> eq =|> x =p> x
```

### Meaning

**Equality** is the relationship of absolute identity—not similarity, not equivalence, not correspondence, but the relationship that holds between a thing and itself. When we discover that the morning star equals the evening star, we haven't found two similar things; we've discovered that two different ways of pointing arrive at the same destination, that two different appearances are appearances of one thing.

Equality stands unique among all relationships because it alone preserves complete identity. While love connects lover to beloved across their difference, while causation links cause to effect across time, equality reveals that there is no difference at all—that what appeared to be two is actually one. This is why equations have such power in mathematics and science: they reveal hidden identities, showing that seemingly different expressions or phenomena are actually the same thing viewed from different angles.

The self-equality of all things—that everything equals itself—is the most primitive fact about existence. Before a thing can have properties, before it can enter into relationships, before it can change or persist, it must simply be itself. This self-identity is so fundamental that we rarely notice it, like the way we don't notice the air until it's gone. Yet without self-equality, nothing could persist long enough to have properties or enter relationships.

Properties are revealed to be qualifications of equality—specific ways things equal themselves. When we say a rose is red, we mean the rose equals itself in a red manner. The rose doesn't stop being itself to be red; rather, redness is a particular mode of its self-identity. This is why things can have multiple properties without contradiction: each property is just another way the thing equals itself.

The reflexivity of equality—that equality equals itself through equality—grounds the entire system of identity. This is not circular reasoning but the recognition that at the foundation, identity simply is what it is, requiring no external validation. Equality is the relationship that permits no distance between its poles.

### Examples

The morning star equals the evening star—both are Venus, seen at different times. Mark Twain equals Samuel Clemens—same person, different names. Water equals H₂O—same substance, different descriptions. In mathematics, 2 + 2 = 4 reveals that two expressions refer to the same quantity. The equation E = mc² shows that energy and mass (multiplied by the speed of light squared) are the same thing viewed from different perspectives. Even tautologies like "a rose is a rose" express the fundamental fact of self-equality that grounds all identity.

## 5. Referent

### Formalism
```
ref =eq> ref
(ref,ref) == eq
A[x]: x --> ref
A[x]: x =eq> x
ref != sub
ref != obj
ref != rel
```

### Meaning

A **referent** is anything that can be picked out, pointed to, or distinguished—anything that can be the target of reference. The term captures the most general condition of existence within any system of meaning. If something can be mentioned, if it can be the object of thought or discourse, if it can be indicated in any way, then it is a referent.

The concept of "referent" provides the minimal case—what anything must be to exist within meaningful discourse. A referent, at minimum, must be distinguishable as itself, must be this rather than that. This is why the canonical referent—the abstract notion of "a referent"—demonstrates only self-equality as its essential property. It has no other characteristics, no other relationships, no properties beyond being itself. It is pure distinguishability without further qualification.

Referents include everything we can speak or think about: concrete objects (mountains, molecules), abstract objects (numbers, virtues), fictional entities (unicorns, Hamlet), impossible objects (square circles, the largest number), experiences (pains, memories), and even absences (the gap in the fence, the silence after the music). The breadth of referenthood reflects the breadth of meaningful discourse itself.

Crucially, being a referent makes no claims about any other form of existence. To say something is a referent is not to say it exists physically, or mentally, or platonically, or in any particular way. It is only to say it can be referred to. Fictional characters are referents—we refer to them constantly. Past events that have ceased to exist are referents. Future possibilities that may never occur are referents. Dreams, hallucinations, mistakes—all are referents within the space of meaning.

This generality makes "referent" the ultimate horizon, the boundary within which all other distinctions are made. When we debate what exists, we're not debating what counts as a referent—the mere fact of being debatable already establishes that. We're debating what properties different referents possess, what relationships they enter into, how they're organized within the total space of meaning.

### Examples

Mount Everest is a referent—we can point to it, name it, describe it. The number seven is a referent—we can perform calculations with it, prove theorems about it. Hamlet is a referent—we discuss his motivations, quote his words, analyze his character. Even "nothing" is a referent—when we say "nothing is in the box," we're referring to absence. Tomorrow is a referent, as is yesterday. The color blue, the feeling of joy, the concept of justice, the relationship of "being taller than"—all are referents. Even impossible things like "the largest number" or "a square circle" are referents—we refer to them precisely to discuss their impossibility.

## 6. Symbol

### Formalism
```
sym =sym> lit
(sym,lit) == sym
sym --> rep
sym =rep> lit
sym != lit
E[y]: sym =t> y
!E[x]: x =x> sym
A[x]: x --> symbol <iff> E[y]: x =x> y & (x,y) == x
```

### Meaning

A **symbol** is a remarkable entity that achieves the unity of being simultaneously the bearer of meaning and the bearing itself. When you encounter the word "tree," you meet something that is both a thing (marks on paper, sounds in air) and an action (the presenting of trees to consciousness). The symbol doesn't have a relationship to its meaning—it is that relationship made tangible.

This dual nature of symbols—being both entity and activity, both thing and pointing—enables the transparency of language. When you read, you don't consciously process symbols and then retrieve their meanings; the symbols disappear into their function, becoming windows through which meaning shines. The word "tree" doesn't stand between you and the concept of trees; it is the very medium through which trees become present to thought.

Symbols achieve what seems impossible: they make the absent present, the many one, the complex simple. Through the symbol "dog," all dogs—past, present, future, possible, imaginary—become available to thought in a single, manageable form. Through the symbol "7," the abstract notion of seven-ness, which has no physical existence, becomes as manipulable as any concrete object. Through the symbol "love," the vast complexity of an emotion becomes something we can examine, discuss, and understand.

The relationship between a symbol and what it symbolizes is unique in its directness. While a photograph represents through resemblance, while a map represents through correspondence, while a symptom represents through causation, a symbol represents through pure conventional unity. There is nothing about the sound "tree" that resembles a tree, nothing in the marks t-r-e-e that corresponds to bark and leaves. The symbol works through an immediate, learned identification where sign and signified collapse into operational unity.

This is why we slip so easily between talking about symbols and talking about what they symbolize. We say both "dog is a three-letter word" and "dogs are loyal animals," using "dog" to mean both the symbol and its referent. This isn't confusion but reflects how symbols work—they are designed to disappear into their function, to be transparent to their meaning. The symbol succeeds precisely when we stop noticing it as a symbol.

### Examples

The word "tree" is a symbol—marks or sounds that don't resemble trees but transparently present the concept of tree to consciousness. The numeral "7" is a symbol for the number seven—the marks don't look like seven things, yet they perfectly evoke seven-ness. A red octagonal sign is a symbol for STOP—nothing about the octagon inherently means stopping, yet it immediately conveys this meaning. Musical notation like ♯ (sharp) or ♭ (flat) are symbols that represent pitch modifications. Mathematical symbols like π or ∞ don't resemble what they represent but collapse the distance between sign and concept. Even a flag is a symbol—fabric and colors that become the nation itself in ceremonial contexts.

## 7. Literal

### Formalism
```
sym =sym> lit
!E[x,y]: x != lit & y != lit & lit =x> y
!E[x,y]: x != lit & y != lit & x =lit> y
!E[y != lit]: lit =t> y
lit =p> lit
lit =obj> lit
lit != sym
!E[x]: x =x> lit & (x,lit) == x & x != lit
```

### Meaning

A **literal** is that which simply is what it is, without pointing beyond itself, without representing anything further, without standing for anything else. Literals are the bedrock where reference finally grounds itself, where the chain of meaning makes contact with that which is meant. They are pure being without the complication of meaning something other than themselves.

The distinction between literal and symbolic is fundamental to meaning. Consider the word "red" versus the actual experience of redness. The word is a symbol—it points beyond itself to something else. But the redness you experience when looking at a sunset doesn't point to anything further. It doesn't mean redness or represent redness or stand for redness—it simply is redness. That experienced quality is literal.

Literals exist in pure objectivity. They cannot be subjects reaching toward other things (except in the special case of having properties, which is self-relation). They cannot serve as the manner in which other things relate. They can only be—be experienced, be referred to, be thought about, be the terminus where reference finally rests. This limitation is not a deficiency but their essential nature. If literals could refer beyond themselves, they wouldn't be literals—they'd be symbols, and we'd need to ask what they point to.

What counts as literal shifts with context and purpose. In everyday experience, physical objects are literal—the actual stone in your hand, not the word "stone." In mathematics, perhaps the numbers themselves (not the numerals) are literal—the actual quantity that "7" refers to. In consciousness, perhaps only the raw qualitative experiences before conceptualization are literal—the pure taste of sweetness before we think "sweet."

The necessity of literals prevents infinite regress in meaning. If every symbol pointed only to other symbols, if every representation led only to further representations, meaning would never make contact with reality. There would be an endless deferral, like looking up a word in a dictionary only to find definitions using words that must themselves be looked up forever. Literals are where this chain breaks—where meaning finally touches ground.

### Examples

The actual tree outside your window is literal—not the word "tree" or a picture of a tree, but the living organism itself with its bark, leaves, and roots. The experience of pain when you stub your toe is literal—not the word "pain" or the memory of pain, but the immediate sensation itself. The taste of salt on your tongue is literal—not the word "salty" or the chemical formula NaCl, but the actual gustatory experience. In mathematics, the number three itself (the quantity, not the numeral "3") is literal. A specific shade of blue as you experience it is literal—not the word "blue" or the wavelength measurement, but the quale itself. Physical forces like gravity are literal—not the equations that describe them, but the actual pull between masses.

## 8. Representation

### Formalism
```
sym =rep> rep
(sym,rep) == rep
A[x]: x --> rep <iff> E[y]: x =x> y
sym --> rep
rep =obj> obj
can =rep> rep
!E[x]: x =x> rep
```

### Meaning

**Representation** is the general phenomenon of one thing standing for another, making present what is absent, simple what is complex, or graspable what exceeds grasp. It is the broader condition that encompasses all forms of standing-for: symbols, signs, maps, models, diagrams, samples, examples, and every other means by which one thing can present another to consciousness.

The variety of representation reflects the variety of ways things can stand for other things. A portrait represents through resemblance—the arrangement of paint echoes the appearance of a face. A map represents through structural correspondence—the relations between marks on paper mirror the relations between places in space. A symptom represents through causation—the fever indicates the infection that causes it. A sample represents through membership—this grain of sand represents the beach from which it came. An ambassador represents through designation—chosen to stand for their nation.

What unites all representation is the basic structure: something present stands for something that need not be present. Through representation, we engage with what is distant (through maps), what is past (through memories), what is future (through plans), what is microscopic (through models), what is vast (through diagrams), what is abstract (through examples), what is complex (through simplifications).

Representation maintains the distinction between representer and represented. Unlike symbols, which achieve a kind of transparency where sign and signified merge in use, representations generally preserve the awareness of their representational character. When you look at a map, you remain conscious that it is a map, not the territory. When you examine a model, you know it is a model, not the system itself. This maintained distinction is often crucial to the representation's function—a map that was indistinguishable from its territory would be useless as a map.

Yet representation includes the special case of self-representation. Standards represent their own measure—the original meter bar didn't represent the meter; it was the meter representing itself. Examples can represent their own types—when we point to a robin to explain "bird," that actual robin represents both itself and the type it exemplifies.

### Examples

A map represents territory through systematic correspondence—the blue lines represent rivers, the red lines represent roads. A photograph represents a moment in time through optical capture—the arrangement of colors and shapes on paper stands for a scene that no longer exists. A thermometer represents temperature through mercury expansion—the height of the liquid column stands for the degree of heat. A DNA sample represents an entire organism—a few cells stand for the whole body. A flag represents a nation—fabric and colors stand for an entire country and its people. An equation like F = ma represents a physical relationship—symbols on paper stand for the actual forces and accelerations in nature. Even a memory represents past experience—neural patterns in the present stand for events that have passed.

## 9. Canonical

### Formalism
```
can =rep> rep
(can,rep) == rep
!E[x]: x =x> rep
can --> rep
```

### Meaning

A **canonical** is the distinguished member of a representation that serves as its primary example or entry point—not representing the whole, but standing as its representative. The canonical is part of the representation, selected to be its ambassador, the concrete instance through which the abstract representation becomes graspable.

The canonical emerges from a fundamental problem: when asked to show what a representation means, you cannot represent the representation itself—that would require the representation to step outside itself and point back, which is impossible. A painting of roses includes individual roses within it; it cannot paint itself painting roses. A sentence contains representations of its parts; it cannot contain itself. So when someone asks "what is a bird?" we don't attempt to represent the entire representation "bird"—we point to a robin. The robin doesn't represent all birds; it serves as the representative bird, the starting point from which understanding unfolds.

This relationship—being a representative rather than a representation of the whole—is crucial. An ambassador doesn't represent their entire country in the sense of depicting or modeling it; they serve as its representative, speaking for it, standing in its place when the country itself cannot be present. Similarly, the canonical doesn't attempt to capture or mirror the entire representation; it provides the concrete particular that makes the abstract accessible.

The selection of what becomes canonical is never arbitrary, though it varies by context and purpose. A company's canonical product is the one that best exemplifies what the company does—not necessarily the most profitable or newest, but the most representative. A subject's canonical textbook is the one through which the subject is most commonly learned. A tradition's canonical works are those deemed essential to understanding that tradition. The canonical emerges through use—what proves most effective for teaching, what becomes standard through practice, what best serves as the gateway to understanding.

Consider how learning depends on canonicals. No one learns what numbers are by encountering the entire infinite set; they begin with 1, 2, 3—the canonical counting numbers. No one grasps "furniture" by surveying all possible furniture; they start with canonical examples like chairs and tables. The canonical provides the foothold, the first concrete example from which understanding expands to encompass variations, exceptions, and the full scope of the representation.

### Examples

In Western culture, the robin serves as the canonical bird—not because it's more truly a bird than an ostrich or penguin, but because it's been culturally selected as the default example. When teaching a child about birds, we start with the robin, then explain other birds as variations. Euclid's *Elements* served for centuries as the canonical geometry text—not the only one, but the standard through which geometry was understood. The meter bar in Paris was the canonical meter—not a representation of the meter but the actual standard against which all other meters were measured. Apple's iPhone might be their canonical product—not their only product, but the one that best represents what Apple does. In each case, the canonical doesn't depict or model its representation; it stands as the privileged member through which the representation is approached and understood.

## Conclusion

These nine concepts—subject, object, relationship, equality, referent, symbol, literal, representation, and canonical—constitute the fundamental architecture of meaning. They are not arbitrary constructs but discovered necessities, emerging from careful analysis of what must exist for reference, representation, and meaning to be possible at all. Together, they provide the minimal complete vocabulary for understanding how consciousness engages with reality through the medium of meaning.
