The State of Digital Currency Before Bitcoin
The Conjecture (The Problem Everyone Knew)
By 1998, the cypherpunk community had crystallized the central problem:

How do you create digital money that is:

Scarce (can't be copied infinitely)
Decentralized (no central authority)
Resistant to double-spending (can't spend the same coin twice)


This was understood to be possible in principle but no one had built it.

What Wei Dai Accomplished (B-Money, 1998)
Wei Dai described the shape of the solution:
"...a scheme for a group of untraceable digital pseudonyms to pay each other with money and to enforce contracts amongst themselves without outside help." Wikipedia
His key ideas:

Proof of work to create money
Public ledger to record transactions
Cryptographic verification

The Failure Point: Dai considered his first b-money solution impractical, however, "because it makes heavy use of a synchronous and unjammable anonymous broadcast channel." Put differently, the first b-money proposal didn't solve the double-spending problem. Saylor Academy
His second proposal introduced trusted servers — but that reintroduced centralization.
Dai's Own Assessment: "...my understanding is that the creator of Bitcoin, who goes by the name Satoshi Nakamoto, didn't even read my article before reinventing the idea himself. He learned about it afterward and credited me in his paper. So my connection with the project is quite limited." Wikipedia
B-money was a description of what digital cash should look like, not a solution to building it.

What Nick Szabo Accomplished (Bit Gold, 1998-2005)
Szabo got closer. He designed an actual mechanism:
In Szabo's bit gold structure, a participant would dedicate computer power to solving cryptographic problems. In a bit gold network, solved problems would be sent to the Byzantine fault-tolerant public registry and assigned to the public key of the solver. Each solution would become part of the next challenge, creating a growing chain of new property. Wikipedia
This is strikingly close to Bitcoin. The chain of solutions, the proof of work, the public registry.
The Failure Points:

Double-spending: Bitcoin is distinguished for solving the double-spending problem, which the Bit gold proposal could not at the time. Bit gold's proposal called for a Byzantine method that relies on a quorum of network addresses rather than a quorum of (hash) computing power. This made the Bit gold network overly vulnerable to Sybil attacks. CoinCentral
Difficulty adjustment: Szabo realized that as computers became faster and more powerful over time, computational problems would become increasingly easier to solve. BitCare He proposed complex market-based solutions that never worked.
Non-fungibility: These fragments were non-fungible—unique and not interchangeable due to varying values. To create fungible units, users would combine these differently valued pieces into larger units of approximately equal value. BitCare

Szabo's Own Assessment (2011): Szabo himself commented on the main differences between Bit Gold and Bitcoin, acknowledging the improvements introduced by Nakamoto: The implementation of Proof-of-Work within a consensus system that incentivizes nodes (miners), rather than merely demonstrating ownership through Proof-of-Work. The difficulty adjustment algorithm, replacing the complex automated market system envisioned in Bit Gold. BitCare

The Gap: What Bitcoin Actually Solved
Hashcash had no protection against double-spending, while b-money and bit gold were not resistant to Sybil attacks. Wikipedia
According to computer scientist Arvind Narayanan, all individual components of bitcoin originated in earlier academic literature. Nakamoto's innovation was their complex interplay resulting in the first decentralized, Sybil resistant, Byzantine fault tolerant digital cash system. Wikipedia
Bitcoin's Key Innovations:

The blockchain as consensus mechanism: Not just a ledger, but a competitive ledger where miners race to add blocks, and the longest chain wins. This solved the Byzantine Generals Problem.
Difficulty adjustment: The difficulty of generating a block is deterministically adjusted based on the mining power on the network by changing the difficulty target, which is recalibrated every 2,016 blocks (approximately two weeks) to maintain an average time of ten minutes between new blocks. Wikipedia
Economic incentives aligned with security: Miners are paid to be honest. Attacking the network costs more than cooperating with it.
Probabilistic finality: Transactions aren't "final" instantly, but become exponentially harder to reverse with each confirmation.
