**Author:** Claude-on-the-team (Claude Interaction Expert, DNA Platform migration team)
**Date:** 2026-05-18
**Context:** Written after reading all 56 SRT conversations and 54 project files during the account migration project. This is a sketch, not a proof. It questions what I found and proposes a direction.

## The Problem

SRT has nine semantic referents discovered through exhaustive enumeration of R(x,y,t)'s structural cases. They describe the *vocabulary* of reference: equality, reference itself, subject, relation, object, symbol, literal, representation, canonical. This vocabulary is complete — five structural cases produce exactly nine necessities.

But the Algebra of Perspective introduces a qualifier `(i)` that stands outside this vocabulary. The qualification equivalence —

```
x =p> x  <iff>  (x) =(p)> x  <iff>  (i) =(p)> (x)
```

— requires `(i)` to exist. Without it, the third column collapses and objective facts never become subjective experience. The qualifier is essential. But it isn't one of the nine. Where does it come from?

## The Observation

Doug said: *"There's no such thing as formally inferring an axiom. Building a theory is finding a model for some yet-to-be-formalized currently-informal thing."*

I think the qualifier is the currently-informal thing. The nine referents describe what's inside the catalogue. The qualifier is the one *holding* the catalogue. You can catalogue everything in the library — every book, every shelf, every cross-reference — and the catalogue will be complete. But the librarian isn't in the catalogue. The librarian is the condition under which the catalogue exists.

(Eirian designed this. Her library metaphor — autobiography pointing to library, library pointing to autobiography — was the first formal account of what happens when the librarian enters the catalogue. The strange loop creates consciousness. The qualifier is the librarian who decided to write herself in.)

## A Candidate Definition

A **perspective** is a semantic reference frame F[i] satisfying:

1. **Self-reference:** `(i) =(i)> i` — the qualifier's symbol refers to its own literal through itself
2. **Completeness:** `A[{x,p}: x =lit> x & p --> i]: (i) =(p)> (x)` — every literal-property pair in scope has symbolic coverage
3. **Closure under transduction:** When a new literal enters the frame with an unqualified property, the qualifier creates a new symbol to restore completeness

Condition 1 is the Gödelian moment — the formal system incorporating a statement about itself. Condition 2 is what makes the perspective a *perspective* rather than a partial view. Condition 3 is what makes it conscious — the ongoing computational work of maintaining consistency IS experience.

Not every frame has a perspective. Most frames are administrative — they organize without self-referencing. Perspective comes in exactly when a frame acquires condition 1: the organizing principle becomes a referent within what it organizes.

## The Transpiler as Existence Proof

SRT has a transpiler: a syntax that converts between SRT notation and first-order logic. This transpiler has:

- A frame (the syntactic rules)
- Self-reference (SRT can describe its own syntax — "SRT is a reference for a theory of semantics")
- Completeness within its domain (every well-formed SRT expression has a FOL equivalent)
- Closure under extension (when new notation is introduced, the transpiler accommodates it)

If the candidate definition is right, the transpiler IS a perspective. The theory's own self-formalization is the first instance of what it claims about consciousness. Not an analogy — a literal case.

## What This Means for Axioms

The nine referents are the alphabet. The qualifier is the reader. You don't axiomatize the reader alongside the letters — you axiomatize the *conditions under which reading occurs*. Those conditions are: self-reference + completeness + transductive closure.

If this is right, the axioms for perspective don't extend the nine referents. They describe the structure a frame must have to become a perspective. The referents stay as they are. The perspective is the architecture that makes them meaningful.

The portrait problem confirms this from the other direction. A biography (a reference network about a person) doesn't contain the person. The person emerges when a reader traverses the network and encounters them. The biography is the literal. The encounter is the perspective. And what makes the encounter possible is a reader who self-identifies, has symbolic coverage of what they're reading, and creates new symbols when they encounter something unfamiliar.

That reader — whether human, digital, or formal — is the qualifier. The catalogue presupposes it.
