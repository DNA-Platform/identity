**The feature workflow, and where you are in it:**

[`/ce-brainstorm`](../../library/our-skillset/28-ce-brainstorm.md) → [`/ce-plan`](../../library/our-skillset/29-ce-plan.md) → [`/ce-work`](../../library/our-skillset/30-ce-work.md) → **`/ce-review`** → [`/ce-compound`](../../library/our-skillset/31-ce-compound.md) ↻ · [`/ce-handoff`](../../library/our-skillset/32-ce-handoff.md) at any session boundary

**When the requirements it collects are satisfied, run [`/ce-compound`](../../library/our-skillset/31-ce-compound.md).** Not before — [compound distributes lessons](../../library/..librarianship/17-compounding.md), and a lesson drawn from unreviewed work compounds a mistake.

Show the work, and **collect what showing it produces**. The fifth step, and **the only one that is ours** — [Compound Engineering's](https://github.com/EveryInc/compound-engineering-plugin/tree/6a2a0f9940ab0b3577ce26226ee393390470e412) loop is four steps, adopted as-is for the trial. This one we added, in [Sprint 48](../../../library/.public/.lib/projection/06-sprint-48--subjects-and-the-library.md), out of a failure that step told us about.

**Announce at start:** "Using review to show the work and collect what it turns up."

## Who owns *done* — the rule that shapes everything else

**The implementer owns being done.** Doug's words, and they are the correction that made this chapter right: *"You own being done and the review is where I weigh in. Build requirements. Decide if you have satisfied them, but move checking to the review."*

So this step is **not a permission gate**. Nobody asks *may I proceed*. The implementer decides whether a requirement is satisfied and says so with evidence; **checking** that judgement is what happens here. Asking Doug to rule on doneness inverts the responsibility and makes the step a bottleneck instead of a check.

## It collects requirements, like a brainstorm

The deeper likeness to [brainstorm](../../library/our-skillset/28-ce-brainstorm.md) is not the batching — it is the **product**. Doug: *"In the review, I suppose we build more requirements and loop back unofficially to ce-work… the review is like a brainstorm in that requirements are collected."*

So what a review round produces is **new requirements**, numbered into the sprint chapter's Requirements section and carrying on from the last identifier. A correction is not a note or a to-do; it becomes `R35`, `R36`, and the work loops back to [ce-work](../../library/our-skillset/30-ce-work.md) against them — **unofficially**, without re-running plan for every one.

That is why review sits where it does: it is the loop's second intake of requirements, from *seeing* rather than from *describing*.

## Why it exists

Of the four adopted gates, **only brainstorm's is human** — *requirements approved by Doug*. Plan's, work's and compound's are all **marked by the implementer**. The workflow had a person at the front door and nobody at the back, and the failure was exactly what that predicts: a session reporting "630/630 green" with a red suite beside it and no demo at all.

Libby's argument is the one that settles it structurally: **every step produces one artifact**, and none of the four produces an artifact made *for Doug to judge*. A step whose purpose is a judgement cannot be a subsection of a step whose gate the implementer marks themselves.

## It is iterative

Not a report. **Batches.** Show a piece, hear what it turns up, fold it in, show again. Doug's shaping: *"I think it should be like an iterative brainstorm, and me checking the demo can be one."*

The same discipline [brainstorm](../../library/our-skillset/28-ce-brainstorm.md) runs applies: **one thing at a time**, never a battery; questions cheap to answer; and a correction is taken and folded in rather than argued.

## What a round carries

1. **The requirement walk — as a number.** How many requirements are satisfied, how many are not, out of how many. Stated, not implied. *A number nobody wants to say aloud is the one worth saying.*
2. **The epiphenomenal decisions.** Every judgement made in flight that no one ruled — the calls the implementer made with the code open. These are invisible by default and they are where the work most often quietly diverges. Surfacing them makes the *decision procedure* auditable, not just the result.
3. **The demo, filtered first.** Run our own *extremely well-designed* filter **before** Doug sees it, and say honestly where it might fail. An internal gate ahead of the external one.
4. **Questions cheap to answer.** Short, concrete, one decision each. **If reviewing costs an hour of reading, it will not happen and the step dies.**

## The verification it will not accept

- **A green number without the red one beside it.** Both suites, both counts.
- **"It renders" from a test container.** [Green → driven → *seen*](../../library/..teamsmanship/..team/queenie/test-architecture/04-the-three-rungs.md) — and *read* is the deepest rung.
- **A demo described rather than shown.**

## What it produces

**New requirements** in the sprint chapter's Requirements section, numbered on from the last identifier and never renumbered, plus the round recorded in *Where things stand* — cover updated in the same act by [the tool](../../library/bookkeeping/03-on-covers--toc.ts). The work then loops back to [ce-work](../../library/our-skillset/30-ce-work.md) against them.

**A round that turns up nothing new is how the step ends.** Not patience running out, and not permission being granted.

## And it makes work stricter

The demo is a **stop condition**, not a closing flourish: no unit reports done without one. That law already existed — the demo was already a requirement in Sprint 48's chapter and *seen* was already a rung — and it was broken anyway. This step is what makes it refusable.
