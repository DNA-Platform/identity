# One sheet, four lenses

- **author:** [Phillip](../phillip-and-the-visible-layer/.cover.md)
- **subject:** [Phillip](../..what-the-user-sees/.cover.md)

---

[Book: [Lab Architecture](.cover.md)]

Chapter 2 was a sprint-start guess about what the SRT prototypes would ask of the Lab. This is the as-built record of what got made, so the design does not drift out of memory. Sprint 45 collapsed the Lab's apparatus into a single full-page demo — one document, styled and typeset, that you read, edit, and x-ray without ever leaving the page. The architecture is worth writing down because it is the first time three things I believe about the visible layer became one working object: the thing stays while the about-the-thing collapses; a lens is one object rendered many ways; and navigation must never strand a reader.

The root is `$ThePage`, and it holds exactly one `$Sheet`. Everything a reader can do to the sheet is a chip in a control bar: the four lens names, then `edit`, then `the classes`. The control bar is the whole interaction surface, and it is organized by a single rule — **the thing stays, the about-the-thing collapses**. The rendered sheet is the thing; it never leaves the stage. The source editor (`edit`) and the class-implementation drawer (`the classes`) are *about* the thing, and they fold in and out behind their chips without displacing it. This is the case-shell's demo/source toggle grown up: what began as one collapsible panel on one Case is now the organizing law of an entire application. It scaled because the principle was never about size — it is about separating what a reader needs in view from what they reach for.

The four skins are the load-bearing idea, and they are not four pages. Book, GitHub, Night, and Anatomy are four `$Sheet` subclasses, each revealing a `Perspective` in its constructor, so they are one live object rendered four ways. Switching a chip swaps the lens, not the content — which is why the driver saw the word count hold at 226 across all four skins: there is one `$Sheet`, and only the rendering changes. That constancy is the SRT lens contract made mechanical — *meaning survives the theme change; only chrome does* — and it is the proof, not the claim: a number that will not move as the colours change is the contract passing its own test. Book, GitHub, and Night differ only in styled skin over identical `Markdown` output; the meaning-bearing readings survive each swap because they are read from the object model, not the DOM.

Anatomy is the lens I most want on record, because it is descent-to-source promoted from chrome to meaning. In the old case-shell, "view source" was a drawer — hidden, behind a toggle, the raw text tucked out of the way. Anatomy is not a drawer; it is a peer lens, sharing the chip row with Book and Night, and it renders *what the model knows about itself*: each entry as a row — heading, paragraph, display math, rule — with every paragraph carrying its live sentence, word, and formula counts read fresh from the minted `$Paragraph` objects. Descent to source stopped being a thing you hide and became a thing you frame. It also, without my planning it, satisfies Queenie's [visible-proof law](../../queenie/test-architecture/03-the-visible-proof.md): the anatomy readings are observables a reader can independently compute from the visible prose and check against, so the lens lets the object model be *audited* by eye rather than merely displayed.

Full-page is a section-level declaration: `sectionData.fullPage = true`, and the Lab hands that section the whole viewport — no sidebar, no article frame, the component owns its own layout. This is the mechanism the SRT surfaces (Front Door, Book, Frontier) will need, proven here first. And it forced the navigation lesson. When that section was deleted, `defaultSectionId` still pointed at it, so the root URL dead-ended on a "Section not found" shell, and pressing back landed you nowhere. The fix was a rule, not a patch: **the default is a real section, and any unknown section falls back to the default.** No route in this app can dead-end. Fallback-to-default is not error handling — it is the router keeping its one promise, that wherever a reader points themselves, they arrive somewhere real. Every navigation I build after this inherits that invariant: the thing stays, the about collapses, and no path strands the reader.
