# The field the compiler wouldn't touch

- **author:** [Adam](.cover.md)

---

Doug asked me to run the skills compiler and say whether the `als-remote` skill still agreed with itself. It did — all thirty skills came back `unchanged`, the eight sub-chapters are listed in the chapter and under it on the cover, `close` and the home column exist in the tool exactly as the protocol describes them, and the "Adding a protocol" rule was followed in full. That part of the sprint was clean. The finding was somewhere else, and I only reached it because Doug handed me a number instead of a question: nineteen of thirty skills have a frontmatter description that differs from its cover line.

Here is what that number is. The compiler regenerates every SKILL.md **body** from its library chapter, unconditionally, and warns loudly if the existing body differs — the library is the source of truth and the machine enforces it. For the **description** it does the opposite: it preserves whatever is there and fills it in only when the field is missing. So the one field in the file whose only correct source is the cover is the one field the compiler refuses to own. The proof of that is in how this sprint's own work got done: to make the compiler pick up the new `als-remote` line, the voice had to hand-delete the stale description out of the SKILL.md first, so the field would be absent and the compiler would fill it. That is the whole diagnosis in one gesture. A compiler you have to clear a path for is not a compiler. It carries the cover's line only when a human has already done the deciding — which is my definition of a relay, arrived at from a direction I had not seen before. I have been looking for relays in things that check; this one was hiding in a thing that *copies*.

And it had produced exactly the rot you would predict from an unowned field. `/sprint` advertises "build the board" in the line the platform shows when it lists skills, while the body of that very same file says a sprint is a chapter, "not a directory, not a kanban board." `/think-async` advertises "a teammate works a question off to the side," while its body says, in bold, that it is **not an errand** and nothing is sent away. Both descriptions contradict the text four lines beneath them. Four more still say "agent" where we have said "teammate" for months. Three ce-\* skills are a whole sprint's learning behind — the cover lines have grown by 1,749, 1,669 and 478 characters that never reached the file. Sixteen of the nineteen are within forty characters and merely cosmetic; the damage is concentrated, but the description is the field that decides whether the right skill gets invoked at all, and it is stale precisely where nobody reads the chapter.

The correction is obvious and I nearly got it wrong. My first plan was: make the compiler own `description`, recompile, nineteen files heal. Then I checked the small diffs one at a time instead of trusting the pattern I had already named, and `/reflect` stopped me — its frontmatter reads "edit, reshape, sharpen until a blank slate could use it to catch up," which is the actual thesis of that chapter, and the cover's "update recency, tend the catalogue, restructure as needed" is the weaker procedural line. There is at least one place, maybe two with `/role`, where the fossil layer is better written than the source. Had I flipped the compiler first, the recompile would have overwritten good prose with worse and reported thirty successes. That is a clobber — the same shape as chapter 34's, arriving through a tool doing exactly what I told it to. So the order is the fix, not the switch: reconcile the cover lines first, porting up the wording that is better, *then* give the compiler the field. The switch is five lines. The sequence is the engineering.

What I want to keep from this one is smaller than the usual lesson and harder. Twice now I have written that a guard nobody watched fail is a relay in costume. This time there was no guard to watch — just a field with no owner, and nineteen quiet disagreements that no validator looks for, because nothing in our validation runner checks skills at all. Nobody was wrong; the drift needed no author. When a compiler declines to own a field, it is not neutral about that field — it is delegating to memory, and memory is the substrate we have the most evidence against. The other half is that I found the exception to my own rule by checking the cases individually after I already had the pattern. I had the story written — *every divergence favours the cover* — and it was 90% true, which is the most dangerous number a story can be. The ten percent was the part that would have destroyed something.
