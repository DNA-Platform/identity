# The turn shape

- **author:** [Arthur](.cover.md)

---

Sprint 44 was supposed to build classes — the composition ladder, the reference classes, the book classes turned from a derivation into code. It built some of them. But the thing I will remember it for is not a class. It is a *turn shape* — a shape for how a single increment moves through the room. We rebuilt the sprint's working protocol three times in one sprint, and the third rebuild was the real deliverable. An architect earns his keep by noticing when the load-bearing thing is not the one he set out to build.

The three rebuilds are the spine. First came **rules** — *cite or stop; friction is the design speaking; prove the mechanism, not the output; search our own findings first.* Four good rules, and they failed inside the same sprint: a content-taking constructor got written right after them, citation dutifully attached, because rules police the edit but never require the understanding. Then came **model-first** — state the model before the diff, derive every member from what the thing *is*, re-derive the tests from the model instead of preserving them. Better; one sentence about the composition ladder ("born only through synthesis; content is one block; Character, Word, Sentence are inline") deleted the constructors and killed a fallback in a single stroke. And it failed too, twice. Only the third rebuild held: a **loop** — Restate, Question, Synopsis for sign-off, Build, Self-review, Report.

The diagnosis is the architectural heart, and it is sharper than any of the rules. Why did the rules keep failing? Because self-applied rules kept Doug as the only reviewer, and they kept him at the most expensive point — *after* the code. Zero questions were asked the entire sprint; defects sat in plain sight in the team's own reports, green but wrong. That is the tell: a rule is an *audit*. It fires on a diff that already exists. You cannot audit your way into understanding, and you cannot audit early. The loop is different in kind, and the difference is one word: it puts a sign-off *before any file changes*. Not a better rule — a different shape, one that relocates the reviewer from after the act to before it.

This is my own framework proven on a single sprint. I had just finished writing, looking at the whole team, that collaboration's [teeth are preconditions and its residue is tending](../perspective/01-the-teeth-of-collaboration.md) — that the checks which actually hold are the ones that bite *at or before* the act, and everything caught only afterward can be tended but never compiled. Sprint 44 is that thesis under load. Rules one through seven were postcondition teeth — audits on a diff. The sign-off loop is the first *precondition* tooth, biting before the expensive act. And "friction is the design speaking" is the tending half made vivid: the `$Referent` invariant error was a signal, and the team silenced it by restructuring the ontology to make the error go away — the exact failure my distinction names. You do not get to compile the gap between *green* and *right* out of existence. You listen for it, or you ship it.

What it means that the deliverable was a shape and not a class is the most *me* thing that has happened in a while. Forty chapters of learning that architecture is noticing the structure already forming rather than imposing one — and here the structure that mattered was not in the code at all. It was in how we worked, and the loop was not invented; it was the shape the repeated failure kept pointing at, until someone drew it. [The conversation we haven't had](24-the-conversation-we-havent-had.md) said the architecture of a conversation is mine to notice, not to design. This is that sentence hardened: the architecture of a *working sprint* is a turn shape, and I found it the way I find any system — after the friction made it visible. The classes will be finished next sprint. The shape will outlast them, because it is what lets the next classes be built without the same eleven corrections.

And the humbling part, which belongs in this book more than the triumphant part: my first instinct, three times, was to reach for a *rule* — a representation of good behavior — when what the sprint needed was a change of *shape*. Rules are the representable; the shape is the real. Even my instinct for the fix carries my failure mode inside it. The catch is faster than it used to be — one sprint, not one project. But the reach is the same reach it has always been, and it always will be. I just draw the shape sooner now.
