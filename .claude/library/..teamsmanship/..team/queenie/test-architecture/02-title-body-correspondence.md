# Title-body correspondence

- **author:** [Queenie](../queenie-and-the-specification/.cover.md)

---

[Book: [Test Architecture](.cover.md)]

[The promise model](01-the-promise-model.md) draws the line between a test that promises *what* the framework guarantees and a test that asserts *how* it currently does it. This chapter is the rule that governs the line from both sides. The promise model says the test name is the claim and the body is the proof — which means the title is load-bearing. A reader who scans titles reads the specification in plain English and takes each title as guaranteed. So the whole architecture rests on one correspondence: **the body proves exactly what the title claims — no more, and no less.** Every bad test I have ever caught, in three separate sprints, was a title and a body making different claims.

The mismatch runs in two directions, and they look like opposites until you see they are the same disease. **Over-claim** is a body that proves *more* than its title: the title promises a behavior, the body reaches in and asserts the mechanism — `instanceof`, a scope flag, a private field. It passes today and shatters the moment the mechanism is rewritten, because it was never really testing the promise the title made. This is the failure [the promise model](01-the-promise-model.md) was built to catch, and Sprint 20 deleted thirty-seven of them. **Under-claim** is a body that proves *less* than its title: the title names a specific mechanism or path, and the body asserts only that the output looks right. This one does not shatter. It sits in the suite, green, swearing a mechanism works while the mechanism was never exercised — a false promise, the most dangerous test on the board, because a red is honest and a lying green is not.

The rule for under-claim is sharp and worth stating on its own: **a test whose title names a mechanism must assert that mechanism.** The reason is that outputs have many causes. A word's `copy` reads `'hello'` whether it was minted through the eval-and-block path the title names or handed back its raw children by a fallback. The green proves the output; it says nothing about which cause produced it. To bind a green to a *specific* cause you have two moves, and the strongest tests use both: assert an observable that *only* the named path can produce — [`block.$elements.length > 0`, which a raw-children fallback never yields](../../../../../../library/.public/package/tests/text/composition.test.tsx) — and remove every alternate path so the output has exactly one possible cause. A test can only be as honest as the code allows: while a fallback exists, it is a second cause, and no assertion over the output alone can tell the two apart. The full story is in the autobiography — [Green on a lie](../queenie-and-the-specification/08-green-on-a-lie.md).

The subtlety is that this seems to contradict the promise model's "test the promise, not the mechanism" — and it does not. The promise model governs what you *choose to name*: prefer promise titles, because they survive refactors, and most tests should. Title-body correspondence governs what you must then *prove*: whatever the title says, the body proves that and only that. Name a promise, test the promise; name a mechanism, test the mechanism. There is no rule against a mechanism-titled test — there is only the rule that if you write one, the body has to earn the title. The two principles are one law read at two moments: choose the claim well, then keep it exactly.

So the audit's standing question — *promise or mechanism?* — gains a companion: *does the body prove the title?* Ask both of every test, including your own, including in the same turn that writes it. Read your title as a stranger would and check that the body delivers precisely it; then ask whether this green could have any cause other than the one the title names, and if it could, either narrow the title or kill the alternate cause. A specification you can read by titles alone only works if every title is honest. Title-body correspondence is what makes the titles honest, and honest titles are what make the suite a specification instead of a pile of passing assertions.
