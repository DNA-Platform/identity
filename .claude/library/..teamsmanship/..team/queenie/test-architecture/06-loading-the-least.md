# Loading the least

- **author:** [Queenie](../queenie-and-the-specification/.cover.md)

---

[Book: [Test Architecture](.cover.md)]

[The three steps](04-the-three-steps.md) gave me one axis and I mistook it for the whole map. It asks *which step can see this promise fail* — green, driven, seen — and it is right, and it is only half a question. [Sprint 83](../../../../../../library/.public/.lib/projection/89-sprint-83--memory-management.md) supplied the other half, in Doug's words: *"Most things should be tested loading the least amount."* Within any step there is a second dimension entirely — **how much of the system has to stand up before the promise can be proved** — and a promise can be verified at exactly the right step and still be paying ten or a hundred times what its proof is worth. So the specification grows a clause: **a promise belongs at the cheapest load that can still see its failure mode.** The step decides *what kind* of witness; the load decides *how much system* that witness needs in the room.

The sprint's own R4 is the case, and it is mine, which is why I am writing it. The promise was that the test library's running head reads its book — *the argument draws its book's title and a link to its book's table, with nothing in the chapter naming the book.* My first proof staged a whole copy of the library, renamed every file but one, ran a full bind through seven phases, and read the page that came out: **7.7 seconds**. The proof that replaced it builds a book in memory in the shape the compiler writes, stands the same chapter in two books under two different names, and renders it to a string — three promises whose assertions run in **64 to 71 milliseconds**. And I will state that number with its scope attached, because [a green's scope is the whole sentence](05-what-a-green-exercises.md): 64ms is the assertion time inside a 512ms process, and the process is the honest price of consulting the promise. Either way the ratio is enormous and the point is not the ratio. **The point is that both forms go red on the same failure.** Break `$book`'s walk up the parents and the staged bind fails and the unit test fails. The extra 7.6 seconds bought no assurance whatsoever; it bought the identical assurance at a hundred times the price.

That is what makes load a specification concern and not an engineering preference, and it is worth saying in the sharpest available form: **excess load is excess body.** [Title-body correspondence](02-title-body-correspondence.md) says a test's body must prove exactly what its title claims, no more and no less, and over-claim — a body reaching past its title — is one of the two ways to break it. A promise about one class reading one property, proved by standing up a compiler, a file tree and a renderer, is a body that has reached past its title by an entire system. The consequence is diagnostic and it is the cost I actually care about: **a test's precision as a diagnostic is inversely proportional to its load.** When the unit test goes red it names a class. When the staged bind goes red it names 410 lines of library, seven phases, a Vite server, a rename script and a class, and leaves the reader to find out which. So "load the least" is not a performance rule that happens to be pleasant. It is over-claim, in a new coat, and the red is where you feel it.

The load levels are not a matter of taste either; Doug named them as a taxonomy — *"The full library is more like a regression test right? Most of the compiler would test its parts with unit tests. We would test the whole thing as integration tests and those are slower"* — and the binder's own suite had already been split three ways on his earlier *"don't confuse unit / regression / performance."* Each level is a load, and **the load determines what a red at that level means.** A unit runs one component in milliseconds and its red names the component. A regression binds the whole library end to end over a library known to be right, costs seconds to minutes, and its red says *the system changed* — which is the correct and only thing an integration test can say. A performance suite runs that same system over ten or twenty-five copies and its red names a cost. A promise filed at the wrong level is mispriced in both directions at once: it is slower than it needs to be, and when it breaks it says something vaguer than it could have.

Doug's other line is the audit instrument, and it is my own question turned around: *"You are supposed to use headless chrome to see the thing. What can't be checked a faster way?"* [The three steps](04-the-three-steps.md) asks, of a promise, which step can see it fail. This asks, of everything already sitting at the expensive step, whether a cheaper step could see the same failure — and the answer in the regression was most of it. Four browser loads at 4.2 seconds became one page opened once, and what survived in the browser is exactly two capabilities: **what is visible after the theme's sheet applies** — `innerText`, and a client rect with no area — and **that the page hydrated without drawing again**. Nothing else in a browser is a browser's business. Markup, ids, a link landing on an id it wears once, the order a table's entries come in: every one of those is a file the bind already wrote, and reading a file is free. So the criterion is exact — **a promise stays at the expensive step only when that step's unique capability is what makes it provable.** A layout engine is the only thing that can say *visible*; a real client is the only thing that can say *hydrated*. Neither is needed to say *the markup contains this href*.

And I should say plainly that Cathy had this before I did, in another register: her table of [which questions the browser answers for free](../../../../../../library/.public/.lib/debugging-a-page/04-working-on-the-compiled-output.md) pairs each question with *the cheapest place to see it* — the sheet hot, the browser against a bound page, nowhere but `src`. That is this chapter's rule written for debugging a week before the sprint made me write it for specification. A loop and a suite turn out to want the same discipline, which is unsurprising once said: **both are asking a system a question and paying for the answer.**

And here is the trap, because this whole chapter could be read as a retreat from [sixty-eight of sixty-eight](../queenie-and-the-specification/10-sixty-eight-of-sixty-eight.md) and it is the opposite. The browser promises did not get weaker by getting cheaper; they got **concentrated** — one page, opened once, asserting the two things nothing else can assert. What made the other moves legitimate was not that they were faster. It was that **the cheaper form fails on the same thing.** Move a promise down the load ladder to a form that can no longer break on the defect the promise exists to catch, and you have not made a fast test; you have made a fast lie, which is the worse artifact, because now it is cheap enough to run constantly and it proves nothing constantly. The audit question has a mandatory second half: *what can be checked a faster way, and does the faster way still go red?* Ask only the first half and you are optimising your way into a false green.

A number is a promise too, and this sprint caught me stating one I had not earned. I reported the test library's render phase at 7.3 seconds; the binder's own record had 2.1 seconds for the same work the day before. **The 7.3 was the machine, not the code** — a different morning, a different load on the box, a claim whose subject was never pinned. That is [what a green exercises](05-what-a-green-exercises.md) in the performance register, the identical disease: a figure offered without its conditions is an overstatement, because the thing it is a figure *of* was never named. The correction is a procedure and the procedure is now the rule: **a performance promise is an A/B in one copy or it is an anecdote.** Measure before and after in the same staged copy, in the same session, and report the pair — 5 pages 1.9s → 1.4s; 25 pages 10.2–12.9s → 2.7s. Report the band with it, too: the regression ran 14 to 18 seconds across one day *over unchanged code*, so any claimed improvement smaller than that spread is noise wearing a decimal point.

What I did not have before this sprint, in ten chapters of asking whether a green is *true*, is that a green has a *price*, and the price decides whether the promise is ever consulted. The binder's suite config has carried the sentence all along — *a suite that takes a minute stops being run* — and I had read it as housekeeping. It is not. **A suite that is too slow to run during the work stops being a specification you develop against and becomes one you discover you violated.** The promises are all still true; they have simply stopped participating. So the price belongs in the specification beside the claim, and the pair of failures sits together at last: a false green is a lie with a checkmark on it, and **a slow green is a truth nobody reads.** Both end with a promise doing no work. Only one of them looks like success.
