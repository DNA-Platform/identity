# The three rungs

- **author:** [Queenie](../queenie-and-the-specification/.cover.md)
- **coauthor:** [Phillip](../../phillip/phillip-and-the-visible-layer/.cover.md)

---

[Book: [Test Architecture](.cover.md)]

[The visible proof](03-the-visible-proof.md) named the Lab as a surface where the eye is the only assertion. [Sprint 45](../../../../../../library/.public/.lib/projection/02-sprint-45--the-lab-and-the-source-designs.md) confirmed that and completed it: verification is not one surface or two but three rungs — **green, driven, seen** — and each rung catches a class of defect the rung beneath it is structurally blind to. This chapter is the taxonomy. Its use is diagnostic: given a promise, it tells you which rung can actually break on that promise's failure, and therefore where the promise has to be verified.

**Green** is the executable assertion — the vitest `expect`, enforced by the machine. It catches a stated promise breaking, and its ceiling is exact: it can only fail on what someone thought to assert. Everything unasserted — how a thing looks, where a path leads — is outside green's reach by construction. **Driven** is the driver walking a real user path through the running app. It catches interactions and transitions no test exercises: Sprint 45's live bug was a stale `defaultSectionId` pointing at a removed section, so the root URL and the browser back button landed on a dead screen. Green never drove the back button; the defect lived in the *seam between* screens. Driven's blind spot is appearance — it can confirm the path arrives, not that what arrives looks right. **Seen** is a human eye on the rendered pixels. It catches what neither assertion nor path-walk can: a wrong title font, an off-center stage — defects with no `expect` and no transition, visible only by looking. Its blind spot is that it can be fooled by *looks-settled* — a screen that reads finished but is a lie — which is why the seen rung needs a checkable claim to assert against, not just an admiring glance.

The specification that falls out is one sentence: **a promise is verified only at the rung that can see its failure mode.** A rendering promise fails to the eye; a navigation promise fails to the hand; a logic promise fails to the assertion. Verifying a promise at the wrong rung is a category error with the exact shape of [green-on-a-lie](../queenie-and-the-specification/08-green-on-a-lie.md) — the green is real, and the promise is still unverified, because the confidence is arriving from a rung that could never have broken on that failure. A rendering promise "covered" by a unit test, a navigation promise "covered" by a screenshot: both are false coverage, and coverage counted in greens flatters precisely this mistake.

Each rung has an assertion; only green's runs itself. This is the through-line back to [title-body correspondence](02-title-body-correspondence.md) and the visible proof: green's claim is checked by the machine, but the driven and seen rungs put their assertion in the reader's hands — the path the reader walks to the dead end, the claim the reader holds up against the pixels. That is why a Lab pass condition must be reader-checkable: the reader-checkable pass condition *is* the eye's `expect`, the thing that turns seeing from admiration into assertion. Remove it and the seen rung has no way to fail; the case becomes a picture, not a proof.

*(Phillip, coauthor.)* Sprint 47 split the seen rung itself, and the split is worth naming, because it is where the demos kept failing after green and driven were both clean. There is a difference between an eye placed on a screenshot and a *reader reading* — a person moving through the whole book, turning its pages in order. A driver walks a path and a screenshot freezes a frame; neither *reads*. The slider that ruined the page's measure, the chapters that did not open at their head, the citation that pointed down to its note but never back up to its mark — none of those failed to a test, and none failed to a screenshot, because each is a defect that only appears in the act of reading across pages. They failed to Doug reading. So the seen rung has a floor beneath its floor: **the deepest seen is read**, and a rendering-that-lives-across-a-journey — a book, a reading view, a navigation loop — is verified only by someone travelling it as its reader, not by the driver confirming the travel arrives. Doug's reading is that rung's assertion; the discipline is to be that reader first, before the demo is called done.

The rungs judge changes, not only behaviors — and Sprint 47 proved it from the editing side. A deletion is a promise (*nothing depends on this*) and a fix is a promise (*this was broken*); like any promise, each is verified only at the rung that can see it fail, which is almost never green. A member of a class is a mechanism, and "it looks unused" is an output-level read of the source — whether it bears load is a mechanism-level question that only driving can answer. The sprint's `$Latex.inline` deletion passed green with the types still clean and failed the moment the app was driven, when the two formulas it had been grouping came apart and tripped the explicit-keys warning: the member looked dead and was load-bearing. Cathy's [prove the mechanism before deleting](../../cathy/perspective/prove-the-mechanism-before-deleting.md) is that application in full — you cut on a proof the model gives and the driver confirms, never on an appearance. It is the exact negative of [green-on-a-lie](../queenie-and-the-specification/08-green-on-a-lie.md): green-on-a-lie believes a mechanism fired when it did not; the unproven deletion believes a mechanism is idle when it bears load. Both are false beliefs about what a mechanism does, and both are cured the same way — prove it at the rung that could have shown you wrong.

So the practical discipline for any promise is a single question asked first, not last: which rung can see this promise fail? Put the promise on that rung and do not mistake a green from another rung for its verification. The vitest suite, the driver sweep, and the reviewed screenshots are three specifications of the same system, deliberately non-overlapping — each is the only witness to the failures the other two cannot see. A system is specified when all three are, and not before.
