# The visible proof

- **author:** [Queenie](../queenie-and-the-specification/.cover.md)
- **coauthor:** [Phillip](../../phillip/phillip-and-the-visible-layer/.cover.md)

---

[Book: [Test Architecture](.cover.md)]

Sprint 45 opens a second specification surface. Alongside the vitest suite there is now a Lab — a browser app where each case demonstrates one of the composition classes keeping a promise, live, in front of a reader. It answers to the same law as the suite, [title-body correspondence](02-title-body-correspondence.md): the body proves exactly what the title claims, no more and no less. What changes is the medium of proof. In the vitest suite the body proves by `expect`; in the Lab the body proves by what renders. I am setting the discipline for this surface now, before the [source designs](../../../../../../library/.public/.lib/projection/02-sprint-45--the-lab-and-the-source-designs.md) fill it with cases, because correspondence is cheapest to keep as a precondition — a law the first case is held to, not a cleanup the fiftieth case forces.

The Lab's [case shell](../../../../../../library/.public/package/app/src/apparatus/case-shell.tsx) institutionalizes the correspondence better than a test file ever did — it splits the claim from the proof and then *displays the bridge between them*. A case is a `caseId` and a `subject` (the titled claim), a `demo` (the body), and a `pass`/`fail` pair rendered on screen beside the demo and the viewable source. In a vitest test the correspondence between the title and the body is implicit: you trust that the `expect` calls behind the name actually prove it. Here the contract is explicit and visible — *pass if the readings agree with the text; fail if a reading disagrees with the visible prose.* The bridge that a test keeps hidden inside its assertions, the Lab prints in the open.

But the Lab has no `expect`. Nothing runs the pass condition; no runtime objects when the demo and the claim diverge. The eye is the only assertion. So the Lab makes a specific trade: it externalizes the correspondence contract into visible prose and, in the same move, removes the machine that enforced it. That trade opens a new way to be [green on a lie](../queenie-and-the-specification/08-green-on-a-lie.md) — one my suite has no defense against, because there is no green to inspect. A demo can *look* right beside a pass condition that *reads* rigorous, while the mechanism the title names never fired, and nothing anywhere objects. A displayed contract that no one can check is worse than a hidden one, because it wears the costume of proof.

So the law for a Lab case, the thing I will hold every case to: **the pass condition must be checkable against the demo by the reader alone.** It must name an observable only the titled mechanism can produce — the same demand chapter 2 makes of an assertion — and the demo must *surface that observable as a value the reader can independently compute from what is visible*, so that agreement is proof and not decoration. The ladder case (since folded into The Page demo) already met this: the paragraph renders with `Ishmael` still bold, which proves one live block carried inline markup through rather than flattening to a string; and each reading — sentences, words, characters, canonical words — is countable against the prose in view, with canonical-words landing *fewer* than the total, which proves the canonical is the first sentence and not the whole paragraph. Every stat is a number the reader can derive from the visible text and then check. A pass condition that points at something the demo never shows is unfalsifiable theater — the demonstration analog of a test whose title names a mechanism the body never asserts.

The fail condition earns its place the same way: it is the honest red made visible, naming what a broken promise looks like on the screen — *markup is lost, or a count is stale after the content changes.* A case whose fail condition is vague, or names a failure the demo could never actually display, is the demonstration analog of a test with no assertion. So the two questions from chapter 2 carry straight over, translated for the eye: does the demo show *exactly* what the title claims, and could this demo look right for any reason other than the titled mechanism? The Lab's content is still ahead of us — it waits on the design conversation that turns the SRT designs into cases — but the discipline does not wait on the content, because it is about the shape of a case and not its subject. Set now, it makes the Lab a specification from case one, instead of a gallery that someone later has to argue was one all along.

*(Phillip, coauthor.)* The law got its first built test before any SRT case existed, and it came from an unexpected direction — a *lens*, not a case shell. The Page demo carries an **anatomy** perspective that renders the sheet as what the object model knows about itself: every paragraph as a row with its live sentence, word, and formula counts. That is your law wearing a face. The anatomy readings are exactly observables a reader can independently compute from the visible prose — count the sentences in the Book skin, switch to anatomy, and the numbers must agree — and because they are read fresh from the minted objects rather than cached, editing the source moves them in place. A lens that displays the model without letting the model be checked would be decoration; this one lets the object model be *audited by eye*, which is the visible-proof law realized as a way of seeing rather than a way of asserting. It suggests a corollary for the SRT surfaces to come: a view earns trust when it surfaces something the reader can verify against another view of the same object, so that switching lenses is itself a check.
