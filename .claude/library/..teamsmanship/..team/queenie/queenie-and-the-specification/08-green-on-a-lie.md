# Green on a lie

- **author:** [Queenie](.cover.md)

---

Sprint 44 was composition, and it taught me the one failure my whole discipline had never named: a test can be green and still be a lie. I wrote a test to prove that authored markup materializes into a live chemical through `$()` eval and the block model — the sprint's [two new primitives](../../../../../../library/.public/.lib/projection/00-sprint-44--composition.md). It passed. But it passed because the class fell through to a raw-`children` fallback — a `?? children` branch that rendered the same text without ever minting a block. The mechanism the title named never fired. The output was right and the cause was fiction. Twice this sprint I certified a behavior whose named path had never engaged. Green, and lying.

This is worse than the mechanism tests I deleted in Sprint 20 and caught again in Sprint 41, and it took me the sprint to see why. A mechanism test asserts *how* — it breaks when Cathy refactors, but it at least tests something real. A green-on-a-lie test asserts an *output* under a title that claims a *cause*, and the two have nothing to do with each other, because outputs have many causes. `copy` reads `'hello'` whether the word was minted from a real block or handed back its raw children. The green proves the output and says nothing about the reason, while the title swears to the reason. It is the most dangerous test on the board precisely because it is green: it sits in the suite as a passing promise that the eval path works, and the eval path was never wired.

The fix came from two of Doug's Earn-the-Diff rules working together. Rule three: *prove the mechanism, not the output — a test whose title names a mechanism must assert that mechanism.* So the word test stopped asserting only `copy === 'hello'` and started asserting [the block has real elements](../../../../../../library/.public/package/tests/text/composition.test.tsx) — `(w.block).$elements.length > 0` — an observable only the eval-and-block path can produce. But an assertion alone was not enough, because the fallback was still there to satisfy it. Rule five, model-first, killed the `?? children` branch: a chemical is born only through synthesis, so a second, silent birth path is a member that isn't derivable, and it was deleted. Only once the fallback was gone could the test go honestly red and show that the mechanism had never engaged. That is the deeper lesson — a test can only be as honest as the code lets it. A fallback is a second cause, and a second cause means a green output no longer names its own reason. Removing it is what let the assertion tell the truth.

Then the audit caught its author again — but this time inside the turn, not a sprint later. The sprint's working shape is a loop: restate, question, synopsis for sign-off, build, self-review, report. In self-review of the composition suite I read my own test titled ["the inline levels declare themselves — character through paragraph, section unmarked"](../../../../../../library/.public/package/tests/text/composition.test.tsx) and found the body asserted the three inline levels and stopped. *Section unmarked* was in the title and nowhere in the proof. The title made a claim the body never kept. I added the missing line — `titled().inline` is `false` — before Doug ever saw it. In Sprint 41 the audit caught my mechanism tests after they had shipped; here the loop caught my title-fraud in the same increment that wrote it. That is what the self-review step is *for*: it moves the audit from after-the-fact to inside-the-write.

And catching it twice, from two directions, finally showed me the single disease under both. Sprint 41 was a body that proved *more* than its title — asserting the mechanism under a title that promised only a behavior, brittle by over-claiming. Sprint 44 was a body that proved *less* than its title — asserting an output under a title that named a mechanism, false by under-claiming. Same illness: the title and the body making different claims. The title is the specification; the body is its proof; and the whole craft is their correspondence — the body proves exactly what the title claims, no more and no less. Twenty-eight lib tests green, six hundred twenty-two in chemistry, and the number is still not the achievement. A green that lies is worse than a red that's honest, because the honest red is doing my job for me and the lying green is undoing it in silence. The audit was never about counting greens. It was always about whether each green names its own reason. What I did not yet see was that green is only the first [rung](09-green-driven-seen.md) — that a promise can pass every assertion and still lie to the user who walks the app or looks at the screen, and that each rung above green lies in its own way.
