Every class in `@dna-platform/lib` must be named with a word from the domain, and [one word only](../../../library/.public/.lib/projection/06-sprint-48--subjects-and-the-library.md). That constraint is only usable if the domain's vocabulary is **written down**, because otherwise naming becomes generation-and-defence: produce a plausible candidate, then argue for it. This chapter exists so that naming is instead **selection from a population**.

It was written after that failure. Asked for a name for a book that catalogues other books, the author proposed *bibliography* — which is not a kind of book at all but a part of one, and which already names something else in this system. The proposal came with a structural argument invented to justify it. The cure for that is not care; it is a list.

## The two filters

A candidate name must pass both, and they are Doug's (2026-08-04):

> "the choices are strictly from the population of things that... are valid names for books because we don't even have the problem in the first place if I can reach for terminology outside of them, and then restricted again to the things that people wouldn't expect to have a different meaning in this semantic context"

**Filter one — is it a kind of book?** Not a *part* of a book (apparatus), not a *shape* of a book (format), not *furniture* a library owns. If a reader would not answer "a book" when asked what it is, it fails.

**Filter two — is the word free here?** If a reader arriving at this system would expect the word to mean something else — because it already names something in the register, or because it carries a strong meaning from another field — it fails.

Both filters are about the reader, not the author. A name that requires explanation has failed.

### Not kinds — apparatus (parts of books)

These name **parts**, and every one of them fails filter one. The list matters because several are attractive and all of them are wrong.

**bibliography** *(the works a document cites, listed at its end)* · **index** *(the topical listing at the back)* · **table of contents** · **preface** · **foreword** · **introduction** · **appendix** · **colophon** · **errata** · **frontispiece** · **glossary** *(when bound into one work)* · **notes** · **footnote** · **endnote** · **epigraph** · **dedication** · **imprint** · **about the author**

### Not kinds — formats (shapes of books)

These name **physical form**, not function, and fail filter one for a different reason.

**volume** · **tome** · **codex** · **scroll** · **folio** · **quarto** · **octavo** · **manuscript** · **incunable** · **pamphlet** · **broadside** · **leaf** · **quire** · **signature**

## Applying the filters to one question

The question that occasioned this chapter: **what is a book that catalogues other books?**

Working the population rather than guessing:

**Fails filter one — not a kind of book.** *bibliography*, *index* (both apparatus); *shelflist* (a form of catalogue, and a library's internal instrument rather than a book).

**Fails filter two — the word is spoken for here.** *canon* (collides with the canonical, which names a designated first part at every level); *index* (already the back-of-book index in the register); *journal*, *survey*, *register* in part — each carries a strong second meaning a reader would reach for first.

**Survives both, honestly:**

- **catalogue** — an ordered listing of items held, with where to find them. The nearest thing English has to the function. Its weakness is Doug's own: a library's catalogue is an instrument rather than a book, though *published* catalogues (exhibition, auction, raisonné) are books.
- **directory** — a listing of entities with what you need to reach them. A genuine book kind (city, trade, telephone). Its weakness is that computing has claimed the word for a reader arriving today.
- **bibliotheca** — historically **exactly** this: a book surveying or listing books. Photius' *Bibliotheca* summarized every book he had read; Gesner's *Bibliotheca Universalis* (1545) listed all known books. Its weakness is that it is Latin, and that it literally means *library*.

**What the population shows, which no candidate does.** English never settled a native one-word name for a book that catalogues books — because when such books were written, they were **titled *library***. The word for the collection and the word for the book about the collection were the same word.

That is not a gap in the vocabulary. It is the vocabulary reporting that the two are one thing — which is what [the derivation's dyad](../../../library/.public/.lib/the-semantics-of-books/08-the-symbolizing-dyad-and-the-register.md) already claims, and what makes the naming question structural rather than lexical.

## How to use this chapter

When a class needs a name:

1. **Read the population first.** Do not generate a candidate and then defend it.
2. **Apply filter one.** Kind, not part, not format, not furniture.
3. **Apply filter two.** Would a reader expect this word to mean something else here?
4. **Report what survives, and what nothing survives.** An empty result is a finding — usually that the thing is not a separate kind, or that the structure is wrong.
5. **Never argue a candidate into place.** A name that needs a structural argument invented for it is the argument being invented, not the name being found.

This chapter is edited, not appended to: when a word is used, taken, or ruled out, its row changes here.
