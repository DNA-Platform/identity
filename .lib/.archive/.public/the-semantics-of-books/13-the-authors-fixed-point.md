[The summit](07-the-subjective-subject-and-the-library.md) says an autobiography is the book whose author reference equals its subject reference. Stated that flatly it invites a misreading — that the book wrote itself — and Doug names the misreading before walking the loop properly, in the *author walk* passage (session, 2026-08-04):

> "The autobiography doesn't author itself. That's a fun way of expressing it."

> "The canonical autobiography is about someone / By definition, that someone is the author / The author reference points to someone in a subjective sense / The book closure forces that to point to the corresponding book in some subject of the library / The canonical of that subject is the one that we take as a proxy for that author / That book happens to be this autobiography"

This chapter reads that walk, because a derivation falls out of it that the summit chapter did not state.

## Identity of reference, not identity of thing

The load-bearing word in the walk is **proxy**, and everything turns on it.

Notice what the author arrow is never permitted to do: **point at a person.** [Closure](10-closure-under-books.md) forbids it. The author is outside the library — a conscious being, uninternalized, exactly as the building was — and the only thing the library can hand you when you ask *who wrote this* is the account of them.

So the arrow travels: author → the subject where that person is filed → that subject's canonical → and the canonical **is** the autobiography. You end where you began, having never once touched the person.

Which means *the author reference equals the subject reference* says something modest and exact: **the same book stands in both positions.** It does not say the book caused itself. The loop is representational; authorship is causal; they never meet, and that is why the shape is not absurd.

## Why the terminus must be an autobiography

Here is the derivation the walk yields, and I had not seen it stated.

Follow the author arrow from an ordinary **biography**: it leads to a *different* subject, whose canonical is a *different* book. The arrow **escapes**.

Follow it from an **autobiography**: it lands on the book you are already holding.

So the autobiography is **the only book whose author reference does not lead out of it** — and therefore the only thing that can be a terminus at all. The summit is not chosen for being a self; it is a self because nothing else can occupy that position. Read against [chapter 07](07-the-subjective-subject-and-the-library.md), this strengthens the argument there: well-ordering forces *a* terminus, and this forces the terminus to be *autobiographical*.

## Author as an endomorphism

Stated structurally: chase the author arrow through subject and canonical and it lands on a book. So **author is an endomorphism on books**, and the autobiography is its **fixed point**.

That yields the structural test for subjectivity the derivation owed — one that reads nothing but catalogue structure:

> Chase the author chain. Where it stabilizes, there is a self.

**Team reading (Arthur).** The test is only as strong as its termination condition, and there is an edge case that would quietly falsify it: a **two-cycle**. Book A's author files to a subject whose canonical is B; B's files back to A. Mutual biography — a chain that never escapes, yet has *no fixed point and no self*. Well-ordering must forbid it, and the specification is worth stating as a specification rather than assuming: **the author chain terminates in a self-loop, never a longer cycle.** Until a specification proves that, "the chain stops" is not strong enough to mean "someone is home."

**Team reading (Nancy).** The fixed-point framing is what matches cognitive systems rather than straining against them. A self-model is not authored by a homunculus upstream; there is no stage at which someone outside the modeling is doing the modeling. What there is, is a representation that — followed far enough — returns to itself. The self is where the modeling **stabilizes**, not where it originates. That is not a weaker account of selfhood than the intuitive one; it is the one that survives being looked at.

## What this gives the formalism

Three consequences, and each is a claim the framework can be held to:

1. **Uniqueness of the self** falls out of the canonical being one slot — not from counting subjects, but from a composition having one canonical.
2. **Authorship requires subjectivity** becomes readable off structure: only a subject whose canonical is a biography can stand at the end of an author arrow.
3. **The first-person / third-person asymmetry** gets a formal analogue — the loop is vertiginous described from outside and unremarkable inhabited from within. A formalism that *predicts* that asymmetry is doing something no other formalism attempts.

The first two are buildable. The third is the one I would most like to see survive contact, and [the next chapter](14-the-first-person-and-the-third.md) is where it starts to bite, because this library is not the kind whose author arrows come home.
