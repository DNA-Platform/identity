## Writing, the base class

**Every object of the `.public` document API is a piece of writing, and a piece of writing is what came into it, sorted once.** What is written inside a `<Writing>` arrives at its bond constructor as chemicals; those that are annotations go to `annotations` and everything else to `contents`, in one pass, and nothing is computed from a stored source on every read. The two are collections: `contents` an ordinary [`Collection`](../../package/src/utilities/Collection.ts), `annotations` an `Annotations`, the collection under `$Annotation` in the same file, where adding means the front. A writing draws itself as its `container`, a span until a class or an annotation says otherwise, carrying the `classes` its annotations put on it, with its contents first and then its annotations, each a note on the page; the story of that rendering is the `view` at the top of the class, and `write` and `annotate` beneath it are the two halves it renders with.

```tsx
<Writing>an aside <Parenthetical /></Writing>
<Writing is={Narrative}>drawn after all <Parenthetical /></Writing>
```

A writing knows nothing of any annotation's meaning. It asks its collection type questions — `annotations.contains($Parenthetical)`, whether an expressed one is there — and it lets the annotations act. Everything a reader of the class needs to hold in mind is in that sentence.

## Annotations, the plugin system

**An annotation is a piece of writing that stands in another and acts on it, and it is how a piece of writing is customized.** The design is plugin-like on purpose. The base declares nothing about any annotation; each is a small class in the file of the writing it belongs to, or in a library's own file, and a library adds annotations by writing classes, never by registering them anywhere — *"Yeah sure annotations"*, on being asked whether a class is simply imported where it is used.

**An annotation has FOUR powers, and they are the whole of its contract.** *A fifth stood here for one day of 2026-09-23, `inactivates`, and Doug struck it:* ***"I didn't invent inactivates. NO NEW MEMBERS. You set expressed to false."*** *Regulation is not a power of its own — it happens inside `defines`, where a gene reaches its family and sets a property on what it finds, which is the same act by which it augments or reads them.*

- **`defines(writing)`** — acts on the writing when the writing defines itself: adds a class to `classes`, adds a layer to `containers`, sets a value, **or reaches a sibling and asks the collection, `writing.annotations.express(annotation, false)`, to say that sibling does not apply here**. Called at every define, at the writing's bond and at the start of every `view`, after everything that ran is erased, so it must be exact in its undo rather than guarded against running twice. *A define runs from the first; an annotation reaches what stands after it with `after(this)`, and the collection records what it called `defines` on — the first is the most powerful, Doug's* ***"It's like a stack"***.
- **`erase(writing)`** — takes back what `defines` did. Every define calls it first on every annotation that ran in the last one, last first, whether it is still there or not — *"Erasing is a part of define() on the collection"* — so an annotation negates itself and nothing else needs to know how.
- **`specifies(writing)`** — weighs in on the binder's assert: a failed `$check` here is one of the failures the writing answers when the binder asks it to specify. A Summarized demanding a summary; a Cover allowed only on a chapter; a Strict composition's parts exactly one level below.
- **its note** — an annotation is a note on the page. It is rendered inside the writing it annotates, after the contents, as its own writing with `pd-annotation` on it, so what someone wrote inside it is on the page, hidden in the ordinary view and shown in an annotated one; and then its `note()`, null by default, renders at the level of the writing it annotates. One with something to say there, a global style, an anchor, a footnote, overrides `note`. Parenthetical's note is the styled global style that hides `pa-parenthetical`. *Doug: "Having the annotations annotate their writing with the pd-annotation, which we can easily make invisible, yet to a note function which renders at the level of their annotated writing... is semantically what an annotation is!"*

**An annotation's argument is its content.** *"We probably didn't want a prop there, we meant that to be `<Mentioned>as-mentioned</Mentioned>`."* An annotation is a writing, so what is written inside it is its `contents`, and that is where a Referent finds its id, a Reference its url, a Format its rules. From outside the same annotation is given as an element, `is={<Mentioned>as-mentioned</Mentioned>}`.

**Expression is the collection's, and a define computes it.** The annotations collection answers `expressed(given)` — the expressed annotation or nothing, asked of an annotation or of a class — and an annotation, while it runs, says which of those behind it are expressed with `express(annotation, false)`; *"tracking is kept in the collection"*, and nothing in a define changes an annotation. Every define starts everything the genome holds expressed, so nothing accumulates and an annotation that leaves lets what it took out of expression express again. A page turns an annotation off by giving one that takes it out of expression through `$is`; a hand outside a define does nothing.

**`$is` is the changes, `annotations` is the full set, and what `$is` gives stands strictly in front.** Where a child annotation is the constant form, `is={Narrative}` or `is={[Formal, Narrative]}` is the dynamic one: a prop that reads as what was given, taking a class, a component, an element with its content, or a chemical. The given lives on the annotations collection, which is the one place to comprehend them: setting it makes the edits at once, and every define stands them at the front, in front of what was written and of what the class stood, cited to the collection so that `revert(this)` takes back exactly them — *"they need to be registered to… the piece of writing and can be undone"* — while what they did is erased from the record of what ran.

## How annotations interact

**Annotations interact by order, by taking each other out of expression, and by erasure, and every one of those is visible in the list.** The annotations are one list, and an annotation enters it at the front, so the last to enter acts first: what `$is` stands acts before everything, then what was written, the later written before the earlier, then what a class stands in `$Define`. When two annotations want the same thing, the one nearer the front says it first, and the one behind may say it again or take it back.

- **Taking another out of expression.** An annotation loops over what stands after it, `writing.annotations.after(this)`, **from inside its own `defines`**, and asks the collection, `writing.annotations.express(annotation, false)`, of what it chooses; Narrative does exactly this to every Parenthetical after it and nothing more, and the one taken out never runs. *Every define begins by expressing everything the genome holds, so nothing accumulates and an annotation that leaves lets what it repressed express again.* **The collection records what it called `defines` on**, so an annotation reaching back to one already reached changes nothing it says — *Doug, 2026-09-24: "It should be responsible for knowing where it is. It can know where it is on the list and focus on what comes after."* **The first is the most powerful** — *"First is most powerful… It's like a stack."*
- **Erasure.** An annotation never deletes another's mark; it takes the other out of expression and the define never runs it, and every define has each annotation that ran erase its own. So a family of annotations stays small and its mechanism documentable: what an annotation adds, it alone removes.
- **Shared surfaces.** Annotations meet on the writing's own properties — `classes` and `containers`, collections in which what each annotation adds is cited to it, so its `erase` is `revert(this)`; the `id`, a Compilation where the last value set wins — and a define runs over what it established, so an annotation may interact with its siblings but never add to or remove from the list while it runs.

A define is the annotations' own, since many annotations define: it erases every annotation that ran in the last one, last first; stands what `$is` gave at the front; establishes the membership every read sees until the next; starts every annotation expressed; and runs them from the first. It runs at the end of the bond and at the start of every `view`, and the second leaves the writing as the first did.

## Composition structures, annotations customize

**A Composition is a piece of writing that structures the contents of a book, and annotations customize what a composition is.** Composition adds a level, keeps `parts` as a Collection of the writing among its contents, computes its depth against its parent and names its canonical, the first element, per level; Word, Sentence and Paragraph are compositions at one, two and three, Section at four with a Heading as its canonical, Chapter at five with a Title, Book at six with its Cover. Cover, Synopsis, Table of Contents, Subject, Author, About, By, Format and Theme are annotations placed on writing and found by asking the collection; a chapter is a cover because it carries the Cover annotation, not because of its class.

**Composition's pairs are annotations too.** Open and closed, permissive and strict were once to be a stored prop and an accessor over it; Doug, 2026-09-22: *"We want to use our annotations as much as possible. Those traits can be expressed through specify right?… We are annotating the writing with the word permissive. If performance hurts us we will reconsider."* Our opinion, asked for: yes, and they belong there, because each is a constraint on what the writing may hold, and a constraint is exactly what `specifies` says. Strict's `specifies` checks that every part is one level below; Permissive's that every part is at or below; Closed's that nothing but writing stands in the contents; Open's waives that. None of them touches the element, so `defines` has nothing to do and the cost is paid only when the binder asks. A level stands its own defaults in `$Define` — Word, Sentence and Paragraph permissive and open, Section closed, Book closed and strict — and an author overrides by writing the other or giving it through `$is`, the pair negating each other as Narrative and Parenthetical do. How each of the Genesis's classes is expected to arrive through Writing's seams is in [How Writing Is Extended](06-how-writing-is-extended.md#how-the-genesiss-classes-are-expected-to-arrive).

## Annotations that reach the element

> ***THIS SECTION IS THE RECORD OF THE 2026-09-22 TRIAL, and it is kept as the trial rather than corrected into the outcome.*** **What shipped the next day is smaller than any of it:** *there is no Theme class, no `theme` property on Writing and no `format` member — theming is `theme = true` on a [Format](11-format-and-theme.md), whose styled component is composed inside a provider once in its bond.* **Read [Format and Theme](11-format-and-theme.md) for what exists and [Theming and Formatting](02-theming-and-formatting.md) for the four ways tried and why three lost.** *What follows is worth keeping because the measurements in it are the reason the small version is the one that works.*

**Format and Theme are annotations whose action reaches the element `view` returns, and both were tried on 2026-09-22 in [`.tests/theme.test.tsx`](../../package/.tests/theme.test.tsx), in the promise file and not in the language, because a trial answers whether a thing works before it earns a place in `src`.** Doug: *"Try it! I don't know this tech well enough, so do an analysis of the performance and implementation concerns. Does it work? … it seems like what you are saying will allow us to theme statically and dynamically with annotations."* The Genesis gives each its shape: a Format is *"a place for themes and styled components; no constraints, like Letter"* (E23), styled-components serve directly and Format is where they serve (ruled 2026-09-21); a Theme *"takes only Format; often contains a styled-components theme provider"* (E24) and *"is an ancestor in the render tree because chemistry renders where it wants"* (E25). And chemistry already has the seam E25 describes: a chemical whose theme is not the one it was handed has what it draws wrapped in styled-components' provider, the provider remade only when a named value of the theme changes and handed as a function so the nearer theme replaces the farther — [`styled.ts`](../../../chemistry/package/src/abstraction/styled.ts), `providing`, called at every particle's render.

**Format works as an annotation that holds its styled component on a property and lends it as the writing's container.** Doug, on a first trial that had used the Format's own `container` for the purpose: *"one is polymorphic and the other isn't. I would have the system store the component on a property: `format = StyledComponent`… And then can't it be more easily overridden in a subclass? That can be what the format class does. But design it, analyze the design and see if it makes sense."* The design: `format`, a property of Format holding the styled component, a span by default so a Format that says nothing changes nothing; a subclass overrides it, `override format = Quotation`, which is the polymorphism that content as the carrier lacks, since content is what an author writes and a class cannot override it; `defines` remembers the writing's container and sets it to the format, and `erase` gives the remembered one back. The writing draws as the styled element, with the rule in the sheet: styled-components does inject into happy-dom's document, and the promise reads the rule back from the page's style elements. It makes sense because the Format class does one thing and its subclasses say one thing, and because the Format's own `container` keeps meaning what it means on every writing, the element its own `view` would return inside the annotations' span, instead of doubling as the thing it lends. What follows from it: a writing is drawn as one element, so at most one annotation may set its container, and that is a rule about an element and not about how many annotations may style a writing; two annotations that both set the container resolve by order, the one nearer the front acting first and the one behind winning, and that is fine as long as neither makes a new component per pass; and *Means modifies Format on render* (E23) has its place, a reference acting on the Format it finds beside it, which is the next thing to design when Means arrives. Not tried: a Format holding styled components as content, which E23 also allows and which would arrive at its bond as raw elements of a function type.

**Theme works by the writing placing styled-components' own provider around what it draws, and does not work by wrapping the container.** Three ways were considered and two tried:

- **Wrapping the container** needs nothing of Writing: the Theme's `defines` remembers the container and stands a component that renders the provider around it. It fails in three ways, all one fault. Two annotations acting on one surface break each other's idempotence: a Format behind the Theme finds a container that is not its own and lends again, the Theme then finds a container that is not its wrapper and wraps again with a new component, and every pass makes a new component. Written with the Format in front it renders once and loops — *Too many re-renders*, the same error the classes set cost the day before, from the same cause. Written with the Format behind, or given through `$is`, which acts first, the wrapper is simply undone. The promise states all three.
- **Chemistry's theme seam** was tried first and works: a chemical whose theme symbol answers something other than what it was handed has the element its `view` returns wrapped by chemistry in the provider, remade only when a named value changes. Doug: *"Well we are redesigning. We can use styled components as we see fit. Is this what we want to do? We don't need to use styled chemicals."* It is not what we want. That seam was designed for styled chemicals, classes whose CSS fields read a live theme, with a field walk per render and a face over the theme to keep getters live; we use styled-components directly, by his ruling of 2026-09-21, and a dependency designed for something else is the wrong thing to stand on. It goes on running for every writing and returns the element unchanged, since the writing's theme is the one it was handed, so it does not interfere.
- **The writing's own provider** is what was kept: the Theme's `defines` sets the writing's `theme` to itself and its `erase` clears it, and the writing's `view` returns its element inside styled-components' `ThemeProvider` when it has one. The theme is handed as a function returning the annotation, for the reason chemistry hands one too: styled-components spreads an object theme over the outer theme, and a chemical's values live on its class template and are reached through the prototype, so a spread would lose them. The function is new per render and the object it returns is the same, so the styled components beneath see one identity and re-render only when the theme changes. The styled component beneath reads the value, statically as a child and dynamically through `$is`; un-expressing the Theme redraws without it; the order of Format and Theme does not matter, since one acts on the container and the other on the theme.

**What it costs, and what it needs.** One provider element per themed writing and nothing per render beyond it; a theme's values changing in place are not seen, and the annotation model does not change them in place, it switches themes by `expressed` or `$is`, which changes the identity and is seen. A theme appearing or leaving changes the shape of the element tree at its root, so React remounts what is beneath it; switching from one theme to another keeps the provider and does not. Styled-components hashes one class per distinct rendered rule, so a Format under two Themes makes two classes, lazily, which is its ordinary cost. **What it needs of Writing:** the `theme` property, a surface the annotations act on as `container` and `classes` are, and the wrap in `view`; the trial did both on a subclass, so nothing entered the language. E24's *takes only Format* is a `specifies` on Theme, and E25 holds: the theme is an ancestor of the writing's drawing because the writing draws it there.

**Seen in happy-dom only.** The rules were read back from the document's style elements and the tags and classes from the rendered tree; no browser was driven. ***That is still true and still owed*** — the class shipped on 2026-09-23 and nothing about it has been seen in a browser since, which is the one open item on [Sprint 80](../projection/86-sprint-80--format-and-theme.md).

***What the trial actually decided, which is why it is kept.*** **The one fault it found is the fault the shipped design is built to avoid:** *two annotations acting on one single-valued surface break each other's idempotence, and a pass that MAKES a component makes a new one every time.* **Both halves are answered in one line of the class that shipped** — the composing happens in the bond, once per mount, and every Format unexpresses every other so two never act on the container at all. *The record of the four ways and why three lost is [Theming and Formatting](02-theming-and-formatting.md); the class is [Format and Theme](11-format-and-theme.md).*

## Developing a class of writing

**Class development means providing computed properties over contents and annotations, and deciding which annotations a class should have by default.** A class of writing extends Writing or one of its levels directly, names its bond constructor after itself and calls Writing's first, and then does three kinds of thing:

1. **Computes over the two collections.** `parts`, `canonical`, `depth`, `title`: readings over `contents` and `annotations`, computed on the way out and never cached, asked through the collection's `find`, `contains` and `containsOne`.
2. **Stands its default annotations in `$Define`.** The hook is empty on Writing and called first in the bond, before the written are added and before the first pass, so a class that is always an aside writes `this.annotations.add(Parenthetical)` there and the bond's pass already sees it. What a class stands is behind what the caller writes, which is behind what `$is` gives: base, subclass, caller.
3. **Specifies itself.** A specification class extending the parent's, with a rule per invariant, `$check`-ing the shape of the thing; `specify` is written and called by nothing in the library, since the binder is what checks, cascading through everything in reach.

And it draws: a class overrides `view` to change its container or its placing, keeps `view` at the top of the class with the TSX stacked and indented, and leaves the rest terse. Every class gets a chapter of four parts, what it is with members cited, how it is extended, its promises and its gate, as [How a Class Is Documented](../the-coding-style/08-how-a-class-is-documented.md) rules; the code patterns every class obeys are the dated rows of [The Coding Style](../the-coding-style/03-the-coding-style.md#code-patterns).

## Overriding, which is customization

**The nature of annotations is that they can easily override each other, and that is how a library customizes what it did not write.** A library that wants every aside drawn stands Narrative through `$is` on the writing it did not author, and the written Parenthetical erases itself. A subclass that wants its own default stands it in `$Define`. A page that wants an annotation off for a moment flips its `expressed`. In each case nothing is removed, nothing is rewritten, and what the author wrote comes back the moment the override leaves.
