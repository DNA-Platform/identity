## The algorithm and the identity, stated (a draft for the report; nothing is in the deck)

Doug, 2026-11-19: *"a beautiful algorithm ... and probably needs to be described a bit more formally in the report."*

**Whose it is.** Moving a neuron virtually, and MEI against type, are from the painting (Doug, 2026-11-07). Raising one
objective over a population is his generalization of the MEI's algorithm (*"It was my idea to general the MEI
algorithm"*). With a type standing for a channel, the type at every spot is the published channel objective (Olah,
Mordvintsev and Schubert 2017, https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/). What was found here is the fixed
point and the invariances.

**The objects.**
- *The twin* f takes an image x to the neural activity of the recorded neurons. Neuron i is a type θ_i (its weights
  on the core's features, and its bias) read at a place μ_i of the core's map: f_i(x) = a(θ_i, μ_i; x). The same
  reading at another place p, a(θ, p; x), is the neuron moved virtually. **Exact**: the twin's architecture
  (`painting.moved`, checked to 2e-7).
- *Raising.* R[J] is the image the lab's recipe (Walker et al. 2019, as the pipeline runs it, start 0) ends on when
  it climbs an objective J. The MEI of neuron i is R[a(θ_i, μ_i; ·)].
- *Completion.* For a set of places S, the tiling T_S(θ) = R[the mean over p in S of a(θ, p; ·)]. The MEI is the
  tiling of one place. The pattern is the tiling of every place of the map: the MEI completed.
- *The pattern literal* is f(pattern): the twin's neural activity for it.
- *Magnitude.* An image multiplied about its own ground: grey level -> ground + c (grey level - ground). Its
  gradation is its intensity at the neuron's place over the MEI's own.
- *The meaning in our space* is Φ(r) = (literal, natural, featureful)(r), the three features of `three_qdas.py`.

**The fixed point** (**measured**, all 548 neurons, chapter 1 entries 3 and 7). For every regular S with spacing
under about 0.6 of the pattern's own spacing, T_S(θ) is the pattern: the same image by its regularity (1.00) and the
same Φ (within 3 bits either way). So a type's pattern is one class, every fine enough tiling of the type, and
completing any member returns the class.

**What the pattern is to its type, and where the fixed point ends** (**measured**, all 548 neurons, entry 5).
- *Uniform.* Under its pattern a type answers the same at every place: over the whole map its answer varies by 12% of
  its mean (6% to 22% by neuron), and by 7% (3% to 15%) weighted away from the map's border, where it varies most. The pattern is the image that expresses the unit equally at every place; the MEI
  expresses it at one.
- *Nothing to gain on a fine grid.* On square grids of spacing 2, 3 and 5 places the image the recipe makes raises
  the grid's spots exactly as much as the pattern does there (1.000, 1.000, 1.001 of it), and the places between as
  much as the spots. So the pattern is what the recipe finds for every fine grid.
- *Something to gain on a coarse grid.* At spacing 8 and 12 the recipe finds an image that raises the spots more
  than the pattern does (for 71% and 91% of neurons; by 1% and 5%) and the places between less (0.62 and 0.60
  against 0.64). That image is the grid's, not the type's.
- **Not explained:** why the changeover lies between 0.76 and 1.2 of the pattern's own spacing. My account of it by
  aliasing (a type answering as the square of a filter's output, so varying at twice the pattern's frequency) is
  **withdrawn**: tested in entry 5, and the answer map has nothing at twice the pattern's frequency.

**His theorem** (chapter 1, entry 11; his words: *"if the tiling is invariant then surely the unit should be able to
be expressed anywhere individually as well"*). By place it is two lines (**exact** given the fixed point): the type
at one place and the type at another are each completed to the same pattern, so whatever is the same for a member
and its completion is the same for the two. Which reading he means is not confirmed.
