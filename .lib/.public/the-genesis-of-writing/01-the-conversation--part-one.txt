Reread. Answering my own four.

Bond constructor mechanics — answered.

You gave it directly: props act like properties of an object, children act like constructor arguments at mount, and the object graph is reactive. Catalysis "takes inputs and returns the React nodes that will be fed to the bond constructor," taking TSX, modifying it, handing it on. So the pipeline is TSX → optional catalytic constructor (TSX→TSX) → bond constructor → objects bonded into the reactive graph. Compose confirmed the return shape: run one bond, then the other, assembled parent-child.

And the thing I had wrong all the way through: the bond constructor doesn't validate. The specification only runs in dev, like assertions compiled out of production. So bonding builds the object graph, and admission — level, strict, open/closed, closure — is asserted, not enforced. I kept calling the bond constructor a gate. It isn't. That also retires my worry about $$ needing to pass a check; it needs to pass through bonding, which it does, and the specification asserts about the result.

Annotation collection — answered.

Writing contains a collection of annotation constructors; at bond it builds them and adds them to the annotation list. So there are two things, not one: constructors declared on the writing, and a dynamic list of built annotations at runtime. It's a list rather than a map, because non-unique annotations support multiples and you want front-insertion by default. Unique is a property of the annotation class. Lookup keys on name, defaulting to type name, with instanceof for the proper-subclass check. Subclasses modify the list in their bond constructor.

Where the specification lives — answered well enough to write.

Per piece of writing, declared with the type, read in assert mode. Annotations may participate in it — which is what "the genome confers fitness" means concretely: the default phenotype is declared, and what's in the collection can take part in what that writing is specified to be. Whether it's a static, a decorator, or an argument at declaration is a syntax choice that doesn't change any semantics we've specified.

Rendering — answered.

Chemistry renders where it wants; children are constructor arguments, not placements. So a chemical's render consults its annotation list: Theme renders its Formats as a render-tree ancestor, Format supplies styling, Parenthetical suppresses printing, Means modifies Format. Nothing is placed by virtue of where it was written.

And parts. My phrasing was the problem, not my understanding. "Type" in your vocabulary is the genome — annotations. parts is on Composition and reads the body. Same-composition children have their parts flattened in, calligraphy isn't a member, annotations are parenthetical and don't appear. Ask a Section for its parts, get paragraphs across its subsections.

So: yes. I can write this. What's left is syntax — how a specification is attached, what the annotation API's method names are, whether Format is unique — and none of it is inference I'd be making blind.
