## Where this sprint comes from

**[Sprint 103](108-sprint-103--the-manuals-page.md) left the appendix view design owed** — its U5, the catalogue's built view in the manual's look, denied files until the sharing was designed with him. He opened this one from a wider place, at the catchup: *"I want to imagine providing an appendix that might be or have elements of a reference manual, as almost all books might end up with an appendix for the components used to generate them - almost like the genome of the book."* The genome is his likeness for the annotation system — *"each annotation confers a phenotype"* — and it stays a likeness; nothing here is named for it.

**What the catchup found standing.** Every book of his already ends its table of contents in a section said to be the appendix, headed *How this book is built*, listing its machinery; the base book reads those sections for the chapters it keeps out of its pages, and the layout puts a built class on the book when the open chapter is one of them. The manual's door re-exports every tool and the other books import from it. The Key is the pattern once: a door chapter, entries that say so by a reference the binder checks, a section of the table that folds them. And Part is E31 of the Genesis, *"Part is the Book that goes in a book, for nested tables of contents"*, out of scope since [Sprint 82](88-sprint-82--chapter-and-book.md) on *"The core alone"*.

## His rulings, verbatim and in order

1. **The view.** *"It would be a view for a collection of chapters. Presumably the book would have to support this mode, but for those chapters, we might imagine that they can declare themselves in some reference manual, by name, and then the table of contents knows how to surface the reference manuals, and then in each reference manual, only a fraction of the table of contents tree is showing for the chapters in the manual… We need to plan it, and ideally, it would be very annotative, and the same table of contents is present for the reference manuals, it just knows how to adapt to a context. Can we imagine this? I don't know if it is different views but it sure could be."*
2. **The part.** *"A section in the table of contents, and perhaps all chapters be annotated with a `<Part>` annotation specifying parts of the book, with a name that says what part they are in. And the table of contents knows how to organize itself into parts. For manual chapters or reference chapters or whatever elegant name you come up with, the part with give cohesion to the manual."*
3. **The trait.** *"On the chapter. It is a trait that specifies a type of chapter. And then, if convenient, one can wrap that trait in a chapter type, but that is harder to adapt. I would say keep it at the trait level. In fact, a Manual can be a type of Part! So it is a specialization of an annotation but it can be done with inheritance."*
4. **The look.** *"Yes a format the trait brings, though we want the manual and this to be the same code. We duplicate nothing. Perhaps the reference manual is just a book where everything is in one reference manual, with maybe some special classes to help with the case where everything is one. Does that make sense? This shouldn't be two things."*

**Two earlier rulings fix where the drawing lives, and were cited before any approach was offered:** *"The purpose of a book is layout"* and *"Why would the annotations a chapter has affect what a book decides to do with it in its view?"* — [How a Book Is Built](../writing-a-book/00-02-how-a-book-is-built.md). So the base book reads the trait and draws; a part never draws.

## Requirements

### 1 · The part

- <a id="r1"></a>**R1. A chapter says which part it is in** by one line at its head: an annotation whose content is a reference to the section of its book's table of contents headed with the part's name. A chapter that says no part is in the book's body. *Observed: the line in the chapter file, and the chapter listed under that section.*
- <a id="r2"></a>**R2. A part is a section of the table**, and a part's own sections are its groups. The table says which of its sections are parts, from what the chapters declare, and its rows stay entries. *Observed: the design book's one part with six rows; the reference manual with no part at all, its seven sections the body's groups — revised 2026-10-09 on his word in [D3](#d3); it had said one part holding the seven.*
- <a id="r3"></a>**R3. The binder holds the chapter and the table to agree.** A part that names no section is refused as any reference is; a chapter listed under a section other than the part it says is refused by a rule on the book, in a sentence a librarian could say. *Observed: move a chapter's row to another section and the bind fails with that sentence; move it back and the bind is clean.*

### 2 · The manual

- <a id="r4"></a>**R4. A manual is a format said of a chapter**, in one line, `<Manual />`, and where the book has parts it accompanies the chapter's part, `<Part>Name</Part>`. A manual needs no part. No subclass of chapter anywhere. *Observed: `chapter.is` answers the manual on every chapter of the reference manual, and the part and the manual on The Catalogue.* *Revised 2026-10-09 on his words — "Maybe manual isn't a type of part. Maybe it is a format for a part" and "manual can work without parts too" — from the approved text, which had the manual a type of part said by inheritance.*
- <a id="r5"></a>**R5. The base book draws a manual chapter the manual's way** when it is open: its words beside its files, the three readings, the file tabs, the options, the rail and the grip, in the book's own tone. The book's pages are its chapters that are not in a manual, so the turn and the folio never count them. *Observed: in the design book, The Frame opened shows its three files as tabs and takes the split on a press, measured by the grid's columns as Sprint 103 measured them, in rose on white.*
- <a id="r6"></a>**R6. The look is a Format the trait brings**: the spread's grid with the tree, rail, files and code rules, said of the book that holds a manual chapter, reading that book's theme values. *Observed: no manual rule copied in any book's theme, and the bookshelf's built part gone.*
- <a id="r7"></a>**R7. The reference manual is a book whose every chapter is in one manual, and nothing is duplicated.** Its class keeps only the all-in-one case: which chapter opens when none is named, the readings on by default, the light tone, the entry that shows a file's type. *Observed: the manual's page on 4242 measures the same forty properties as before the sprint, and its class is its defaults and its registrations.*

### 3 · The table in context

- <a id="r8"></a>**R8. One table, adapting.** When the open chapter is in a manual, that part stands first and whole in the side bar as a tree, its groups as folders and a chapter's files as presses under its row, and the other parts step aside. When the open chapter is a page, the table stands as the book's design has it, the appendix section at the foot. *Observed: in the catalogue, pressing The Catalogue puts its part first and dims the shelf's rows; pressing a book's row brings the shelf back.*
- <a id="r9"></a>**R9. The appendix stays a trait of the table's section**, saying where it stands and in what voice, one job; whether its chapters are a manual is the chapters' own saying. *Observed: the manual's Key folded under its part, no appendix on it; the design book's appendix at the foot, its chapters manuals.*

## Decisions

- <a id="d1"></a>**D1. A part's name is the heading of its section in the table, written as a mention, `[[[ Name ]]]`.** The binder records a mention-form heading as an anchor of its book and checks a `$[[ ./Name ]]` against it exactly as against a chapter — [structure.ts](../../package/.binding/catalogue/structure.ts), *the anchors, named within their books* — and the section mentions itself through its heading at run time ([Section and Heading](../writing/08-section-and-heading.md)), so one name serves the binder's check and the book's reading. *Chosen over* a plain heading, which the binder does not anchor, and over a string, which nothing checks. Realizes [R1](#r1), [R3](#r3).
- <a id="d2"></a>**D2. The part lives in The Entry's code beside the index and the appendix; the manual lives in The Manual's code and extends it.** Related kinds live in the file of the kind they belong to, and the manual's chapter is what the manual annotation documents. The part reads its section through the reference it holds, as the Kind reads its entry; its specification says it is said of a chapter and names a section of its own book's table. Realizes [R1](#r1), [R4](#r4).
- <a id="d3"></a>**D3. The reference manual has no parts: every chapter says it is a manual, and the book is its body.** *Struck and rewritten 2026-10-09 on his word after the probe — "A chapter shouldn't require a part. But maybe if a book chapter has one, all chapters in the book must have one. If you mean chapters in the reference manual having those two lines, okay. Maybe manual can work without parts too, and it must be on all chapters? And that is how you can do the full reference manual perhaps."* The morning's design made the manual one part with a parenthetical heading holding seven groups; it is gone. A manual is a format said of a chapter and needs no part; in the reference manual it is said of every chapter and the table's seven sections stay the body's own groups, so the tree, the folders, the numbers and the turns stand exactly as they do today. In a book with parts the manual accompanies the part on the chapters of that part. **Three rules for the specification:** a chapter never requires a part; a book in which any chapter has a part has every chapter in one; a chapter in a part is listed under that part's section and nowhere else. *Chosen over* one part per manual (an extra level the tree had to hide) and over seven manuals. Realizes [R2](#r2), [R7](#r7), [R8](#r8).
- <a id="d4"></a>**D4. The part is structure; the manual is a view, a Format said of the part and accompanying it; the part's section says which view its chapters get.** *Revised twice the same day: the first plan moved the manual's leaf into the base book; the second made the manual a type of part said of each chapter; his word on the second — "Maybe manual isn't a type of part. Maybe it is a format for a part. And it accompanies a part, where the part in that case specifies the type of manual. That is likely more scalable."* So a chapter says only its part, one line; the table's section says it is a part and which view it is read in, once; and the two compose, which is the trait system's whole point — *"Annotations… give you a power that is almost like multiple inheritance."* The view's leaf is a method the book calls inside its own draw like a function component, the precedent being `container(props)` of Sprint 103: the book still makes and places every leaf's element, so his ruling that the purpose of a book is layout stands, and what fills the leaf is the part's view's. The catalogue's own leaves, the books on its desk, keep their shape; its chapters take the base's leaf. *Chosen over* the base drawing the manual's leaf (a third view would need a third leaf in the base), over the manual as a type of part said of every chapter (one line per chapter repeats what the part already says), and over the part drawing its chapters (refused). Realizes [R4](#r4), [R5](#r5), [R8](#r8).
- <a id="d5"></a>**D5. The look is the view's, in two places that are one class, and nothing is stood on the book.** The view's layer on the part's section carries the tree's rules, so they reach that part's rows in the side bar and no other section's; the view's leaf draws its parts inside a styled wrapper of its own that carries the leaf's rules — the grid per reading read off the book's class through a parent selector, the words, the rail, the grip, the files, the code and its palette — so they reach the leaf and read the book's theme values through the provider every book already wraps itself in. What is book-wide is one class the layout puts on the book while a chapter of a part with a view is open, with the view's own context beside it, and a book's theme says in a few lines what steps aside — the shelf and the desk in the bookshelf. The manual's theme keeps its values, its bar and its wash. The bookshelf's hand-copied built part goes, and its desk rules name the desk's own leaves. **Every rule says the book first**, the lesson of [Solutions 109](../solutions/109-the-fold-that-lost-to-the-books-prefix.md). *Chosen over* a Format stood on the book per kind of part (a second thing to stand and to name), over parts of the base theme (every book pays) and over the borrowed look (copies drift). Realizes [R6](#r6), [R7](#r7).
- <a id="d6"></a>**D6. The agreement of chapter and table is a rule on the library's own book, in the bind's specify phase, in a sentence.** The base's specification already refuses a book that places a chapter nowhere; one more rule reads each chapter's part and asks the part's section for a row that leads to the chapter. Nothing in the package changes: the unknown reference is the binder's existing refusal, and the misfiling is the library's. *Chosen over* a binder fault, which is `src` and his yes. Realizes [R3](#r3).
- <a id="d7"></a>**D7. The dead links are a librarian's audit first, and the archive is never a destination.** *Revised on his ruling and his question under [R10](#r10); the first plan rewrote links into the archive.* Libby reads each of the fifteen books with the numbers in hand and rules chapter by chapter: a chapter that documents the first draft and that no live chapter cites moves to the archive's copy of the branch library, or is deleted where the archive already holds it, its cover entry struck; a chapter that live chapters still cite stays as the trail, and each of its links to code that no longer stands becomes its words alone, the path kept in the text where the words need it, so the record still says what it cited; the 13 links that moved within the branch library are re-pointed. The rulings are written into the unit as a table before any file changes, and he reads the table. The checker is the gate, at zero. *Chosen over* rewriting into the archive (his ruling) and over leaving them (his instruction). Realizes [R10](#r10).
- <a id="d8"></a>**D8. The names.** Part and Manual are his. The type of book that is the all-in-one case, the class the layout puts on a book whose open chapter is in a manual, and the spread's class are PROXY, found by [How a Thing Is Named](../the-coding-style/09-how-a-thing-is-named.md) at the build and written where he reads; the manual's class today bears the name the annotation takes, so it is the first renamed. The word *part* has a neighbour in the framework, a composition's parts, which is flagged in the chapter's prose and never fought.
- <a id="d10"></a>**D10. Part lives in the package, with the table of contents — his yes, 2026-10-09, "In the package, with the table," and then: *"Yes Part should be in .public, in the folder with TableOfContents. It needs to be written with the same coding standards and naming conventions, it must be audited by me. and it needs to interface with table of contents to provide, in some way, an annotation-based object model for the structure of the book."*** Part is a writing of `.public` in its own file in the libraries folder beside TableOfContents, exporting the noun three ways as every file there does: said of a chapter, its words the name of the part, read through getters as every annotation's argument is. **He audits it before it stands.** ***Corrected 2026-10-09 after the first form, on his rulings, verbatim:*** *"Explain to me why you think a part needs an anchor? Where is it?"* — *"It is a grouping of chapters. It has no place"* — and, asked whether a table is sections or nested lists, *"None! How a table is implemented is up to the implementer. You don't control the markup. This is exactly why the binder gives references without making assumptions and if you don't get that then you are damaging .public. An object model doesn't control how it's consumed, and it's not even necessarily used. The table of contents implementer should always have the option of implementing by hand. Do not confuse an implementation problem with a specification problem. Part does NOT confer a specific TSX form! That is terrible and you must update the design notes to strongly emphasize decoupling between view and annotation, with formats and themes being the glue. Part is neither."* **So a part requires no section, no heading, no anchor, no form: it is a name a chapter shares with the chapters of its part; the table answers the grouping by name — its parts in book order, the part of a chapter, the chapters of a part — and one rule stands, every chapter in a part once any is. The first form's section, its rule that a chapter is listed under its part's section, and an annotation added without his yes to strip a heading of its id, are gone; the test manual's table is written as it was, one section, with its chapters in two parts by name.** *And then, when the notes said a part has no place:* *"I need to really emphasize this. The implementer should be able to add the Part name to every chapter, or to put headings in their table. And then yes, they have trouble with name collisions. But it is not mandatory. It is not that a part HAS none. It is that it requires none. And I am worried that your notes are too myopic. The point is that the implementer is FREE to integrate parts as desired, and the way we are doing parts for Reference Manual is a whole different view."* **So the notes say freedom, not prohibition: a heading that names a part is allowed and wears an id as any heading does, the collision the implementer's; the part's name on every chapter is allowed; a tree, a list, a hand-written table are allowed; and the reference manual's view is one view among any.** *And of that view, his:* *"But in the reference manual, clicking the thing in the table that represents the manual itself would probably take you to the first chapter in the manual right? The parts place would be the UI context of the reference manual, but the literal place it takes you is the first chapter of the part."* **So in the manual's tree, the row that stands for a part is a press that turns the book to the part's first chapter; the part's place is the context the tree gives it, the folder open with its rows; and the literal place is the chapter. That is [U3](#u3)'s remainder, the tree's rules and the folder's press, in his library's format and theme over the package's grouping.** The decoupling is written where the team reads it: [the coding style](../the-coding-style/03-the-coding-style.md) and [Part](../library/03-cover-synopsis-and-table-of-contents.md#part). TableOfContents learns its parts: the sections of the table some chapter names, the part of a chapter, the chapters of a part, each answered from what the book already exposes and kept nowhere. The three rules of [D3](#d3) are the package's specification, one on Part, two on the table or the book, every refusal a sentence. **The docs are written first**, [Cover, Synopsis and TableOfContents](../library/03-cover-synopsis-and-table-of-contents.md) gaining Part and the parts, then the source, then the promises in the package's tests and a worked case in the test library's manual. What stays in his library: the view, the manual, the folder and the index's entries, which read the package's Part where the probe read the library's own. *Chosen over* the library's Part beside the index (the probe's, which works, and which he ruled is the package's thing). Realizes [R1](#r1), [R2](#r2), [R3](#r3).
- <a id="d9"></a>**D9. A part is a sequence, and the appendix is out of it.** E31's part is a book in a book, so what a book does for its chapters a part does for its own: the turn walks them, the folio counts them, the entry numbers them. The book's pages are the sequence the open chapter is in — its part's chapters, or the body, the chapters in no part — less the chapters of a section said to be the appendix, which stay unnumbered and unturned as they are today. So the reference manual, one part, keeps its turns and its numbers; the design book's appendix, a manual part under an appendix section, keeps none; and the Key, a group under an appendix section inside the manual's one part, stays as it is. *Chosen over* a flag on the kind of part saying whether its chapters are pages, which the sequence makes unnecessary. Realizes [R5](#r5), [R7](#r7), [R9](#r9).

## <a id="the-object-model-concretely"></a>The object model, concretely

*His words at the plan: "The model is to create book Parts. Build that abstraction. And then make another annotation that is either a type of part or a format that assumes a part as a part of its spec called Manual, and then whatever else you need to make this an implementation story that could work for just about any book in this library. Do you see that the design is an anchor in the form of the solution, and can't be reduced to simple features." And then: "Maybe manual isn't a type of part. Maybe it is a format for a part. And it accompanies a part, where the part in that case specifies the type of manual. That is likely more scalable. Can you please keep exploring the abstraction, looking at the code, seeing what needs to be implemented to make this work and bring a design to me that you have confidence can work on both existing pages - the actual reference manual and the bookshelf for the library catalogue?" Every class below lives in his library, in the manual's chapters' files; nothing in the package changes. Members are named as the plan sees them and every name is the build's to confirm by the naming chapter. **The design below is the third and the one brought to him; the first two are kept in D4.**

**In The Entry's code, beside the index, the appendix, the folder and the entry — the table's data model of parts.**

- **Part**, in his form of the afternoon, `<Part>Name</Part>` at the head of a chapter, its words the name of the part, which is the heading of the part's section in the table of contents. Read through getters and kept nowhere: its name from its words; its section, the table's section whose heading has that name. Its specification: said of a chapter; names a section of its own book's table; that section's rows lead to the chapter, [D6](#d6). From the table's side, the table of contents answers its parts, the part of a chapter and the chapters of a part, which is the object model of the book's structure he asked for in [D10](#d10), where the package's Part and the table meet. *The morning's form, a Part written two ways with a view on the section, is superseded and kept only here.*
- **View**, PROXY name — his word, *"a view for a collection of chapters"* — a Format said of a part's section, the base of every format for a part; the manual is one and a gallery would be another. What a view gives, each overridable and none by default: its leaf, a method the book calls inside its draw with the chapter, like a function component, that draws the chapter beside whatever the view adds — none, so the book draws its plain leaf; its row, what the entry of one of its chapters adds to its note; its context, the class the book wears while a chapter of its part is open. Its layer on the section carries the tree's rules for that part. Its specification: said of a section of the table that is a part.
- **Index**, said of the table, is the data model: the parts, the sections said to be parts, in table order; the part of a chapter; the view of a chapter, through its part; the sequence of a chapter, its part's chapters less those under an appendix section, or the body; its entries now walking a part's groups.
- **Appendix** keeps its one job: a section at the foot of the table, in a smaller voice, whose chapters are out of the sequence.
- **Entry**: its note adds what the chapter's view says its row shows; the manual's numbered entry keeps the icon and the number, counted in the sequence.
- **Folder**: said by the book of every part and group at bound, except a part whose heading is parenthetical; its defines says the part that holds the open chapter is open, as an entry says its chapter is.

**In The Manual's code — the manual and the all-in-one case.**

- **Manual**, a view, written once in the table beside the part, `<Part /><Manual />`. Its leaf is the body of the manual's leaves today — the words holding the chapter, the files with the tab bar of file tabs, the words tab, the dock and the options, the listings with the chosen one opened, the rail of file presses with their skeletons, the grip — drawn inside a styled wrapper of the view's own that carries the leaf's rules, the spread's grid per reading among them, read off the book's class.
