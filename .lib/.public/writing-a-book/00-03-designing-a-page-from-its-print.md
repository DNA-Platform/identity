## The art of making libraries

**Doug, 2026-10-07:** *"I am pretty sure the library semantics are as universal as the index / page semantics of a website. But still, it has connections that emphasize things a bit differently than a normal website, and you take upon yourself the task of adapting that navigation to whatever view you like."*

A library's words — subject, book, cover, synopsis, table of contents, *filed under* — are the navigation, as universal as a site's index and pages. They connect with a different emphasis: a book is filed under what it is about, a cover stands for a book wherever the book is named, a table is the book seen by its parts. Designing a page is adapting that navigation to a view without changing what the words mean. The bookshelf is the proof: a side bar, a shelf and a desk are one table of contents placed three ways — by its rows' names, by its rows' covers, by the open row's synopsis — and nothing new had to exist for any of them.

## The method

**0. Ask of every thing on the page which of the library's words it is.** Subject, book, cover, synopsis, table of contents, chapter, reference, *filed under*. If it is one, it is already built and already navigates; design its look. If it is none, ask which word it *belongs to* and what is said of that word to make it — an annotation on a cover, a type of row in a table, a class on the book — and never a new mechanism. The bookshelf, answered this way: the bar is the cover; the side bar, the shelf and the desk are the table of contents placed three ways, by its rows' names, by its rows' covers, by the open row's synopsis; a row carries its book's cover and synopsis by import, which is how a catalogue knows another book; the mark is the cover's illustration by reference; *read on* and the built view are classes on the book; the `▷` is the row's second reference. Nothing on the page was a seventh word. **This is the rule that lets one person design many books**: the words do not change from book to book, only the placing does.

1. **Start from the bound print.** `.me/..public/<book>/index.html` holds the book exactly as the binder prints it. The design book's tool [The Print](../../../../.me/.design/o6-the-print~print.mjs) makes the page from it — `node .me/.design/o6-the-print~print.mjs dougs-story .me/.design/6-the-story~035.html` — the bar, me, the table of contents, the front and every page hidden, the framework's class names kept and the hashes dropped, a `<style>` of your own in place of the printed sheet; run again after a bind, it refreshes the print and keeps the page's style and script. Every word on the page is the book's; nothing is mocked.
2. **Keep it beside the chapter that designs it**, as `N-the-<view>~0NN.html` in the design book, and open it in a browser from the file. One page, many rounds; photographs come later from the design book's camera. The worked page is [The Bookshelf](../../../../.me/.design/5-the-bookshelf.tsx) in Doug's library, with his decisions under it in his words and the fitting of the page to the library's words.
3. **Change one thing per round and say what it was.** He looks, he says, you edit, he refreshes. Never redo what was close — *"Your original one was closer to correct. We could have tweaked it. But you completely redid it."*
4. **Find comparables before inventing.** A house is priced from the houses like it. Name the site that already does the interaction and take its element; the answer to *would any website ever look like this?* must be yes, with the site named.
5. **Port when seen, and measured when done**, with the workbench open, until the live page measures as the page does — `look <book> built style="<selector>|<props>"` for every value the design chapter names, never a photograph alone: the first such run on the bookshelf found two drifts the eye had missed, a bar in the paper's colour and a name in the wrong ink, each a base rule outranking the theme's. The HTML is the design, exactly — values into the theme's fields, regions into `write()`, the breakpoint into the narrow rule; photographs only check. Doug: *"we literally designed in HTML."*

**The order of questions, traced for one thing on the page — the mark in the bar.** *Which word is it?* The cover's, seen small: a window onto the cover's drawing. *What data does it need that the chapter does not say?* The drawing, and where the window is cut — so the cover gains an `Illustration`, an `Svg` in a paragraph with the file beside the cover, and a `Window`, two numbers said of the cover; a cover type exposes both as properties. *Who draws it?* Something with a `write()` of its own, so a noun: `Mark`, a word given a cover, which paints the cover's scheme and window where it stands and draws the drawing inside. *Who places it?* The book, in `library()`, from whichever cover it can reach — its own, its subject's, its author's — through one seam, `coverOf`. *Where does its rule go?* The theme's part `marks()`, which reads `var(--band)` and `var(--window-x)` and knows no book. *What is said of it?* Nothing; a mark is not a state. Every element of the bookshelf went through the same six questions, and the ones whose answer to the first was *none of the words* — the kicker, the way to the book — were drawn by the book as its own elements and nothing more.

**While porting, the page is open; nothing is bound in order to look at it.** The workbench — `node .me/.manual/6-developing-a-library~workbench.mjs`, then `look <book>` after a save — is his manual's [Developing a Library](../../../../.me/.manual/6-developing-a-library.tsx); one bind when the work is done, since the bind alone checks each book's rules, the print and every link.

## The rules that held

- **A region is designed from what its chapter carries.** The bar is the cover and nothing else: the subject it is filed under, its own title, its author. It needs no other book, so it scales, every book has it, and it stays this book's. If a region needs data the chapter does not say, the chapter gets an annotation and a custom `Cover` exposes it as a property; the region never gets a dependency.
- **The cover is the book's importable data model.** Doug, 2026-10-07: *"the cover can become an importable data model for a book… other parts of the book can import the cover rather than the whole book, to access that information. This is a sensible way to share state… and the cover of other books can be injected in annotatively so that it's not a part of what would cause things to render."* What a cover says is said in annotations — `Author`, `Subject`, `About` today, an `Illustration` and a scheme next — and a Cover of the library's own, a chapter of the manual that the appendix lists, exposes each as a property. A bar, a mark, a theme read the book's cover by importing the cover file; a catalogue's row holds another book's cover as [`Synopsis`](../../package/src/libraries/Synopsis.tsx) already holds another book's synopsis — an annotation given the imported chapter as its child — with the append left out, so it is data the shelf draws from and never a chapter that draws.
- **A reference can show its referent's cover before it is followed.** The subject's mark in the bar unfolds on hover to the subject's name — what a press will show. The canonical echo, made an interaction.
- **One drawing, many places, by reference.** A mark is a window onto the drawing on the cover — an SVG `use` on the page, the one file in the book — never a copy at another scale. *"If we changed one, yours wouldn't change."* Its border is the drawing's line width at that scale; its window is a rule of the book, not of the place, so the same mark is the same everywhere.
- **A book has a colour scheme, not a colour, and the scheme comes from what the book is.** The catalogue wears the site's opal and teal; the story a Field Notes notebook; the design book a blueprint; the manual gunmetal and brass. Three colours in blocks — the Penguin tri-band — the third colour its own band, not a repeat. Pastels are dusty, a quarter of a candy's chroma; a formula in colour space is a guide, never the chooser.
- **Black on no bar, a side bar a breath off white, colour kept for accents**: dots, spines, the open row's strip.
- **Sizes are the files' multiples of one body.** A 14px body; covers 132 wide; the desk compact enough that the shelf stays in reach under it.
- **A lockup is built on the brand rules**: one mark height, a gap of a third of it, the wordmark's cap height on the mark's centre line, tracking a touch tighter, one weight, clear space of one mark.
- **An open book keeps its shape.** The desk's words are as tall as its cover; a long entry fades and *read on* is a state of the page that holds while other books are picked.
- **Nothing is named that is not on a design or in the library.** The frames are one file with four attributes, `data-layout`, `data-tone`, `data-at`, `data-view`: a theme is a tone, never a book's; a layout is where the library's bar goes; a page is what it is at; a view its rank. Doug: *"Maybe think about what these designs are, and name them accordingly."*

## What is still the binder's

A page knows of another book what it imports: a catalogue's row imports its book's cover and its synopsis, and the subject's mark is the subject's cover imported, so a book's colours and its drawing are said once, on its cover. What no import answers — the reverse of a reference, the chain upward, a count — the binder holds while it runs and hands to no page yet; a design names the fact in the library's words, *filed under*, *by*, *refers to*, and the binder compiles it in the direction the screen needs. Doug: *"If not, we should add that."*
